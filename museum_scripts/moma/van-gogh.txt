The swirling blue canvas in front of you is The Starry Night, and the first honest thing to say about it is that it is probably smaller than you expected. Twenty-nine inches tall, about three feet wide. The posters and the coffee mugs have promised you something monumental, and here it is, a modest rectangle you could carry under one arm. So stand as close as the crowd allows and look at the surface instead. The sky is not smooth. Every star sits in its own whirlpool of ridged paint, laid on so thick that the brushstrokes catch the gallery light and throw tiny shadows of their own.

Vincent van Gogh painted it in June of 1889 in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, in the south of France. He was thirty-six, and he was a patient at the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole, where he had entered voluntarily the month before, after a series of breakdowns in Arles. From his window he could watch the countryside in the dark, and that spring he told his brother Theo in a letter that he had looked out long before sunrise, "with nothing but the morning star, which looked very big." That star is still here. Find the cypress first, the dark flame rising along the left edge. The largest, whitest orb just to its right, low over the hills, is Venus, which really did blaze in the pre-dawn sky over Provence that June.

But do not mistake the painting for a view. He was not allowed to paint in his bedroom, so this canvas was made downstairs in a studio room, by daylight, from memory. The village under the hills was not visible from his window at all, and its church steeple is not Provençal. It is Dutch, a spire from the country of his childhood, planted quietly in the south of France. The real and the remembered are braided together here, and the sky belongs to neither. He was unsentimental about the result. In a letter that month he mentioned it almost in passing, as a new study of a starry sky. Thirteen months later he was dead at thirty-seven, never suspecting that his night study would become one of the most recognized images on earth. It reached this museum in 1941, through the bequest of Lillie P. Bliss, one of the three women who founded the place.

Now find the other night in this room. A desert under a full moon, a river in the distance, and a lion pausing over a sleeping woman. This is The Sleeping Gypsy, painted in 1897 by Henri Rousseau, and it is everything The Starry Night is not: wide, still, and silent, more than six and a half feet across. The woman lies in a robe of colored stripes, her walking stick still in her hand, a mandolin and a water jar beside her. The lion has found her. Its eye is open, its tail is raised, and nothing happens. Rousseau described the scene himself: a wandering musician, overcome by exhaustion, fallen into a deep sleep, and a lion that catches her scent yet does not devour her. A moonlight effect, he wrote, very poetic.

Rousseau was a toll collector on the edge of Paris, entirely self-taught, and in his fifties when he painted this. In 1898 he wrote to the mayor of Laval, the town where he was born, offering the canvas for eighteen hundred to two thousand francs, so that his hometown might keep a souvenir of one of its children. The mayor declined. The professionals of his day mostly laughed at him. Forty years on, in 1939, the painting came here as the gift of Mrs. Simon Guggenheim.

Two nights, then, hanging in one room, both of them invented indoors, both by painters the experts refused to take at their word. One sky boils and one holds its breath, and neither was ever really about the weather. The rest of the room shifts as the museum rearranges its collection, though painters working in France in those same years, names like Cézanne and Gauguin and Seurat, have long kept these two company here. Take a slow lap and see who shares the walls today. Then let the doorway pull you onward. The galleries ahead carry the story into the new century, and it moves quickly from here.
