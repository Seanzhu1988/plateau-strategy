Start with the plainest things around you. A metal box. A run of fluorescent light. A canvas holding nothing but a date. Gallery 413 is called One Thing After Another, and the phrase belongs to Donald Judd, who argued in his essay Specific Objects that the best new work of his moment was neither painting nor sculpture. What he wanted was "simply order, like that of continuity, one thing after another." This is Minimalism, and its plainness was a quiet revolution. Instead of brushwork and inspiration, artists ordered steel, vinyl, and aluminum from fabricators, wrote instructions, repeated a single unit until it became a rhythm. One thing to know before you settle in: these rooms are rehung often, so hold the titles as chapters rather than fixed casts. Lately this one has held a Judd from 1968, a Sol LeWitt cube structure, a Dan Flavin light, and date paintings by On Kawara, who painted the day's date in plain type and destroyed any canvas he could not finish by midnight. Two of his dates here have read December 17 and 18, 1979, the very edge of this floor's story.

The next room takes its name from Wipe Cycle, a 1969 installation by Frank Gillette and Ira Schneider, a bank of nine monitors first built for a New York show called TV as a Creative Medium. Mixed into its images is a camera feed of whoever stands before it, played back on a delay, so the watcher becomes the watched. It anchors a room about the years when artists first got their hands on video and used it to talk back to television.

From there, the galleries move one idea at a time. Hard edged abstraction, all sharp lines and flat color. Six decades of Romare Bearden. Midcentury design meeting the real bodies that had to use it. Artists picturing the public events of their era.

Then the walls close in, in the best way. Gallery 418 holds James Rosenquist's F-111, painted in 1964 and 1965, ten feet tall and eighty-six feet long, wrapping the room on every side. Rosenquist had earned his living painting billboards above Times Square, and he brought that scale indoors, making the painting for his first solo show at the Leo Castelli Gallery and sizing its panels to that gallery's four walls so the picture would surround you completely. An F-111 fighter-bomber, then still on the drawing board, stretches the full length, tangled with an angel food cake, a Firestone tire, a fork in spaghetti, a girl under a chrome hair dryer, a beach umbrella opening against an atomic flash. Rosenquist saw the plane as an economic machine as much as a weapon, a jobs program with wings, and his painting lets consumer plenty and war share one long, bright surface.

Gallery 419 slows the pulse. In 1979 Sophie Calle invited twenty-nine people, friends, acquaintances, and strangers, to sleep in her own bed in Paris, in eight-hour shifts over eight days, so the bed was never empty. When someone failed to arrive, she hired a stand-in or climbed in herself. She photographed the sleepers at regular intervals, noted their habits, served meals, changed the sheets. The Sleepers, the grid of photographs and notes that came of it, was one of her earliest works, and it points straight at the decades ahead, when art would follow life into its most private corners.

The last room, 421, may be the quietest on the floor, and it is where the story has been heading. In May 1969, at a gallery in Munich, Mel Bochner measured the room itself and declared the measurements the art: strips of black tape and printed numbers recording the span of every wall, doorway, and window. Measurement Room has been remade here, honoring Bochner, who died in 2025 at eighty-four. Nothing is added to the space except attention. That is Conceptual art's wager, that an idea alone can hold a room, and together with Minimalism's plain objects it carries this floor all the way to 1980. When the numbers on the walls have done their work, let them point you out. This floor ends here, so take the escalator or elevator down to Floor 2, where the collection picks up at 1980 and runs to today.
