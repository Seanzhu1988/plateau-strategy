Stand where you can take in the whole wall. The painting in front of you is nearly nine feet tall and more than seventeen feet wide, and almost nothing on it was made by a brush touching canvas. This is One: Number 31, 1950, by Jackson Pollock. Threads of black, white, brown, and silvery gray loop, snap, and cross from edge to edge, thousands of them, layered until the surface stops being a picture of anything and starts behaving more like weather.

None of it happened in a city studio. In November 1945, Pollock and his wife, the painter Lee Krasner, left Manhattan for an old fisherman's homestead in Springs, a hamlet in East Hampton, on Long Island. Behind the house stood a small storage barn. In 1946 Pollock had it moved about twenty-five yards, set on new footings, laid with a wood floor, and given a window cut into its north wall. That plain wooden floor became his working surface. He unrolled canvas across it and painted from above, moving around all four sides, dripping and flinging ordinary house enamel from sticks, from hardened brushes, from cans punched with holes, once even from a turkey baster. He said the floor put him at ease, that he felt nearer to the painting there, able to work from all four sides and be in it rather than in front of it.

The summer and fall of 1950 were the peak of that method. Pollock was thirty-eight, and in those few months he produced three mural-size canvases in swift succession. This is one of them. Its siblings ended up elsewhere, Autumn Rhythm across town at the Metropolitan Museum, Number 32 in a museum in Düsseldorf. That same summer, a photographer named Hans Namuth began coming out to Springs, shooting Pollock at work through summer and into fall, crouched at the canvas edge, paint tracing arcs through the air. Those photographs, and the film Namuth made, fixed the image most people still carry of this painter in motion.

Fame had arrived just ahead of them. In August 1949, Life magazine ran a four-page spread asking whether this man might be the greatest living painter in the United States, and millions of readers met the drip paintings before the art world had settled its own verdict. Then, in November 1950, Time magazine quoted an Italian critic who dismissed the work as chaos. Pollock fired back a telegram, "No chaos, damn it," adding that he was busy painting for his show opening November 28. That show, at the Betty Parsons Gallery in Manhattan, included the great 1950 canvases. Almost nothing sold. The paintings visitors passed over that winter are now counted among the most important American pictures of the century, and this one entered the Museum of Modern Art's collection in 1968.

The room's name, New World Stage, points at the larger story. Before the Second World War, modern art had one capital, and it was Paris. After 1945, with Europe exhausted and many of its artists scattered, the center of gravity crossed the Atlantic, and New York became the city where painting's next chapter was written. Pollock fit the moment almost too well, born in Wyoming, raised in the West, working in a barn beside a Long Island creek. In that same telegram he pointed out that he had never even been to Europe. The canvas in front of you helped make the shift undeniable.

Now use your feet. Walk up close, close enough to see how a single white line thins, speeds up, and breaks into droplets, and follow one thread as far as you can before losing it. There is no center here, no place the eye is ordered to rest, and no top or bottom while it was being made, since Pollock worked from every side. He insisted none of it was accident, and up close you can start to believe him, each layer landing in rhythm, each color answering the ones beneath. Then step back until it all gathers into a single continuous field. Its title, after all, is One.

When you are ready, take one last look from across the room, where the whole surface settles almost into landscape, then let it go. The galleries ahead on this floor carry the story deeper into the decades New York made its own. The paint here was barely dry when that story began.
