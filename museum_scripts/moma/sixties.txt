Thirty-two cans of soup, and every one of them is looking at you. Red and white, silver at the top, one can to a canvas, each twenty inches tall and sixteen wide, filling the wall in tidy rows like stock on a shelf. This is Campbell's Soup Cans, painted by Andy Warhol in 1962, and your eyes are lying to you. They look printed. They are not. Step as close as the museum allows and the factory finish falls apart. The red drifts toward orange on some cans. The shading on the silver lids comes and goes. The little gold fleurs-de-lys ringing the bottom of each can were pressed on one by one with a rubber stamp, and they wobble. Warhol painted all of this by hand, canvas after canvas, keeping everything identical except a single line of type, the flavor. Tomato. Onion. Pepper pot. Clam chowder. Thirty-two canvases, one for every variety Campbell's made at the time. When the art dealer Irving Blum saw soup paintings leaning against a wall in Warhol's New York apartment and asked why there was more than one, Warhol's answer was plain arithmetic. There were thirty-two varieties, so there would be thirty-two paintings.

Warhol was thirty-three, a highly paid commercial illustrator who wanted to be taken seriously as a painter, and for his subject he chose the most ordinary thing in his life. "I used to have the same lunch every day, for twenty years," he said of Campbell's soup. The same thing, over and over. That sentence is the engine of this wall. Instead of painting his feelings, he painted repetition itself, the rhythm of the supermarket aisle, and dared the art world to call it art.

The art world's first answer came in Los Angeles. These canvases made their debut on July 9, 1962, at the Ferus Gallery on La Cienega Boulevard, Warhol's first solo show of paintings and often called the first Pop art exhibition on the West Coast. Warhol himself never made it out to see it. Blum, who ran the gallery, had trouble getting the canvases to hang level, so he built a narrow ledge and stood them in a single row along it, the way a can would sit in a grocery aisle. They were priced at about a hundred dollars apiece. The city mostly shrugged. A gallery down the street stacked real Campbell's cans in its window with a sign warning shoppers not to be misled, since the real thing cost thirty-three cents for two. Still, a handful of canvases sold, five by most accounts, one of them reserved by the actor Dennis Hopper before the show even opened.

Then Blum did the thing that makes this room possible. He decided the thirty-two belonged together, and one by one persuaded the buyers to give their paintings back. He called Warhol and asked to buy the whole group. The price was one thousand dollars, paid off at a hundred dollars a month. Blum kept the set together for more than three decades, and in 1996 it came here, partly as his gift and partly as a purchase reported at fifteen million dollars. And notice the museum's quiet joke: the cans are usually hung in the order the varieties first came on the market, a grocer's timeline in paint.

One more date. The Ferus show closed on August 4, 1962, and that same night, across town in Los Angeles, Marilyn Monroe died. Within weeks Warhol had turned to her face and to silkscreen, the mechanical process that carried the rest of his career. So the wall in front of you holds some of the last paintings he ever made mostly by hand. He spent months teaching his hand to imitate a machine, then traded the hand for the machine itself.

The room around you borrows its name, Things, from a 1965 novel by Georges Perec about a young couple who want everything they see. The artists gathered here took that appetite seriously, brands, packages, television, the bright new abundance of the sixties, and as you circle you may find groceries everywhere, perhaps even a pair of plaster cheeseburgers with everything on them. When you are ready, keep walking. The decade continues in the galleries ahead, and if Warhol's Gold Marilyn Monroe is on view, she usually floats a few rooms from here on her field of gold, born in the months after that August night. Follow the soup, and see where it leads.
