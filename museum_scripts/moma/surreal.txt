The first thing to do in this room is find the smallest painting in it. Follow the small knot of people, there is almost always one, and work your way close. This is The Persistence of Memory, painted by Salvador Dali in 1931, and if your first thought is that it cannot possibly be this small, you are in good company. Nine and a half inches tall, thirteen wide, oil on canvas, barely bigger than a sheet of paper. Most people have spent a lifetime seeing it on posters and mugs, enlarged to the size of its own fame, and the real thing rests on the wall like a postcard from a dream.

Step in and look. A bay of still water. Golden cliffs along the horizon, and those are real cliffs, the Catalan coast near Port Lligat, where Dali made his home, painted with the precision of someone who knew them by heart. A dead olive tree, its branches cut. And then the famous impossibility, pocket watches gone soft. One droops from the olive branch. One slides off a rectangular ledge like a piece of cloth. One is draped over a pale, fleshy creature stranded in the middle of the picture. Give that creature a moment. Long eyelashes, a closed eye, and in profile it is a version of Dali's own face, asleep or melting, hard to say which. Then find the one watch that kept its shape, orange and face down at the lower left, crawling with ants. Ants, in Dali's private language, meant decay. Even the hard watch is being eaten.

Dali told the story of this painting himself, ten years later, in his autobiography, and it is a very Dali story. A dinner in his Paris apartment in Montmartre that ended with a ripe, runny camembert. Around ten at night, guests gone, the painter, twenty-seven years old, lingered at the table thinking about softness. In the studio stood an unfinished landscape of Port Lligat, waiting, he said, for an idea. As he reached to turn out the light, he saw it, two soft watches, one hanging from the olive branch. He claimed the whole picture was finished two hours later, and he gave the watches a name, "the camembert of time."

Here is what the painting itself says about that. Conservators at this museum recently examined it, shining light through the canvas and mapping the pigments with X-ray scanning. The branch does not continue underneath its watch. Neither does the ledge, nor the sleeping face. Dali had decided in advance exactly where each soft watch would go, left those areas blank, and filled them in later, most likely over weeks. The two hour miracle is almost certainly a myth, told by a man who understood that a painting about the slipperiness of time deserved an origin story with a suspicious clock in it. His fellow Surrealists saw through him. The poet Andre Breton accused him of meaningfully falsifying his own memories, which may be the truest label the picture ever wore.

It reached New York in 1932, shown at a small gallery run by Julien Levy, and sold for two hundred fifty dollars. In 1934 a donor who chose to remain anonymous gave it to this museum, and it has lived here ever since, growing into one of the most recognized images in modern art.

Now widen your gaze, because the whole room is the argument. In 1924 Breton published his Manifesto of Surrealism, steeped in Freud, declaring a break from rationalism in favor of imagination, desire, and the unconscious. The gallery is called A Surreal Lens because the movement's tools were never only brushes. Photography and film mattered enormously, prized for what they could pull up from beneath the surface of ordinary life, and Dali himself praised photography as a carrier of true poetry. So as you circle the room, whatever mix of paintings, photographs, sculptures, and film is up on your visit, watch for the same move repeated everywhere. An ordinary thing, a street, a cup, a face, tipped just far enough to turn strange. Names like Magritte, Miro, Ernst, Oppenheim, and Giacometti tend to keep the little Dali company in this room.

When the watches have finished with you, take one last look at how much a small thing can hold. Then let the doorway draw you onward, and let the fifth floor keep unfolding, one room, one idea at a time.
