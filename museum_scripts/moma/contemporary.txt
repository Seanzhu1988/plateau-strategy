Take a slow look around before you settle on anything. The walls hold paintings, but also photographs enlarged to the scale of paintings, screens with moving images, sculpture built from things you might find in a hardware store or a closet. Some of what surrounds you was made before you were born, and some of it may have been made last year. You are on the museum's second floor, sixteen rooms numbered 201 through 216, and together they carry the story of art from 1980 to today, right up to the present tense.

That present tense is why this floor behaves differently from the rest of the building. When the museum reopened in October 2019, after an expansion that cost about 450 million dollars and added more than forty thousand square feet of gallery space, it made an unusual promise: about a third of the collection galleries would be rehung every six months or so. Here, where the story is still being written, that churn matters most. So the rooms around you run loosely in order of time, and each is built around a single idea. One room may belong to a single artist. Another to one medium, or one city in one charged moment, or a question a group of artists could not put down. Read the short text at each doorway. It names the idea, and the idea is the thing to hold, because the particular works come and go.

Think about what 1980 means as a starting line. The Cold War was in its final decade, and the Berlin Wall would fall in 1989. In 1981 doctors reported the first cases of the illness that would be named AIDS, and that epidemic runs under the art of these years like a current, in grief, in rage, in tenderness. The art market boomed. Painting was declared dead and kept refusing to die. Artists turned the tools of advertising and television back on the culture that made them, and then the internet arrived and widened everything again. In these decades the art world finally began to look like the world, and the makers in these rooms come from more places than in any earlier chapter of the collection.

One work from the museum's collection can stand for the spirit of this floor, whether or not it hangs here today. In 1991 Felix Gonzalez-Torres took two ordinary battery-powered wall clocks, each about fourteen inches across, hung them side by side so their rims touched, and set them ticking in unison. He titled the piece Untitled, with the words Perfect Lovers in parentheses. The clocks begin together and slowly, inevitably, drift out of step, and eventually one of them stops first. He made it as his partner, Ross, was dying of AIDS, and he said it was "the scariest thing I have ever done." Two identical objects, a little store-bought hardware, and one of the most quietly devastating portraits of love and time in the collection. If you find it in these rooms, give it a full minute. If you do not, carry the idea with you, because it teaches you how to read this floor, where modest materials often hold the heaviest feeling.

So resist the urge to hunt with a checklist. No one, including the museum, knows yet which of these works will matter most in a hundred years. That uncertainty is what the constant rehanging admits, and it is what makes this floor honest. Try a simpler game instead. Find one work made in the year you were born, and one made in the past five years, and ask yourself what changed in between. You are not looking at settled history here. You are looking at the argument while it is still going on.

When you are ready to move on, two directions reward you. The escalators and stairs lead up, where floors four and five run the story backward through the 1940s and on toward the 1880s, so the art grows older as you climb. Or drift down to ground level, where the sculpture garden opens to the sky. Either way, before you leave, find the newest thing in the room and give it one last look. It will not hang here forever.
