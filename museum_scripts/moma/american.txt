The pink dress reaches you first. A woman in a field of dry grass, seen from behind, propped on her arms, her body angled toward a gray farmhouse far up the hill, its weathered outbuildings huddled under an overcast sky. This is Christina's World, painted by Andrew Wyeth in 1948, when he was thirty one. It is smaller than its reputation, about two feet eight inches tall and four feet wide, and it is made of egg tempera, an old, slow medium built up in small dry strokes. Step close and the field turns almost unbearably particular, blade by blade. Step back and the whole picture goes quiet again.

The woman is Anna Christina Olson, and the field is real. It slopes down from her family's farmhouse on Hathorn Point in Cushing, Maine, land the family had held since the late seventeen hundreds. Christina was fifty five when this was painted. As a girl she developed a muscular condition that was never firmly diagnosed, perhaps polio, and it slowly took her ability to walk. She refused a wheelchair for the rest of her life. She got where she was going by pulling herself along the ground with her arms, which is exactly what you are looking at. One day Wyeth watched from a window of the house as she crossed the field below, and the image would not let him go.

He knew her well by then. In July 1939, twenty two years old and summering with his family down the coast at Port Clyde, he met a young woman named Betsy James, and on that same first day she walked him down the road to meet her friend Christina. Andrew and Betsy married the next year. For nearly thirty summers he came back to the Olson farm, painting the house, Christina, and her brother Alvaro in roughly three hundred paintings and drawings, until her death in January 1968. For this picture Christina posed for the idea but not the body. Betsy, then in her twenties, modeled the torso, which is partly why the figure reads younger than the woman who inspired her. Notice the tension in her, the arms locked, the silhouette nearly frozen against the grass. This is not repose. Wyeth said his challenge was to do justice to "her extraordinary conquest of a life which most people would consider hopeless." And it was Betsy who named the painting. The title tells you how to read it. Not Christina, but Christina's world, a state of mind as much as a place, the whole distance between a body and its home.

Fame came slowly, then completely. First shown at the Macbeth Gallery in Manhattan in 1948, the picture drew little notice from critics. Alfred Barr, this museum's founding director, saw something else in it and bought it for eighteen hundred dollars. It has lived here ever since, rarely allowed to travel, and became one of the most recognized American paintings of the twentieth century. The farmhouse grew famous along with it. The Olson house still stands in Cushing, kept by the Farnsworth Art Museum and named a National Historic Landmark, and you can walk that field yourself some summer.

Now let the room widen around the painting. It is called American Idioms, and the plural is the point. These are the many accents in which American artists spoke between the Great Depression and the Second World War, narrative pictures from painters who often knew one another, trained together at places like the Art Students League in New York, or worked for public programs like the WPA. Voices from Chicago and Pittsburgh hang beside New Yorkers, and self taught painters hang beside the schooled. Depending on the day, you may find Edward Hopper's House by the Railroad here, a lone Victorian house beside empty tracks, painted in 1925, which in 1930 became the first painting ever to enter this museum's collection, and which film lovers know as a model for the Bates house in Psycho. Look too for Grant Wood's farm country in spring, Archibald Motley's ecstatic church meeting, Alice Neel's sharp portrait of the poet Kenneth Fearing, and, if it is up, an early Jackson Pollock, still working the muscular land in the manner of his teacher, Thomas Hart Benton.

That Pollock is the hinge. Within a few years he would put his canvases on the floor and let the paint fly, and American painting would trade its regional idioms for an international voice. That story waits one floor down, in the galleries that begin at 1950. Before you go, give Christina one more look. The house is far. The ground is dry. She is not defeated. When you are ready, walk on.
