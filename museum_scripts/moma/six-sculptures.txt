The tallest figure in the room wears a bowler hat. He is bronze, four and a half feet tall, leaning on the wisp of a tree, ankles crossed, easy as a man waiting outside a cafe. Give him a moment, then turn slowly. This is one of the museum's smallest galleries, a single quiet room on the garden side of the fifth floor, and the doorstep of the whole collection. The rooms beyond carry the story of modern art forward from the 1880s. This one comes first, an overture called Six Sculptures: figures from the first half of the twentieth century, all answering one question. Once a figure no longer has to be a marble hero, what can it be? The cast shifts now and then, but the conversation stays the same.

The man in the bowler is Man in the Open Air, made around 1915 by Elie Nadelman, a sculptor from Warsaw who reached New York in 1914, just as Europe went to war. The pose is pure classical sculpture, weight settled on one leg, the body a long gentle S. The bowler and the necktie are the American street, and Nadelman loved that street, collecting American folk art by the thousands. He boasted that the curve was the only line he needed, and the whole man is curves, even his tree.

Now find the smallest bronze, a head sprouting petals. This is Tiari, modeled by Henri Matisse in Nice in the summer of 1930, just after he sailed home from Tahiti. The tiari is Tahiti's white gardenia, worn tucked behind the ear, and here a woman's head and the blossom fuse into one smooth thing, a painter thinking with his thumbs.

Henry Moore's Helmet Head Number 1, from 1950, is armor with something alive inside. An outer shell opens in narrow slits, and an inner form peers out, part sentry, part prisoner. Moore first explored helmet forms as war broke out in 1939, and during the Blitz he drew Londoners sheltering in the Underground. A decade later this small object still asks: does a shell protect what it holds, or trap it?

The two figures arching toward each other, their heads bursting into spikes, are The Impossible, III, made in 1946 by the Brazilian sculptor Maria Martins. You cannot tell whether they are about to embrace or to strike, and that is the point. "It is nearly impossible to make people understand each other," she said. Martins was a diplomat's wife who became one of the fiercest sculptors of wartime New York. Marcel Duchamp fell for her, and the reclining figure in his final, secret masterpiece was modeled on her body. The museum bought this bronze the year she finished it.

Picasso's Goat Skull and Bottle reads as a somber still life in gray, until you come close. The goat's horns are bicycle handlebars. Its eyes are the heads of heavy bolts. The ridged fur is corrugated cardboard, and the candle in the bottle throws out rays made of nails. He built it in Vallauris in 1951 and had it cast in bronze three years later, then painted. The grays remember the war he had just lived through, and the candle is the oldest of symbols, a small flame standing for a life.

The welded iron profile, Head, from 1938, is by David Smith, who learned to weld in an automobile plant and later worked out of a Brooklyn ironworks. Cast iron and steel, factory fire, no marble anywhere. The museum took it in as early as 1943, a bet on a new American way of making sculpture.

If a small bronze plaque has been tugging at your eye, go read it. It is by Jenny Holzer, from the early 1980s, one of thirty in her Living series, cast in the official format of historical markers but carrying words no marker would dare. A seventh guest in a room named for six, it asks of reading what the others ask of looking.

Before you move on, notice which figure you kept circling back to; that one is yours. Then take the doorway deeper into the floor, where the collection begins in earnest with the painters of the 1880s. And keep one thought: this quiet room faces the Sculpture Garden, so when you reach the ground floor later, walk out under the open sky and meet the big outdoor bronzes, cousins of the figures behind you.
