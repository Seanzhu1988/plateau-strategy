The water starts before you reach it. Three canvases hang side by side, each about fourteen feet wide and six and a half feet tall, and together they run just over forty feet, about the length of a city bus. Look for a horizon and you will not find one. There is no shoreline, no sky, no bank to stand on, only the surface of a pond seen from above and within at once, lily pads drifting in loose islands, clouds crossing underneath them as reflections, blue and lavender pooling into green. Claude Monet painted this between 1914 and 1926, the last twelve years of his life, and he meant it to do exactly what it is doing to you now, to surround you, to slow your breathing, to soften the edges of things.

The pond is real, and it was his. In 1883 Monet moved his family to Giverny, a farming village on the Seine about forty miles northwest of Paris. He rented a long pink farmhouse, bought it seven years later, and in 1893 bought a strip of marshy ground across the road and the railway line. He wanted a water garden. To fill it he diverted a little stream called the Ru, a branch of the river Epte, and his neighbors fought him over the permit, afraid his imported plants would poison the water their cattle drank. He won, planted willows and irises, set water lilies on the surface, and arched a wooden Japanese footbridge across the narrow end, painting it green rather than the traditional red. Then he spent the rest of his life looking at it. Roughly two hundred fifty paintings of that one small pond survive. He painted it the way other people keep a diary.

Panels at this scale belong to his last great act. By 1914 Monet was seventy-four. His second wife, Alice, had died in 1911, his eldest son, Jean, in 1914, and his eyesight was failing; cataracts, first diagnosed in 1912, were muddying his reds and dimming his blues. Instead of stopping, he enlarged. In 1915 he built a vast new studio at Giverny, big enough for canvases like the ones in front of you, and set out on what he called his grandes décorations, his great decorations, more than forty large-scale panels painted alongside scores of smaller canvases. He worked through the First World War and through an eye operation in 1923 that partly restored his sight. In 1922 he formally gave a cycle of these panels to France, a gift of peace after the war. Those hang today in two oval rooms at the Orangerie in Paris, opened to the public in May 1927. Monet had died at Giverny on December 5, 1926, at eighty-six. He never saw them installed.

The triptych in front of you came to New York through a loss of another kind. In 1955 this museum bought one of the last great Water Lilies, a single canvas about eighteen feet wide, and it quickly became one of the best-loved paintings in the building. On April 15, 1958, a fire broke out during renovation work on the second floor, started, the reports said, by a workman's cigarette. One man died, and the big Monet was destroyed. The grief was public enough that the museum resolved to replace it, and in 1959, through the Mrs. Simon Guggenheim Fund, it acquired the three panels hanging here. So this room is also a kind of second chance, and it has been treated like one, a space set apart for these paintings.

Walk the full length of it, slowly. From across the room the pond assembles itself; from two feet away it falls apart into strokes laid over strokes, decisions changed in plain sight. Both distances are true, and the painting needs you moving between them. Other late Monets often keep the triptych company in this room; if a canvas of tall agapanthus leaves is hanging today, or a footbridge nearly dissolved in reds and golds, you are seeing the same pond through the same stubborn eyes.

If there is a bench, take it for a minute. This is a room people come back to, and sitting in it you can feel why. When you are ready, let the water let you go, and step back out onto the fifth floor, where the galleries carry the story onward, into the century of painting this quiet pond helped to open.
