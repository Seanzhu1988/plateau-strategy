The first thing that registers is the size. Eight feet tall, seven feet eight inches wide, an almost square wall of flesh pink and blue, and five women who look straight back at you. Their bodies are built from flat, splintered planes, as if the figures had been broken apart and reassembled at angles. The two faces on the right are the strangest, scored and striped like carved masks, and the crouching woman at the lower right manages an impossible move, her body turned away while her head swings fully around to find your eyes. This is Les Demoiselles d'Avignon, painted by Pablo Picasso in Paris in the summer of 1907, and it has been unsettling people for well over a century.

Stay with what you see for a moment before the story. Picasso was twenty-five when he began, a Spanish immigrant in a cramped, cluttered studio in Montmartre, eager to make his name in France. Through the winter of 1906 and into 1907 he filled sheet after sheet, hundreds of sketches and studies in pencil, ink, and paint, working toward what would be the largest and most ambitious picture he had ever attempted. In an early oil sketch there were seven figures, including two men, a sailor at the center of the room and a medical student standing at the left. Somewhere along the way Picasso cut the men out. The five women remain, looking out at the spot where the men once stood, which is now where you are standing. That is part of why the gazes feel so direct. That little seven figure sketch belongs to this museum too, and it is often shown in this room. If you spot a small canvas crowded with figures in a curtained interior, painted in the same pinks and blues, that is the picture this one grew from.

The title looks back to Barcelona, where Picasso spent his youth. Carrer d'Avinyo, Avignon Street, ran through the city's red-light district, and the demoiselles are its workers. The polite name was never his. He called the picture mon bordel, my brothel. The gentler title was attached by the writer Andre Salmon in 1916, when the canvas finally went before the public at a Paris exhibition, almost nine years after it was painted. Picasso never warmed to it. The friends who had seen the painting in his studio in 1907 were shocked, and he seems to have understood how far ahead of its moment it was. Even after that first showing, the canvas went back to him and stayed, rolled up, until 1924.

Look again at how different the five figures are from one another, because the differences are deliberate. The striding woman at the far left, fist clenched, echoes ancient Egyptian sculpture. The two center figures lift their elbows in poses traced to Roman copies of Greek statues. The two on the right evoke African and Oceanic masks, objects then arriving in Parisian museums and studios through colonial channels, where Picasso sought them out, along with Iberian sculpture from his native Spain. Behind all of it stand El Greco and Cezanne. Five figures, one shallow curtained space, and no single style holding them together. The picture forces a question the museum itself now poses: what did Picasso take from these sources, and how should we weigh that taking today?

The afterlife is its own story. In 1924 the couturier and collector Jacques Doucet bought the painting for twenty-five thousand francs, paid off in monthly installments. In 1939 it came here, purchased for twenty-four thousand dollars, most of that raised by selling a Degas from the collection, a trade still visible in the words by exchange on the wall label. Picasso's dealer claimed Cubism began with this picture. Picasso himself shrugged at the label. "When we invented Cubism, we had no intention of inventing Cubism," he said.

Since the museum rehung this floor in March 2024, this room has been arranged around the painting, gathering studies and works made just before and after it, so you can watch the leap as it happens. When you are ready, keep moving. These galleries run with the century, and the rooms ahead pick up the years that follow, when what happened on this canvas stopped being one studio's scandal and became the language of modern art.
