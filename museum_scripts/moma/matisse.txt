The whole wall ahead of you is one painting. Five figures hold hands and wheel around the crest of a green hill, under a sky of flat, saturated blue. The canvas is enormous, about eight and a half feet tall and nearly thirteen feet wide, and the ring of dancers turns at the scale of real bodies in a real room. This is Dance, the first version, painted by Henri Matisse in his Paris studio on the Boulevard des Invalides in early 1909.

Before the story, notice how little is here. Three colors do almost all the work. Blue for sky, green for earth, and a pale rose for the dancers themselves. No faces to speak of, no trees, no horizon detail. Matisse has stripped away everything that does not move.

Now the story. In March 1909, a Moscow textile merchant named Sergei Shchukin, one of the most daring collectors of new French painting anywhere in the world, commissioned two large decorative panels from Matisse. One would be Dance, the other Music, and they were to hang on the staircase of his Moscow mansion, so that guests climbed past them on the way up. The painting in front of you is how Matisse worked out his answer. He painted it quickly, at full size, as a compositional study, finding the rhythm of the thing at the actual scale it would have to live at. The finished version, completed in 1910, hangs today in the Hermitage Museum in St Petersburg.

Matisse later told a story about where the dance itself came from. On Sunday afternoons he liked to go to the Moulin de la Galette, the old dance hall up in Montmartre, and watch the crowd dance the farandole, a chain dance from the south of France in which everyone links hands and gets pulled along faster and faster. Back in the studio, he said, he composed this picture while humming the same tune the dancers had used, so that the whole canvas would keep to one rhythm.

You can check that with your own eyes. Follow the circle clockwise, starting from the tall dancer at the left, leaning into the turn like someone hauling a rope. The momentum passes from body to body, through arms and clasped hands, around the top of the ring and down again. Then look at the front of the circle, nearest you. There the chain is broken. One hand reaches back, the other stretches forward, and they do not quite meet. The near dancer lunges to close the gap, and the entire painting seems to hang on that inch of empty canvas. A closed circle would simply rotate. The broken one pulls.

When the finished panels were shown in Paris at the Salon d'Automne in the autumn of 1910, they caused a scandal. The nudity, the flatness, the poster-bright color, all of it was mocked. In the final version Matisse had gone further than what you see here, turning the dancers' bodies a hot brick red against deeper green and blue. Shchukin, in Paris for the exhibition, briefly lost his nerve and backed out of the purchase. Then, on the long journey home to Moscow, he thought better of it. He wrote to Matisse that he was ashamed of his weakness, and that "one should never flee the battlefield without putting up a fight." The panels went to Moscow and onto his staircase. After the Russian Revolution his collection passed to the Soviet state, which is why the final Dance lives in St Petersburg while its first, paler, quicker twin lives here.

It came to this museum in 1963, a gift from Nelson Rockefeller, given in honor of Alfred H. Barr, Jr., the museum's founding director, and the galleries around you on this floor carry Barr's name. The room itself is usually kept in Matisse's company, hung with work from the years when critics called him a wild beast, a fauve, for letting color off the leash, though the exact neighbors change as the museum rehangs.

When the circle finally lets you go, continue through the fifth floor. The rooms ahead carry the story of modern painting deeper into the twentieth century, made by artists who had all, in one way or another, seen what happens when five dancers are given a hill, a sky, and nothing else.
