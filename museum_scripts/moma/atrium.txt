Stand near the center of the floor and look straight up. The ceiling is barely a ceiling at all. It is a skylight hanging a hundred and ten feet above the street, at the top of a white shaft of daylight that drops through the whole building and lands where you are standing. This is the Donald B. and Catherine C. Marron Atrium, the open heart of the Museum of Modern Art. High above you, openings in the walls let the upper galleries peer in, and you may spot other visitors pausing there to look down at you. The emptiest room in the museum is also, on many days, its most ambitious.

The space is young by museum standards. It opened in November 2004, when MoMA unveiled a top to bottom rebuilding by the Japanese architect Yoshio Taniguchi, timed to the museum's seventy fifth anniversary. Taniguchi's idea was almost self effacing. Keep the architecture quiet so the art can be loud, and let one tall void pull daylight down into the center of everything. The atrium is that void. Its name honors Donald Marron and his wife Catherine. Marron was a Wall Street financier who ran the brokerage Paine Webber for two decades, filled its offices with contemporary art, joined this museum's board in 1975, and served as its president from 1985 to 1991. When he died in 2019, the museum he helped steer still carried his name at its core.

In the first years after the reopening, the atrium held single monumental works. Barnett Newman's steel Broken Obelisk stood here, an upside down obelisk balancing tip to tip on a pyramid, and Monet's Water Lilies hung at the top of the stairs nearby. But the room soon revealed its second calling, which is performance, and time based art of every kind.

In the winter of 2008 the Swiss artist Pipilotti Rist wrapped these walls in Pour Your Body Out, video projections twenty five feet high, meadows and orchards and swimming bodies washing around an enormous circular couch where visitors sprawled to watch. The number in the work's full title, 7354 Cubic Meters, measured the very emptiness she set out to fill.

Then, in the spring of 2010, came the piece this floor may be remembered for longest. For nearly three months, eight hours a day, six days a week, Marina Abramović sat silently at a plain table in this room, in a work called The Artist Is Present. Anyone could sit in the chair across from her and hold her gaze for as long as they wished. More than a thousand strangers did. Some stayed a minute. Some stayed all day. Many wept, and portraits of the sitters, streaked faces and all, traveled around the world. By closing day she had sat for more than seven hundred hours, the longest single performance she had ever attempted, and the queue for that chair had become an event in its own right.

Two years later the atrium turned into a concert hall. In April 2012 the German electronic pioneers Kraftwerk performed on eight consecutive nights here, one studio album per night in order of release, from Autobahn through Tour de France, with three dimensional projections towering over the crowd.

That is the pattern to hold onto. This room is a stage as much as a gallery, and the museum treats it as a public square, a place for the one work at a time that needs all this air. So whatever you find here today, a sculpture the size of a house, dancers rehearsing, a flood of projected light, or simply the hush of the shaft itself, you are seeing the current answer to a standing question. What do you do with a hundred and ten feet of nothing? Take a slow lap of the floor, let your eyes travel up the walls one story at a time, and notice how the sound of the crowd softens as it rises. Then find the stairs beside the atrium and keep climbing. The collection unfolds from the top of the building downward, and the fifth floor, where the oldest chapters of modern art begin, is worth the whole ascent.
