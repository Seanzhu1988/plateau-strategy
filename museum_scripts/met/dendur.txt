Walk to the near edge of the water and stop there. What you are looking at is the oldest thing in this building by a wide margin, and it is not a copy. This is the Temple of Dendur, cut from Nubian sandstone on the west bank of the Nile about fifty miles south of Aswan, and finished by the year ten of the common era.

It was Roman work, which surprises people. Egypt had been a Roman province for twenty years when this was built. The emperor Augustus commissioned it, his governor Petronius had it built, and on the walls Augustus is shown in a kilt and a pharaoh's crown, making offerings to Egyptian gods. He never came anywhere near the place. A new ruler was speaking to Nubians in the visual language they already trusted.

The temple is small for something so famous. Forty three feet from front to back, twenty one across, sixteen to the roof. Look at the walls and you will see they are not vertical. They lean inward as they rise, and at the very top they throw back outward in a hollow flare above a rounded roll. That inward lean and that outward flare are Egyptian architecture in two gestures, and once you have seen them you will see them everywhere.

It is here because it was going to drown. The Aswan High Dam raised Lake Nasser over its valley, Egypt gave it to the United States in nineteen sixty five, and it arrived in more than six hundred crates.

Now look at the room, because the room is arguing with you. The pool in front of you stands for the Nile. The sloping wall behind stands for the cliffs it sat beneath. The great stippled glass wall is there to give it northern light close to the light it knew. And the temple still faces east, exactly as it did in Nubia. The museum moved the building and refused to turn it.
