Walk to the near edge of the water and stop there. What you are looking at is the oldest thing in this building by a wide margin, and it is not a copy, not a cast, not a reconstruction. This is the Temple of Dendur, cut from Nubian sandstone on the west bank of the Nile about fifty miles south of Aswan, and finished by the year ten of the common era.

It is Roman work, which surprises almost everybody. Egypt had been a Roman province for twenty years by the time the first block was set. The emperor Augustus paid for it, his governor of Egypt, Petronius, had it built, and on these walls Augustus himself appears in a linen kilt and a pharaoh's crown, making offerings to Egyptian gods. He never went anywhere near this place. What you are looking at is a new ruler speaking to Nubians in the visual language they already trusted, and it worked well enough that the building is still standing.

Now, who was it for. Isis, above all, whose great temple stood upriver at Philae. But also two local brothers, Pediese and Pihor, sons of a Nubian chieftain, who had drowned in the Nile and been treated as gods afterward. That is worth holding onto. This is an imperial building carrying an emperor's face, and it is also a shrine to two boys from the neighbourhood who died in the river.

Look at the walls now, and look at their shape rather than their carving. They are not vertical. They lean inward as they rise, and then at the very top they throw back outward in a hollow flare that sits above a rounded roll. That inward lean and that outward flare are Egyptian architecture reduced to two gestures. The gate standing in front leans harder than the temple does, which is why it reads as heavier even though it is smaller. Once you have seen that lean you will find it everywhere, in every Egyptian room in this museum.

Come closer to the stone and look for the scratches, because this building has been written on for two thousand years. The earliest graffiti is demotic Egyptian, spidery and quick, cut about five years after the temple was finished. Around four hundred of the common era there are Greek and Coptic inscriptions, from the years when the temple was pressed into service as a Christian church. And then, all over the lower stones, the names of nineteenth century travellers who arrived by boat and decided the correct response to a two thousand year old temple was to carve their own name into it. Every one of those marks is now protected as part of the object.

It is here at all because it was going to drown. The Aswan High Dam raised Lake Nasser across the whole valley, and the temple sat in the flood zone. Egypt gave it to the United States in nineteen sixty five, in thanks for American help saving the monuments of Nubia, and Jacqueline Kennedy carried that request in public.

Then something genuinely strange happened. Institutions all over America competed for it, and the newspapers called the contest the Dendur Derby. The Smithsonian wanted to put it on the banks of the Potomac. Boston proposed the Charles River. Bids came in from Cairo, Illinois, and from Memphis, Tennessee, and you can hear the joke those towns were making. The argument against every outdoor scheme was simple and correct. Sandstone that had stood in Nubian desert air for two millennia would not survive a northern winter of frost and acid rain. So it had to go indoors, and on the twenty seventh of April nineteen sixty seven it was awarded to this museum. It arrived in more than six hundred crates.

Which brings you to the room, and the room is making an argument. This is the Sackler Wing, opened in nineteen seventy eight to the design of Kevin Roche. The pool in front of you stands for the Nile that ran past the temple's front steps. The wall sloping up behind it stands for the cliffs it sat beneath. That great stippled glass wall and ceiling exist to break the light and give a Nubian building something closer to Nubian daylight.

And the temple still faces east, exactly as it faced east in Nubia. The museum moved the building four thousand miles and then refused to turn it.

Take a slow walk around the far side before you leave. Most people photograph the front and stop. The back wall carries the plainest stone in the room, and it is the surface where the graffiti is easiest to read.
