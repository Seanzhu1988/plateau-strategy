Southern Railway car number 1200. Defending Freedom, Defining Freedom: Era of
Segregation, in the history galleries, Concourse level two, one floor up from
Slavery and Freedom.

First thing: you do not stand and look at this one. You walk through it. Go in
at one end and come out the other, and pay attention to the second you cross the
partition, because that wall is the entire object.

The car is sixty nine feet long and weighs seventy seven tons. Southern Railway
listed it on paper as a partitioned coach with reclining seats. Roughly two
thirds of the length was the white section. Roughly one third was the section
marked colored. The divider is permanent. It is not a curtain or a rope, it is
built in.

Now look at the two details most people walk past.

Look up in the colored section. There are no luggage racks. This car ran the
long distance between Washington and New Orleans, so that means a full day and a
night with your suitcase stacked around your feet. Then find the restrooms and
the lounge on each side of the wall. The white side got the larger ones. The
difference was not an accident of layout, it was the design.

Here is the fact worth carrying out of the building. This car was not born
segregated. Pullman built it in 1922 as an ordinary open window coach and it ran
that way for eighteen years. In 1940 it went into a shop in Spartanburg, South
Carolina, and came out cut in two, renumbered 1200. Somebody drew that wall.
Somebody welded it in. And when the car went back through a shop in the 1950s
the partition stayed, and it returned to service in 1952.

Then there is how it got into this room, which is a good story on its own.

It was sitting at the Tennessee Valley Railroad Museum in Chattanooga. Pete
Claussen, who ran Gulf and Ohio Railways, found it and helped arrange the
donation. Restoration took years, because seventy year old rolling stock comes
with rust, lead and asbestos. Then it was trailered to Washington and streets
were closed to get it through.

In November 2013 two five hundred ton cranes lifted seventy seven tons over the
open shell of this building and lowered the car sixty feet into the lower level.
The museum would not open for another three years, in September 2016. The car
went in first because once the walls were up, nothing that size was ever getting
in. You are standing inside a building that was finished around this coach.

One last thing. There is audio playing in both sections, period dialogue.
Stand a moment on each side of the wall and listen, because it is not the same
on both sides, and neither was the trip.

Then step back outside the car and look at it whole. Green, gold lettering, one
ordinary coach. From twenty feet away nothing about it looks divided. All of it
is on the inside.
