The Star-Spangled Banner. Second floor, in the dark chamber off the central
hall. Photography is not allowed inside, so put the phone away before you go in
and give your eyes about a minute.

Walk to the far end of the rail first, before you look closely. The flag lies
almost flat, tilted back about ten degrees, and from the far end you get the
size in one go. Thirty feet by thirty four. Each stripe is two feet wide. Each
star is about two feet across, wider than your shoulders.

It used to be bigger. It was made thirty by forty two feet and it weighed
around fifty pounds, and several feet are simply gone from the flying end. Not
lost in the battle. Cut off with scissors, over the best part of a century, and
handed out as souvenirs. Count the stars while you are standing there. There
are fourteen. The fifteenth was given away to somebody and nobody today knows
who has it.

Fifteen stars and fifteen stripes, because in 1813 that was the country:
thirteen colonies plus Vermont and Kentucky. This is the only period when the
stripe count moved. Look at the material too. The stripes and the blue are
English wool bunting, the stars are cotton, and this is a warship-sized garrison
flag, built to be seen from a distance in bad weather.

A woman in Baltimore named Mary Pickersgill made it, on commission, for four
hundred and five dollars and ninety cents. She had help: her daughter Caroline,
two nieces, and a Black indentured girl of about thirteen named Grace Wisher.
Six weeks, the summer of 1813. It was far too big to lay out in her house, so
the brewer George Brown let her spread it on the floor of a malt house at his
brewery and finish it there, on her knees.

Look for the red patch on it. It is not damage. It was reportedly sewn on by
Louisa Armistead, the widow of the officer who commanded Fort McHenry and
ordered the flag in the first place, and it is the family's mark on their own
flag.

Now the part everyone half knows. On the thirteenth and fourteenth of September
1814 the British fleet shelled Fort McHenry for about twenty five hours. A
Washington lawyer named Francis Scott Key was stuck on a truce ship in the
harbour, watching, unable to leave. At dawn the flag was still up. He wrote a
poem about it, somebody set the words to an existing tune, and in 1931 that made
it the national anthem. The song came from this object. Not the other way round.

One more thing, because it is the reason you can see it at all. In 1914 a
needlewoman named Amelia Fowler and her team sewed a heavy linen backing onto
the flag to hold it together, about one point seven million stitches. By the
1990s the backing itself was the problem, so between 1998 and 2008 conservators
took every one of those stitches out, in public, behind glass, where visitors
watched them work. It cost more than twenty one million dollars.

That is why the room is so dark. The flag now sits in a two-storey chamber under
no more than one foot-candle of light, at a controlled temperature, with the
oxygen and the vibration held down. Every minute of light it gets, it never gets
back. The dimness is not atmosphere. It is the price of it still being here.
