Under the Wave off Kanagawa, the print everyone calls The Great Wave, by Katsushika Hokusai. It lives at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, in the Japanese galleries, and the number on its label is JP1847. One warning before you go looking. It is a woodblock print on paper, and paper fades, so the Met keeps it in the dark and only puts it out from time to time. If it is not on the wall, that is why.

Start with the size, because it will surprise you. The most famous wave in the world is about the size of a placemat. You have seen it a thousand times blown up on mugs and phone cases, so the real thing looks almost modest, and that is part of its power. All of that force fits in your two hands.

Now look at what is actually happening, because it is not one wave. It is a single frozen instant. Three long boats, low in the water, full of men rowing for their lives, and a wall of sea rearing up over them with its crest breaking into claws. Hokusai drew the foam as fingers, reaching down, about to close. Nobody in the boats is looking up. They are bent to the oars. You are the only one who can see what is coming.

And here is the trick that makes it great. Find the small pale triangle in the distance, in the trough of the wave. That is Mount Fuji, the sacred mountain, the tallest thing in Japan, and Hokusai has made it tiny, calm, and far away, cupped inside the hollow of the wave as if the sea could swallow it. The mountain that never moves, dwarfed for a moment by the water that never stops. That is the whole idea, hidden in plain sight.

Look at the blue. That deep dark blue in the wave was new. It is Prussian blue, a synthetic color just arriving from Europe, and Hokusai seized on it because it held its strength where the old plant blues faded. So the print is also a small record of the world opening up, a Japanese artist reaching for a German chemical to paint a wave.

Remember too that this is not a painting, it is a print, one of thousands pulled from carved wooden blocks, made to be affordable, sold cheap. It was popular culture, not a treasure, and that is why so many survive in museums around the world. The Met has this one. London has one, Tokyo has one. You are looking at an original, and there are siblings.

Hokusai made it around eighteen thirty one, when he was past seventy, part of a series on views of Mount Fuji. So when you find it, do the two things it asks. Feel the wave about to fall. Then find the little mountain, and see which one the picture is really about.
