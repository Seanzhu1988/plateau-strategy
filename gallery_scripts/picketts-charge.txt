Pickett's Charge, by Mark Bradford. Third floor, the inner ring, the round wall
that has no corners and no end.

Start by noticing the wall itself. Gordon Bunshaft designed this building as a
concrete drum, and the inner ring is one continuous curve. Almost nothing hangs
well on it. This was made for it. Eight canvases, each more than forty five feet
long, nearly four hundred feet of painting in a closed circle, unveiled here in
November 2017.

Walk it clockwise. That is the direction the thing is built to be read in, and
you cannot cheat it, because you can never see the whole work at once. Whatever
you were just looking at is behind you. That is the argument the piece is
making, so give it twenty minutes if you have them and five if you do not.

Underneath all of it is another painting. Bradford started with a printed
reproduction of Paul Philippoteaux's cyclorama of 1883, the enormous circular
canvas of the last Confederate assault at Gettysburg. He pasted that down, then
buried it under layer after layer of paper, cord and glue, then went back in and
scraped and scored and ripped and sliced down through the layers.

So here is what to look for, and it is the whole visit. Everywhere the surface
is torn, something older comes up through the gap. A horse's leg. A rifle. Part
of a face. Those are not painted by Bradford. Those are the 1883 picture,
surfacing where he cut down to it. Once you see the first one you will not stop
seeing them.

Then look sideways along the surface, with the light raking across it. You will
find raised lines running under the paper. That is the cord, laid in before the
paper went on, and the tears tend to follow it.

The panels have names. One is called Witness Tree, which is the term for a tree
that stood on the battlefield during the fighting and is still standing now, the
only living thing there that actually saw it. Another is called Dead Horse. Look
for the tree and you will find it.

Why a cyclorama. In 1883 that format was mass entertainment. You paid, you
walked into a round room, the painting wrapped all the way around you, and the
point was to convince you that you were standing in the battle. It was a machine
for making one version of history feel settled and total. Bradford has taken
that machine, wrapped it around you again in the same round shape, and then torn
it open so you can see it is made of paper.

One practical note. The Sculpture Garden across Jefferson Drive has been closed
for its rebuild and reopens on the thirty first of October 2026, with the Rodins
back outside. Until then this third floor is the reason to come in, and it is
free, like everything else on this side of the street.
