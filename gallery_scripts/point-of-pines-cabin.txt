The cabin from Point of Pines. Slavery and Freedom, in the history galleries,
Concourse level three, the lowest floor of the museum, not far from Harriet
Tubman's shawl.

First thing: walk the outside of it before you look in. It is sixteen feet by
twenty. Pace that out on the gallery floor and you have the whole footprint of a
house. Not a room. The house.

Second thing: look up inside, at the loft. Between nine and twelve people slept
up there. The ground floor was for the work of the day, cooking, washing,
mending, and the loft was where the family went at night. Hold the sixteen by
twenty in your head and put twelve people in it, and you have most of what this
object has to say.

Now the two details worth walking over for.

Count the doors. It was built with one. The second one was cut in later by the
people living there, after slavery ended, when the building stopped being
something done to them and started being somewhere they were choosing how to
live. Families added interior walls, whitewashed the outside, and painted with
the pale blue that Lowcountry households used against spirits, the colour people
there call haint blue. Every one of those changes is a decision made by somebody
who was not asked when the cabin went up.

Because it did go up as housing for enslaved people. Built in 1853 on the Point
of Pines plantation on Edisto Island, South Carolina, one of roughly ten near
identical cabins in a row, on what such places called a slave street. Eight
years later the war started. The building outlasted it.

Here is the fact worth carrying out of the building, and it is the reason a
guide brings you here rather than to a photograph.

People lived in this cabin until the 1980s.

Not a museum copy, not a reconstruction. This exact structure, standing on that
island, occupied through Emancipation, through sharecropping, through Jim Crow,
into a decade with cable television in it. Members of one family were in it from
around 1910 until the last of them moved out. Somebody alive today was a child
in this room. That is the distance the country has actually travelled, and it is
much shorter than the label makes it sound.

Then look at the boards themselves and think about how it got here. The Edisto
Island Historic Society gave it to the museum in 2013. Smithsonian staff and
people from the island took it apart by hand that May, plank by plank, over
about three weeks, recording each piece so it could be put back exactly. It was
struck from the National Register that October, because the thing the listing
described was no longer on that ground. It was on a truck to Washington.

Like the railway car one floor up, it came in before the building was closed.
The museum did not open until September 2016. This gallery was built around a
house that was already standing in it.

One last thing before you go up. Step back and look at the roofline against the
gallery wall. It is a small, plain, well made building. Whoever swung the hammer
in 1853 was almost certainly enslaved on that same plantation. The carpentry is
theirs. It is the oldest thing in this room that anyone signed with their hands.
