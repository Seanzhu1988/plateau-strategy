Washington Crossing the Delaware, by Emanuel Leutze. It hangs at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, in the American Wing, and the number on its label is 97.34. You will not miss it. It is twelve feet tall and twenty one feet wide, and it takes up most of a wall.

Start with the moment it shows. Christmas night, seventeen seventy six. The Revolution is going badly, Washington's army is beaten and shrinking, and he gambles everything on a surprise. He ferries his men across the icy Delaware River in the dark to strike the enemy at Trenton at dawn. It works. It saves the war. This painting is that crossing, the general standing in the prow, the light breaking behind him, the future of the country balanced in an open boat.

Now, the honest part, because this is a painting that has to be looked at with open eyes. Almost none of it is accurate. The flag Washington's men are holding, the stars and stripes, had not been designed yet on that night. The boats were the wrong kind. The river ice does not form in slabs like that. And no general would stand up in a small boat in a current full of ice, striking a pose. Leutze was not painting a photograph. He was painting a feeling, and he got the feeling exactly right, which is why the mistakes have never mattered.

Look at who is in the boat, because this is the part people walk past. Washington is the hero, yes, but around him Leutze painted a cross section of the whole struggling country. A Black man pulls at an oar near the front. A figure who may be a woman, in a red shirt. A man in a Scottish bonnet. A western frontiersman. Farmers. This is not one great man alone. It is a boatful of ordinary people rowing him through the ice, and Leutze wanted you to see them.

Here is the strange thing about it. Leutze was German American, and he painted this in Germany, in eighteen fifty one, more than seventy years after the event, partly to inspire the failed revolutionaries of his own Europe with the example of a revolution that won. The most American picture ever made was painted across an ocean, by a man thinking about somebody else's fight for freedom.

And it nearly did not survive. The first version was damaged in a studio fire and later destroyed in a bombing during the Second World War. The one at the Met is the second version, which he painted right after the first. So this enormous thing in front of you is itself a kind of survivor.

It became the way a whole nation pictures its own founding, printed in schoolbooks for a century, and once you have seen it you cannot unsee it. So stand back to take in the size, then step close and find the faces in the boat, the ones doing the rowing.
