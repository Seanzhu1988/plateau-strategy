Cypresses, by Vincent van Gogh. Gallery 825 at the Metropolitan Museum of Art,
and the number on the label is 49.30.

If you have seen The Starry Night, you have already seen this tree. He painted
both in the same summer, from the same asylum at Saint Remy, in the south of
France, and the cypress that rears up the left edge of that famous night sky is
this one. There he pushed it into a corner of a view it did not really belong
in. Here it gets the whole canvas.

He was obsessed with them, and he said why. He wrote to his brother Theo that the
cypress was as beautiful in line and proportion as an Egyptian obelisk, and that
nobody had yet done them as he saw them. He was right that nobody had. Painters
used cypresses as scenery, the dark vertical you put at the edge to frame a
landscape. He put one in the middle and painted it like a living thing.

Stand close and look at how the tree is built. The strokes do not lie flat. They
twist upward and curl over, thick ridges of paint laid wet into wet, so the trunk
looks like it is turning in a wind you cannot see. The green is almost black, and
there is red in it, and there is blue. Nothing here is one colour.

Then look at the sky behind it. That churning is not weather. He used the same
rolling strokes for air that he used for the tree, so the whole picture moves at
one speed, and the tree and the sky are made of the same stuff. That is the idea.
He is not painting a tree standing in front of a sky. He is painting a world in
which both are alive and neither is still.

One thing about cypresses that he certainly knew, and that changes what you are
looking at: in the south of France they are the graveyard tree. They are planted
in cemeteries and beside the dead. He was in an asylum, he had a year to live,
and he chose to paint, again and again, the tree that means death, and he painted
it as the most vividly alive thing in the field.

Give it a moment from the doorway of the gallery, from as far back as you can
get. Up close it is paint. From the door it is a flame.
