The Greensboro lunch counter. Second floor, west wing, in Unity Square. Stand
square in front of it before you read anything on the wall.

The first thing to notice is how ordinary it is. Four stools, about eight feet
of counter, chrome and vinyl, the fittings of any American five and dime in
1960. Nothing about the object is monumental. That is the point of it, and it
is why the Smithsonian went to the trouble of taking it out of a building in
North Carolina and rebuilding it here. The counter it was cut from ran far
longer than this. You are looking at one small piece of a very long piece of
furniture.

On the first of February 1960, four freshmen from North Carolina Agricultural
and Technical College walked into the F. W. Woolworth store in downtown
Greensboro. Ezell Blair Jr., who later took the name Jibreel Khazan, Franklin
McCain, Joseph McNeil and David Richmond. They shopped first, deliberately, and
kept the receipts. The store would take their money at every other counter in
the building. Then they sat down at this one, ordered coffee, and were told the
counter did not serve them.

They did not leave. They sat until the store closed. The next day they came
back with more students, and the day after that with more, and within a week
the sit-in was in other cities and other states. By April there was a new
organisation, the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, founded in Raleigh
by the students who had learned they could do this. Four teenagers on four
stools, and no strategy beyond staying seated.

Here is the part most people walk past, and it is worth the extra minute. The
Greensboro counter was desegregated in July 1960, and the first Black people
served at it were not the students. They were four of the store's own Black
employees. On the twenty fifth of July the manager had them bring dressy
clothes to work, and at about two in the afternoon, with the store quiet, they
changed out of their uniforms, sat down, and ordered from the menu. One of them
was Geneva Tisdale, a cook, who had been making the food at that counter for
years and had never been allowed to eat at it. She served herself.

The store stayed open for another thirty three years. When Woolworth closed it
in 1993, the company donated this section to the Smithsonian, and the rest of
the building in Greensboro became the International Civil Rights Center and
Museum, where the counter is still in place. If you ever get to Greensboro, you
can stand in the room this came out of.

One last thing before you move on, and it is about where you are standing. A
lunch counter turns a seated customer's back to the whole room. There is nowhere
to stand in a place like this except behind the stools, which is where the
crowds stood, the ones who came to shout and the ones who came to sit down
beside them. Four teenagers ordered coffee with their backs to all of that and
did not get up. Everything that followed came out of the decision to sit
down.
