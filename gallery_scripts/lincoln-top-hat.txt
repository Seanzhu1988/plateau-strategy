Lincoln's top hat. It is in The American Presidency: A Glorious Burden, so take
a map at the door and walk straight to it, because this is a museum where people
wander for an hour and find the hat by accident with ten minutes left.

Stand close enough to read the band. Black silk, wrapped around the base of the
crown. Lincoln had it put on for his son Willie, who died in the White House in
1862, and he never took it off the hat. A mourning band was ordinary in that
century. Wearing one for three years was not. The most photographed man in the
country walked around Washington with his grief on his head, in public, where
everyone could see it, and almost nobody remarks on it now.

The hat was made by J. Y. Davis, a Washington hatter. That is close to
everything the record says about it. Nobody knows when Lincoln bought it, and
nobody knows how often he wore it. There is one date we are certain of, and it
is the fourteenth of April, 1865, when he put it on to go to Ford's Theatre.
That is the whole documented life of the object. One maker, one certain
evening.

Now look at the height of it. Lincoln was six foot four and this adds most of
another foot. He used the hat as a filing cabinet and kept letters and notes
inside the crown, which people who worked with him wrote about at the time. He
was not a man trying to be inconspicuous, and on the night this matters, he was
sitting in a box above a full theatre where a good part of the audience had
come partly to look at him.

What happened to it afterwards tells you how raw the country was. The War
Department gathered up what Lincoln had left at the theatre, and with Mary
Lincoln's permission it went to the Patent Office, which passed the hat to the
Smithsonian in 1867. Joseph Henry, the first Secretary of the Smithsonian,
ordered his staff not to exhibit it under any circumstance, and not to mention
the matter to anyone, on account of there being so much excitement at the time.
So the most important object of the century went into a back room and stayed
there. The public did not see it again until 1893, when it was lent to an
exhibition by the Lincoln Memorial Association. Twenty six years in the dark.

Here is what to notice before you move on, and it is the reason to stand here
rather than look it up later. There is nothing presidential about the shape of
it. It is a piece of ordinary formal clothing, the sort of hat several thousand
men in that city owned, and it is in this building for one reason only, which is
where its owner was sitting when he wore it. Most of what a museum keeps is
important because of what it is. This one is important because of a single
evening it happened to be present at.

Take a moment on the mourning band on your way out. He put it there for a boy
of eleven, and it outlasted him.
