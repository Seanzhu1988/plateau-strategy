Julia Child's kitchen. National Museum of American History, first floor, east
wing, inside the exhibition called Food: Transforming the American Table. You
look into it through glass, all the way around, so walk the whole circuit before
you settle anywhere. Most people stop at the first window and leave.

Start with the walls. They are pegboard, and painted on the pegboard in black
marker is the outline of every pot and pan hanging there.

Her husband Paul drew those. He designed the whole room in 1961, in their house
on Irving Street in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and the outlines mean that anyone
cooking in here, a guest, a film crew, a friend drying dishes, can see at a
glance where a thing belongs. Take a skillet down and its shadow is still on the
wall waiting for it. It is the cheapest organising system ever devised and it is
sitting in a national museum.

Now look at the height of the counters. They are thirty-eight inches. Standard is
thirty-six. Julia Child was six foot two, and two extra inches is the difference
between a working surface and a lifetime of stooping. Paul built the room around
his wife's body. Once you see it you cannot stop seeing it, because the whole
kitchen is slightly taller than the one you have at home.

The colour is his too. That blue-green was Paul's choice.

Go around to the island in the middle. There is a gas cooktop on one side and an
electric one on the other, which is not indecision. It is a cook who wanted both
and had the room to keep both.

Here is the part that surprises people. This is not a set and not a replica. It
is the actual kitchen, fourteen feet by twenty, lifted out of the house and put
back together here exactly as it stood in November 2001, down to the hundreds of
tools. She cooked family dinners in this room for forty years. She also taped
three television series in it during the 1990s, In Julia's Kitchen with Master
Chefs, Baking With Julia, and Julia and Jacques Cooking at Home. The room did
both jobs without being redecorated for either.

She gave it to the museum in 2001, when she left Cambridge for California. It
opened here in August 2002, days after her ninetieth birthday. The copper pans
that had gone their own way were reunited with it in 2009.

Before you move on, do this. Pick one window, put your eyes at counter level, and
look across the room the way she would have. Not down at it. Across it.

That is the whole point of the kitchen. Nothing in here is rare. There is no
treasure, no gold, no object worth stealing. It is pegboard, marker pen, a
mortgage-era stove and a room built two inches too tall by a man who measured his
wife. It is in a museum on the National Mall because somebody understood that a
workplace tells you more about a person than a portrait does.
