Most people cross this floor without stopping. They come in from the east
door, see a room that goes up a hundred and twenty feet, take one photograph
of the ceiling and go looking for the elevators. Stand still for a minute
instead, and look down.

You are in the Potomac, the round gathering space at the centre of the
building. Find the tall window in the south wall, the one on your right as
you came in. Set into it are eight large prisms, an installation by the
artist Charles Ross. They are doing something to the sunlight coming through
that glass, and what they do lands on the floor you are standing on.

Around the middle of the day, when the sun is at its highest, the prisms
throw the spectrum across the stone. Not a smudge of colour: bands, stacked
in order, red through violet, laid out in a line that walks slowly across
the floor as the afternoon goes. Children find it before adults do. They
lie down in it. Nobody stops them, and nobody should.

It moves with the season as well as the hour. The higher the sun, the more
there is of it, so the strongest show of the year is around the summer
solstice in June, and on a grey January afternoon there may be nothing at
all. That is not a fault. It is the point. This is a building that tells you
what day it is.

The idea did not come from a lighting designer. Donna House, the botanist
who planned the landscape outside, used to hold crystals up in the sun to
throw rainbows. Johnpaul Jones, the lead design consultant, Cherokee and
Choctaw, took that and put it in the south wall. The building's whole
orientation runs with it. The main entrance faces east, toward the morning
sun, which is why you walked in the direction you did.

Now look outward through that window at the ground. Forty uncarved boulders
sit in the landscape out there. They are called the Grandfather Rocks, and
they were chosen from a quarry in Canada. Before they were moved, the
Montagnais blessed them for the journey. When they arrived here, they were
blessed again. They were not carved, shaped or polished for the museum. They
were asked to come.

The outside of the building is Kasota limestone, from Minnesota, and it was
deliberately given a rough, banded face so it reads as rock cut by wind and
water rather than by a saw. Douglas Cardinal, who is Blackfoot, led the
design, and the museum opened on the twenty first of September, 2004.

So here is the useful thing. Every other object in this museum will be here
at four in the afternoon exactly as it was at ten in the morning. This one
will not. If you want it, come between about eleven and two, and come on a
clear day. Then find a bench on the north side of the Potomac, sit where the
light is heading, and let it reach you.
