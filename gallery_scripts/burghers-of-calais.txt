The Burghers of Calais. Auguste Rodin, 1884 to 1889. Hirshhorn Sculpture Garden,
across Jefferson Drive from the museum building. Free, no ticket.

First, the practical thing, because it decides whether you can see it at all.
The Sculpture Garden has been closed for four years for a rebuild and reopens on
31 October 2026. Before that date there is a fence and nothing to look at. After
it, the Hirshhorn says sixty works will be out there, up from about forty, and
the Burghers is one of the pieces going back.

Now the thing worth knowing before you stand there.

In 1346 Edward III put Calais under siege. The city held out until starvation
ended it the next year, and Edward made an offer: he would spare the town if six
of its leading citizens walked out to him, nooses around their necks, carrying
the keys to the city and the castle. A wealthy man named Eustache de Saint
Pierre volunteered first. Five others followed him.

Rodin got the commission from the city of Calais in 1884. What a town council
expects for that money is a hero on a column. What Rodin gave them was six men
walking to what they believe is their execution, at the worst moment of it,
before anybody knows how it ends.

So here is what to do when you get there. Do not photograph the front. There
isn't one. Rodin refused to build the usual pyramid with a leader at the apex,
and set the six as separate men who happen to be moving in the same direction,
each one alone inside it. Walk all the way around, slowly. One holds the key. One
has the rope. One has his hands up near his head. They do not look at each
other, and no two of them are having the same feeling about this.

Then look at where your eyes land, because that is the argument. Rodin wanted
this on the ground, no pedestal, so that people going about their day in Calais
would almost walk into them. The council put it on a tall base in 1895 anyway.
Only much later was it moved in front of the new town hall and set low, the way
he asked. When you stand at the Hirshhorn cast, notice that these men are
slightly larger than you and roughly at your level. That is not a display
choice. That is the sculpture doing what it was built to do, which is to stop
being a monument you look up at and start being six people you are standing
with.

Two details most people walk past. The hands and the feet are too big for the
bodies, deliberately, because that is where he put the strain. And there is no
victory anywhere in it, which for a civic monument in the 1880s was close to an
insult.

French law allows no more than twelve original casts of any Rodin. This is one
of them, bought by Joseph Hirshhorn, whose collection started this museum.

Give it four minutes and one full circle. It is the best sculpture on the Mall
and it is outdoors and free.
