The Bell X-1, Glamorous Glennis. National Air and Space Museum, hanging in the
Boeing Milestones of Flight Hall, the big room you walk into from the Mall.

Look at the shape before you look at anything else. It is a bullet. That is not
a figure of speech. The fuselage was drawn from a Browning .50-caliber machine
gun round, because in the mid 1940s nobody knew what happened to an aircraft
past the speed of sound, and the one object anyone had watched go that fast and
stay stable was a bullet. So they built a bullet and put a man inside it.

Now find the cockpit and notice what is missing. There is no bubble canopy. The
windscreen is faired flush into the nose, because anything standing proud of
that shape would spoil it. The pilot sat down inside the bullet and looked out
through a slot.

Then look for the door, low on the right side. It is small, and it is the reason
for the best story in this hall.

This aeroplane does not take off. It lands, on the dry lakebed, but it carries
nowhere near enough fuel to spend any of it climbing, so it is carried up in
the bomb bay of a B-29 and dropped. The pilot pulls that hatch shut himself.

The weekend before the flight, Chuck Yeager fell off a horse and broke two ribs.
He was twenty-four, an Air Force captain, and he knew that reporting it would
take him off the aircraft, so he told two people: his wife Glennis, whose name
is painted on the nose, and Jack Ridley, a fellow pilot on the project. With
cracked ribs he could not haul the hatch closed against his own chest. Ridley
cut down a broom handle and handed it to him to use as a lever. That is how the
door of the first supersonic aeroplane was shut. With a piece of a broom.

The engine is a Reaction Motors XLR-11: four rocket chambers, six thousand
pounds of thrust, burning ethyl alcohol cut with water against liquid oxygen.
It runs for minutes, not hours. That is why the whole machine is only thirty
feet eleven inches long with a twenty-eight foot wing and no room for anything
you would call comfort. It is not a vehicle. It is one experiment, flown over and
over until it worked.

On the morning of the fourteenth of October, 1947, over Muroc Dry Lake in the
Mojave Desert, the B-29 let it go. Yeager lit the chambers and took it to Mach
1.06, seven hundred miles an hour, at forty-three thousand feet. First aircraft
to fly faster than sound.

Stand under the nose where you can read the name, and hold two things together.
This is the fastest anything with a person in it had ever gone. And the last
problem standing between it and the sound barrier was a door that a man with two
broken ribs could not pull shut.
