Columbia, the Apollo 11 command module. Second floor of the National Air and
Space Museum, gallery two oh six, the room called Destination Moon. It stands in
its own glass case in the middle of the floor, so you can walk the whole way
round it, and you should.

Start with the arithmetic, because it is the part the photographs cannot do. The
rocket that left Florida on the sixteenth of July nineteen sixty nine stood three
hundred and sixty three feet tall. This cone is what came home. Twelve feet ten
inches across the base, nine thousand one hundred and thirty pounds. Everything
else, the three rocket stages, the service module, the lunar lander, was burned
up, thrown away or left on the Moon. You are looking at the only piece of that
whole machine that made the round trip.

Now look closely at the surface, because most people read it as damage. It is
not damage. It is the design working. The shield is a resin and honeycomb skin
built to destroy itself, to char and melt and carry the heat away with the
material as it goes. Reentry ran somewhere near five thousand degrees Fahrenheit.
The blunt end went first, on purpose, because a blunt shape pushes the shock wave
out ahead of itself and holds the worst of the heat off the hull. Every scorch
mark you can see is heat that did not reach the three men inside.

And there were three of them, for eight days, in about two hundred and eighteen
cubic feet. That is the inside of a large car, with the seats occupied, for the
whole flight. There is no other room. What you see is the entire spacecraft.

Here is the part almost nobody standing at this glass knows. In two thousand and
sixteen the Smithsonian laser scanned the inside to build a three dimensional
model, and the scan found handwriting that nobody had looked at since nineteen
sixty nine. A small calendar drawn on a wall, days crossed off. Numbers and
coordinates in pen and pencil beside the navigation sextant, copied down off the
radio from mission control, the working notes of a man doing sums in flight.

One of those men spent the landing alone in here. While Armstrong and Aldrin were
on the surface, Michael Collins flew this ship around the Moon by himself, and on
every pass behind the far side the Moon cut the radio and there was no voice from
Earth at all for about forty seven minutes at a time.

After splashdown in the Pacific on the twenty fourth of July, Collins climbed
back into it and wrote on one of the equipment bay panels. The scan brought his
handwriting back. It reads: Spacecraft 107, alias Apollo 11, alias Columbia. The
Best Ship to Come Down the Line. God Bless Her. Michael Collins, CMP.

Before you leave, look at the case beside it. That is Neil Armstrong's suit, the
one he walked in. The two objects in this room are the ship that carried them and
the thing a man wore to stand on another world.
