The 1903 Wright Flyer. Second floor of the National Air and Space Museum, in the
gallery called The Wright Brothers and the Invention of the Aerial Age.

Start with what it is made of, because the photographs never tell you. Spruce and
ash for the frame, cotton muslin over it, and wire. The fabric was a bedsheet
cloth sold at the time under the name Pride of the West. Apart from the engine,
the chains and the wire, there is no metal in this aircraft, and the wire is what
holds the whole shape together. If you have ever built a kite, you have built
most of this.

Now find where the pilot lay. It is on the lower wing, just left of the engine, a
shallow hollow with a padded cradle in it. He did not sit. He lay flat on his
stomach with his head up, because a body lying down pushes less air than a body
sitting up, and with twelve horsepower you cannot spare any.

That cradle is the invention, and almost everyone walks past it. It slides from
side to side, and wires run from it out to the ends of the wings. When the pilot
moved his hips, the wings twisted, one trailing edge up and the other down, and
the aircraft rolled. Everyone else in the world was trying to build a machine
that would hold itself level. The Wrights built one a person could steer, by
leaning into the fall, the way you steer a bicycle. They made bicycles. Wilbur
got the idea in the shop, twisting an empty cardboard inner tube box in his
hands.

Three more things while you are here. The horizontal surface is in front, not
behind, so the aircraft is steered up and down by a wing out ahead of the pilot's
head. The two propellers, hand carved from spruce, turn in opposite directions,
driven by bicycle chains, one of them crossed to reverse it. And there are no
wheels. It ran along a wooden rail laid on the sand.

The engine was built in the back of their bicycle shop by their mechanic Charlie
Taylor. About twelve horsepower, a hundred and eighty pounds.

Four flights on the seventeenth of December, nineteen oh three. Orville first,
twelve seconds and a hundred and twenty feet, less than the wingspan of an
airliner. Wilbur, one hundred and seventy five feet. Orville again, two hundred.
Then Wilbur, fifty nine seconds and eight hundred and fifty two feet. After that
a gust caught it on the sand and wrecked it, and it never flew again. Everything
this aircraft ever did, it did in one morning.

It also nearly stayed in England. The Smithsonian spent years crediting another
man's machine as the first capable of flight, so in 1928 Orville shipped this one
to the Science Museum in London and left it there. It came home in 1948, the year
Orville died. He never saw it hanging here.
