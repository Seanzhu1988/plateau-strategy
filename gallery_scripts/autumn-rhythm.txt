Autumn Rhythm, Number 30, by Jackson Pollock. It hangs in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, in the galleries of modern and contemporary art, and the number on its label is 57.92.

First, a word about what you are not seeing here. This one is still under copyright, so we do not reproduce it. Look it up on the Met's own site, or better, go and stand in front of it, because this is a painting that photographs badly and has to be met in person. It is enormous. Nearly nine feet tall and seventeen feet wide, a wall of a painting, and the size is not a detail. It is the whole point.

Now, how it was made, because that is half of what you are looking at. Pollock did not stand at an easel. He tacked the raw canvas to the floor of a barn on Long Island and moved around it, over it, dripping and flinging and pouring ordinary house paint off sticks and stiff brushes and cans with holes in them, the brush almost never touching the surface. He called it a way of being in the painting rather than in front of it. So this is a record of movement. A man walking the edges of this thing and reaching in, and if you follow one long black line you are tracing the path his arm made in the air.

Look for the layers, because it reads as chaos and it is not. There is a ground of browns and blacks, then white laid over that, then more black, then a thin turquoise you will miss until you hunt for it. Each layer is a separate pass, on a separate day. The colors are almost all earth, black, brown, white, tan, which is where the autumn in the title comes from. No red, no blue, nothing that names a season out loud. The season is in the palette.

And here is the thing people get wrong. They say it is random. It is not. Stand back, all the way back, and the whole surface begins to breathe, an even, all over rhythm with no center, no corner more important than any other. Your eye cannot find a place to rest, so it keeps moving, and that motion is the composition. Pollock threw out the old idea that a painting should have one main event. He spread the event across the entire field, evenly, edge to edge.

He made it in nineteen fifty, at the height of a few short years when he could do this at all. The Met bought it in nineteen fifty seven, the year after he died in a car crash, which tells you how fast the world caught up with him.

So go and find it. Stand close enough to lose yourself in the tangle, then walk slowly backward until the tangle turns into weather.
