Harriet Tubman's shawl. Slavery and Freedom, in the history galleries, Concourse
level three, the lowest floor of the museum.

First thing: it is smaller than the story around it. Thirty six and a half
inches by twenty eight and a half, about ninety three centimetres by seventy
two. White silk lace and linen. Hold your hands apart the width of a bath towel
and that is the object.

Second thing: the room is dim, and that is on purpose. Light is what destroys
cloth. A textile this old is on a clock, and every hour under a bright lamp is
an hour spent. It is also why a piece like this goes back into storage for long
stretches, so if the case is empty when you come, that is the museum keeping it,
not losing it.

Now the part worth carrying out of the building.

Queen Victoria sent this around 1897, the year of her Diamond Jubilee, and
invited Tubman to the celebration in England. Tubman did not go. She was in her
seventies, she had no money, and she had just bought land next to her own house
in Auburn, New York, to make a home for the aged and the poor. The shawl came
instead, with what is reported to have been a silver Jubilee medal.

Stand there and hold the two ends of that in your head at once. A woman born
into slavery in Dorchester County, Maryland, hired out as a small child, who
watched two of her sisters sold south. Who walked herself out in 1849 and then
went back roughly thirteen times to walk other people out. Who guided the
Combahee River Raid in June 1863 and freed more than seven hundred people in a
single night. And a queen, on the other side of an ocean, sending lace.

Then look at how it got to this case, because that is the second story and
almost nobody tells it.

It was never a trophy. It stayed in the family. It came down to Meriline
Wilkins, Tubman's great great niece, who died in 2008 at ninety two, and who
left it and thirty eight other things to a historian named Charles L. Blockson.
A grade school teacher had once told Blockson that Black people had no history.
He spent the rest of his life assembling the proof.

In 2009 he gave all thirty nine items to this museum. The museum did not exist.
There was no building on this corner, just a plan and a piece of ground, and it
would not open until September 2016. He handed over Tubman's shawl, her family
photographs and a hymn book to an institution that was at that point a promise.
Blockson died in 2023, at eighty nine.

Look for the hymn book near the shawl. It was published in 1876 and it is signed
in pencil. Tubman never learned to read or write, which makes that pencil mark a
harder thing to look at than a signature usually is.

One last thing before you move on. She outlived the Jubilee by sixteen years.
She died in 1913 in Auburn, in the home she had built with her own money, and
the shawl went into a drawer in a family house and stayed there for the better
part of a century until it arrived here.
