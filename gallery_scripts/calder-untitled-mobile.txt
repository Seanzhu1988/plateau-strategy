Untitled, by Alexander Calder. East Building, hanging in the atrium. You have
almost certainly already walked underneath it.

Most people photograph this thing and move on. Do one thing first. Stand still,
pick one blade, and watch it for thirty seconds. It will move. There is no
motor anywhere in it and there never has been. It turns on the air currents of
the room you are standing in, which means the doors opening behind you and the
people crossing the bridge above you are what is driving it.

Now the number that makes the rest of it make sense. It weighs about 920
pounds, which for something 76 feet across is roughly the weight of a
motorcycle. Calder did not design it that way. He designed it in welded steel,
which is what he had used his whole working life, and in steel this shape would
have come to about 6,000 pounds. Three tons hanging over a public floor is a
problem, but the worse problem is that at three tons it would not have moved.
It would have been a very large object bolted to a ceiling. A mobile that does
not move is a failed mobile.

So it was rebuilt. The fabrication went to Paul Matisse, an artist and engineer
and the grandson of Henri Matisse, who made the blades out of aluminum
honeycomb, the kind of lightweight panel construction that comes out of
aircraft work. That is why the arms can be that long and the whole assembly can
still swing on a draft from an open door.

The commission goes back to 1973. I. M. Pei was building this atrium and wanted
something in the air in it, so the sculpture is site specific in the strict
sense. It was made for this volume and it has never hung anywhere else.

Calder approved the final form. Then he died, in November 1976, and the mobile
was not installed until the eighteenth of November 1977. He never stood where
you are standing and looked up at it. It is his largest mobile and it is his
last major one, and he only ever saw it as a model and a set of drawings.

Two places to see it from, and they give you different objects. From the floor,
directly underneath, you get the scale and you get the ceiling behind it, and
the blades read as flat shapes sliding across each other. Then go up. Take the
stairs or the lift to the upper level and find the bridge that crosses the
atrium, and from up there you are level with it rather than beneath it. From
that height you can see the whole armature at once, the way each arm hangs off
the one above it and balances the one below, and you can watch a small movement
at one end travel through the joints to the far side.

The catalogue line, if you want it, is Untitled, 1976, aluminum and steel, gift
of the Collectors Committee, accession 1977.76.1.

One last thing before you go. The whole assembly hangs from a mount fixed to
the roof of the building. Everything you are looking at, every joint and every
counterweight, is holding up a shape whose only job is to be pushed around by
the weather of a room.
