The Crypt of James Smithson. Immediately inside the north entrance of the
Castle, in the small room on your left, behind an iron gate.

Almost nobody goes in. People come through the north doors looking for the
information desk and the coffee, and the man who paid for all of it is standing
six feet away behind a grille. Go in. It takes two minutes and the room is
usually empty.

What you are looking at is a marble monument the shape of a sarcophagus, and the
body is underneath it, in a red Tennessee marble base built to hold the coffin.
Read the inscription. It says he died at Genoa on the twenty sixth of June 1829,
aged seventy five. The Smithsonian dates his birth to about 1765, which would
make him sixty four. The stone over the founder gets his age wrong by eleven
years, and nobody has ever corrected it, because nobody is entirely sure when he
was born.

Now the part worth carrying around with you. James Smithson was an English
chemist and mineralogist. He never crossed the Atlantic. He never set foot in
the United States, had no American family, no American business, and left no
explanation. He left his estate to a nephew, and wrote that if the nephew died
without heirs it should go to the United States of America, to found at
Washington, under the name of the Smithsonian Institution, an establishment for
the increase and diffusion of knowledge among men. The nephew died without heirs
in 1835. That sentence is the reason every building around you is free.

The money arrived in 1838 as one hundred and four thousand nine hundred and
sixty gold sovereigns, shipped across and melted down at the Mint in
Philadelphia into five hundred and eight thousand, three hundred and eighteen
dollars and forty six cents. Congress then argued for ten years about what to
build with it and settled on this in 1846.

He was not here for any of it. He was buried in a small cemetery in Genoa and
stayed there for seventy five years. Then the Regents learned that a quarry next
door was expanding and the cemetery was going to be moved. In December 1903 they
appointed one of their own to go and get him: Alexander Graham Bell. Bell sailed
to Italy, collected the remains, and brought them back in January 1904. There
was a procession up from the Navy Yard. Handing him over, Bell said his mission
was ended and he delivered into their hands the remains of this great benefactor
of the United States.

The monument came too. Sixteen crates, shipped out of Italy in December 1904 and
reassembled here.

Before you leave, look at the gate you walked through. It is not a decorative
grille from a catalogue. It was made from the iron fence that stood around his
grave in Genoa. They took the fence apart, brought it with the man, and built it
back across his door.

The building itself is Seneca Creek sandstone from Maryland, red, and deliberately
so. James Renwick designed it and it went up between 1847 and 1855, in a city
that was building everything else in white marble.
