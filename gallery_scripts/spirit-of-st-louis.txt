The Spirit of St. Louis. National Air and Space Museum, on the National Mall.
The galleries here reopened in stages after the renovation and this aircraft has
been moved more than once, so check the map at the door.

Walk around to the nose and look for the windshield. There isn't one.

That is the thing to notice, and almost nobody does, because you expect a
cockpit to have a window in front of it. Lindbergh had the main and forward fuel
tanks built into the fuselage ahead of the pilot, with the oil tank behind them
acting as a firewall. He wanted the weight in front of him rather than at his
back. The price of that decision was his forward vision. He crossed the Atlantic
seeing out the side windows.

A Ryan employee who had served in submarines built him a periscope, which
Lindbergh helped design. It is not clear he ever used it.

Now find the seat, if you can get an angle through the door. It is wicker. Stiff,
fitted to his tall thin frame, and deliberately uncomfortable. He was going to be
alone in that chair for a day and a half with nobody to take a turn, and the
thing most likely to kill him was falling asleep. So the chair was built to stop
him getting any rest at all.

The aircraft itself is a Ryan M-2, a strut-braced mail plane, heavily rebuilt.
Donald Hall designed the changes under Lindbergh's direct supervision, and Ryan's
staff built the whole machine in sixty days. The wings were stretched to
forty-six feet to carry the fuel. It is only twenty-seven feet seven inches long,
which is shorter than most people picture, and standing beside it is the moment
the flight stops being an abstraction.

One engine. A Wright Whirlwind J-5C, two hundred and twenty-three horsepower,
air-cooled, sitting uncovered on the front. There is no second engine, because a
second engine is a second thing that can fail, and there is no second man,
because a second man is weight. Everything about this aeroplane is one decision
made over and over: carry fuel, carry nothing else.

He left New York on the twentieth of May, 1927, and landed at Le Bourget outside
Paris thirty-three hours and thirty minutes later, on the twenty-first. First
solo nonstop crossing of the Atlantic.

Stand where you can see the nose and the tail at once and hold two numbers
together. Thirty-three and a half hours. No forward window. He flew across an
ocean at night, in fog and ice, navigating by dead reckoning, and for the entire
crossing the direction he was actually going was the one direction he could not
look.
