Nighthawks, by Edward Hopper. Gallery 262 at the Art Institute of Chicago, and
the number on the label is 1942.51.

Start with the thing most people never notice, and once you notice it you cannot
stop. There is no door. Look along the glass, all the way round the corner and
down the near side. There is no way into that diner and no way out of it. The
four people inside are sealed in a glass box, and the painting will not tell you
how they got there.

That glass is the second thing. It is one enormous curved sheet with no frame and
no visible edge, which real diners in nineteen forty two did not have, because
nobody could make plate glass bend like that cheaply. He invented it, so that
nothing would interrupt your view and nothing would let you in.

Now the street. It is empty. Not quiet, empty. No cars, no passers by, no litter,
no dog. The shops opposite are shut and their windows are dark. The only light in
the whole picture comes from inside that room, and it spills out onto the
pavement in a hard flat sheet, because the diner is lit by fluorescent tubes,
which were new then. Hopper is one of the first painters to notice what
fluorescent light does to a face at two in the morning, which is nothing kind.

Look at the people. The couple's hands are nearly touching and are not. Neither
of them is looking at the other. The man with his back to you is a shape, a hat
and a shoulder, and you will never see his face. The server is bent, busy, the
only one doing anything. Four people in a lit room and not one connection between
them.

Hopper finished it weeks after Pearl Harbor. People have read the war into it
ever since, and he denied it flatly. He said he was probably painting the
loneliness of a large city, and then added, unconsciously. His wife Josephine,
who posed for the woman, kept the notebooks, and her entry for this painting says
the man is a sinister figure and the whole thing suggests night, and nothing more.

So do not look for a story. There is not one. That is what the missing door is
for. You are outside on an empty street at two in the morning, looking at people
you cannot reach and will never know, and the painting has arranged, very
carefully, for you to stay out there.
