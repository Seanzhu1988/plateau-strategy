Warriors' Circle of Honor, the National Native American Veterans Memorial.
Outside on the museum grounds, at the end of the curving stone path through the
trees. No ticket, and it is open at every hour, including after the museum has
locked its doors.

Stand on the granite first. The paving under your feet is a set of concentric
rings with an opening at each of the four directions, so there is no front and
no back and no queue. Walk in from whichever side you arrived on. That is the
first thing this memorial does differently from everything else on the Mall.

The circle is stainless steel, about twelve feet across, standing upright on a
carved stone drum. Look through it rather than at it. The middle is empty on
purpose, so what you see inside the ring is the sky and the trees and whoever is
standing on the far side. Water runs across the drum beneath it. That water is
not decoration for you, it is there so that ceremonies can be held here, and
they are.

Now the four lances at the edge. If there is cloth tied to them, visitors tied
it. Veterans, families and tribal leaders are invited to tie a cloth for a
prayer, and anyone may, which means the memorial is never quite finished and
looks slightly different every week. Most people walk past without knowing they
are allowed. You are allowed.

The man who designed it is Harvey Pratt, Cheyenne and Arapaho of Oklahoma. He is
a Marine, a Vietnam veteran, a Southern Cheyenne Peace Chief, and he spent his
working life as a police forensic artist in Oklahoma, drawing faces from
descriptions. He is not an architect by trade. His concept was chosen unanimously
by the jury out of more than one hundred and twenty submissions.

Here is the fact worth carrying with you. Congress authorized this memorial in
1994. Nothing was built. The law was amended in 2013, the design competition
followed, and the memorial finally opened on Veterans Day in 2020, twenty six
years after it was approved. It opened to an empty lawn, because that was the
pandemic autumn and the unveiling had to be held online. So the veterans it
honors did not get to walk into their own memorial until two years later.

That day came on the eleventh of November, 2022. More than fifteen hundred
Native veterans from more than one hundred and twenty Native nations processed
onto these grounds and around this circle. If you want one image to keep from
this stop, keep that one.

Read the engravings on the benches before you leave. Every branch of the armed
forces is here, because the people this honors served in all of them, and in
every American conflict going back to the Revolution. American Indian, Alaska
Native and Native Hawaiian, named separately on the memorial's own terms rather
than folded into one word.

One practical note. Because it sits in the trees off the main path, most visitors
leaving the museum turn toward the Mall and never see it. Come out and turn onto
the stone path instead. It is a three minute walk and it is usually quiet.
