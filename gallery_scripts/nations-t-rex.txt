The Nation's T. rex. The David H. Koch Hall of Fossils, Deep Time, ground floor.
You will find it in the middle of the hall, crouched over a fallen Triceratops.

Do not walk straight at it. Come round to the left side first, to the arm.

That arm is the reason this particular animal matters. When it came out of the
ground in Montana it brought with it the first complete Tyrannosaurus forelimb
anybody had ever found. Everything written before that about how these arms
worked was assembled from pieces. Two fingers, and the whole thing is roughly
the length of your own arm on an animal that weighed as much as a delivery
truck. People laugh at it. Then somebody points out that the bone walls are
thick and the muscle attachment scars are enormous, and the laughing usually
stops.

Now look at the colour of the bones, and this is the part almost nobody is told.
Not all of what you are looking at is fossil. About eighty five percent of the
skeleton was recovered, which is extraordinary, and the missing pieces were
filled in with casts. The casts are deliberately a slightly different shade. You
are allowed to see the join. Stand back a step, let your eye run along the spine
and down the ribs, and the two tones separate. That is the museum telling you
exactly how much of this is the real animal.

The animal was about eighteen years old when it died. That is not a guess. You
can cut a fossil bone the way you read a tree, count the growth lines, and get
an age.

Here is how it got here. In 1988 a rancher named Kathy Wankel was out on
federal land near the Fort Peck Reservoir in eastern Montana and saw bone
weathering out of the ground. She reported it. A team from the Museum of the Rockies, working under Jack Horner,
dug it out over 1989 and 1990. Because it was found on public land, the fossil
belonged to the United States Army Corps of Engineers, which is why for years
this was called the Wankel rex and is now called the Nation's T. rex. It belongs
to the country in a way that most museum dinosaurs do not.

It reached Washington on the fifteenth of April 2014, by truck, on a fifty year
loan from the Corps of Engineers. This building was one
hundred and three years old and had never had a Tyrannosaurus rex of its own.

Then look at the pose, because it was argued over. It is not standing and
roaring at you. It has one foot on the Triceratops, its jaws are closing on the
back of the skull, and it is about to take the head off. That is a specific
claim about behaviour: the neck is where the meat is and where the bone is
thinnest, and modern predators do exactly this. The museum chose an animal doing
its job rather than an animal posing for you.

Which is also why the Triceratops matters, and most people never look down.
It is not scenery. It is the second half of the sentence. A predator on its own is a portrait. A predator
and its prey is an ecosystem, and an ecosystem is what this whole hall is about.

One more thing worth thirty seconds. The hall itself. It opened on the eighth of June 2019 after five years
closed, thirty one thousand square feet, more than seven hundred specimens. If
you came here as a child before 2014 you saw a different room entirely, with the
dinosaurs standing in rows facing the aisle like portraits in a gallery. This
version runs backwards through time, from now to the deep past, and it puts you
inside the story rather than in front of it.

Best time to stand here: right at opening, ten o'clock, or after about half past
three when the school groups have gone. In the middle of the day you will be
looking at this over shoulders.

One last thing before you move on. Go round to the far side, get low, and look
up along the jaw from underneath. That is the angle the Triceratops had.
