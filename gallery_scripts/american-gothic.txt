American Gothic, by Grant Wood. It hangs at the Art Institute of Chicago, in Gallery 263, and the number on its label is 1930.934. This one is still under copyright, so we do not reproduce it here. Look it up on the Art Institute's own site, or go and see it, because it is small and quiet and rewards standing close.

You already know it, even if you think you do not. A stern man in overalls holding a pitchfork, a woman beside him in a colonial print apron, a white farmhouse behind them with one pointed window. It is on mugs and cartoons and a thousand parodies. It may be the most recognized American painting there is. And almost everything people assume about it is up for argument.

Start with who they are. Everyone says husband and wife. Wood said no. He meant a farmer and his unmarried daughter, the two of them standing for a whole way of rural life. The models were people from his own world, his dentist and his sister, posed separately, in a studio, not on a farm at all.

Now the window, because that is where the title comes from. Look at the little house behind them and find the upstairs window, the pointed arch one. That is a style called Carpenter Gothic, a bit of borrowed medieval fancy on a plain wooden farmhouse, and Wood saw a real one in a small Iowa town and built the whole picture around it. American Gothic. The title is about the house first, and only then about the two faces in front of it.

Here is the fight that has never ended. Is he mocking them or honoring them. When it was first shown, in nineteen thirty, city critics took it as a joke, a jab at narrow, sour, small town America. Some Iowans were furious. But Wood, painting in the first year of the Great Depression, said he meant it warmly, as a picture of the plain, stubborn, dependable people who he thought would hold the country together when everything else was falling apart. Look at the faces and decide for yourself. That tightness around the mouth, is it disapproval, or is it endurance. The painting will not settle it, and that refusal is exactly why it has lasted.

Look at how it is made, too. Everything is crisp, hard edged, almost too clear, the seams of the overalls, the rickrack on the apron, each prong of the pitchfork. Wood loved the old Northern painters of Flanders and Germany, and he borrowed their sharp, patient finish and pointed it at an Iowa porch. The pitchfork, by the way, rhymes with the seams of the man's overalls and the mullions of that window. He tied the whole picture together with the shape of three lines.

So go and stand in front of it. It is quieter than its fame. Read the two faces, pick your side, and know that half the room beside you has picked the other.
