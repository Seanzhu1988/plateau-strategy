The African bush elephant. The Rotunda, National Museum of Natural History,
ground floor. You cannot miss him. Come in from the Mall doors and he is
straight ahead, thirteen feet two inches at the shoulder, under the dome.

Almost everybody walks up to the front, takes the photograph and leaves. Give
him two more minutes and go round to the left, because the thing worth noticing
is not the size. It is the pose.

He is walking. Trunk lifted, ears fanned out, front foot coming forward. That is
not how a trophy is usually mounted, and it did not happen by accident. William
L. Brown, the Smithsonian's chief taxidermist, worked on this hide with Norman
Deaton for about sixteen months, and while he did it he went across town to the
National Zoo and watched live elephants move. What he built is a specific gait,
a fast walk, caught mid stride. Once you have seen it you cannot unsee it. Stand
at the side, not the front, and the animal is going somewhere.

Now the tusks, and this is the part nobody tells you. They are fake. The real
ones weigh about ninety three pounds each, and no hide and armature will hold
that much ivory in the air for decades without deforming. What you are looking
at are fiberglass casts, installed in 1988 to replace earlier casts made of
celluloid. The ivory itself is in the museum's collection. It is a fair trade,
and the museum has never hidden it.

He has a name, informally. Henry. His formal name is the Fenykovi elephant,
after Josef Fenykovi, a Hungarian big game hunter living in Spain, whose
expedition shot this animal in southeastern Angola in November 1955. Fenykovi
donated the hide to the Smithsonian. The mount was unveiled in March 1959, and
at that moment it was the largest land mammal on display in any museum in the
world. It still is.

Here is the part that makes him worth stopping for rather than just
photographing, and you are standing on the evidence.

Look at what he is standing on. In 1959 it was a plain pedestal. In 1999 the
museum built him an open diorama of the Angolan savanna, which lifted him about
three feet higher and put him back in a landscape. In 2015 they took the
savanna away again and put in the straight edged marble platform you see now,
which carries a visitor information desk and panels about elephant ecology,
about where elephants sit in geologic time, and about poaching.

Read that sequence again. The animal has not moved since 1959. The story
underneath him has been rewritten twice. He arrived as a hunting trophy, the
largest one anybody had. He became a habitat specimen. He is now the museum's
argument about why elephants are being killed and what is lost when they are.
Same hide, same pose, three different meanings, and the only thing that changed
was the label and the floor.

Two more things while you are here.

If you get close on the left flank and look at the skin in raking light, you
will find small patched areas slightly different in colour. In 2025 conservators
cleaned him one brushstroke at a time and filled the cracks with hand coloured
beeswax. Eleven tons of animal, cleaned with brushes. That is what conservation
actually looks like.

And the second thing: this rotunda is where everybody in the building agrees to
meet, which is why the desk is built into his pedestal. If you are here with
people and you are going to split up, this is the place to say you will come
back to. There is no better landmark in the museum.

Best time. Right at opening, ten o'clock, you get the floor almost to yourself
and the light from the upper windows is coming down across the ears. By eleven
there is a ring of people two deep at the front. Late afternoon, after about
half past three, it clears again.

One last thing before you go. Step back to the far wall, look up, and take in
how much of the dome he fills. Then remember he was a real animal, walking in
Angola, seventy years ago.
