The Hope Diamond. The Harry Winston Gallery, at the entrance to the Janet
Annenberg Hooker Hall of Geology, Gems and Minerals.

First thing: it is smaller than you are expecting. About an inch across, twenty
five millimetres, the size of a large walnut. Forty five and a half carats.
People arrive braced for something the size of a fist and are quietly
disappointed for about four seconds, and then they see the colour.

You do not have to fight for a spot at the glass. The case turns. Stand still,
let it come round to you, and watch it through one full rotation, because the
blue is not the same blue from every angle.

The colour is boron. A few boron atoms scattered through the carbon, a handful
per million, and that is the entire difference between this and a clear stone.
Boron also makes it a semiconductor, which almost no diamond is. If you put it
under ultraviolet light and then switch the light off, it does not glow blue. It
glows red, and keeps glowing after the lamp is dead. That red is a fingerprint,
and it is how the scientists here tell it from every other blue diamond.

Now look at what it is mounted in, because nobody does. Sixteen white diamonds
around the blue one, and the chain holding it carries forty five more. Most
people look at the middle and never count the rest.

The story, quickly, and it is a real one. A French merchant named Jean Baptiste
Tavernier bought a rough blue stone of about a hundred and twelve carats in
India, from the Kollur mine, and sold it to Louis the Fourteenth in 1668. The
king's cutter recut it in 1678 down to sixty seven carats and it became the
French Blue, the crown jewel of France. On the eleventh of September 1792, in
the middle of the Revolution, the royal storehouse in Paris was robbed over
several nights and the stone vanished.

It reappears in London in September 1812, recut again, at forty five and a half
carats. Twenty years and two days after the robbery. Under French law at the
time, twenty years was the limit for prosecuting the theft. Whoever surfaced it
waited out the clock.

The name comes from Henry Philip Hope, a London banker whose gem catalogue of
1839 lists it. Cartier sold it in 1911 to Evalyn Walsh McLean of Washington, who
wore it to parties and once let her dog wear it. Harry Winston bought her whole
collection in 1949 and gave this stone to the nation in 1958.

He sent it in the post. Plain registered mail, brown paper, two dollars and
forty four cents in postage, a hundred and forty two dollars of insurance on a
million dollar valuation. A postman named James Todd carried it up the steps of
this building in a paper package and handed it over the counter.

One last thing. For centuries nobody could prove this stone was the French Blue.
Then in 2005 a lead cast of the missing jewel turned up in a drawer in a Paris
museum, and the model settled it. It is the same stone.
