Ginevra de' Benci. West Building, main floor, in the Italian Renaissance rooms.

Start with the size, because photographs lie about it. Fifteen inches by
fifteen, near enough square. This is the only painting by Leonardo da Vinci on
permanent public view anywhere in the Americas, and you could cover it with a
sheet of paper.

Second thing, and it is the thing to look for. The panel has been cut. Look at
the bottom edge. At some point, most likely because of damage, the lower part
was taken off, and what went with it were her arms and her hands. A Leonardo
portrait with the hands removed. If you want to feel what is missing, think of
the Mona Lisa, painted about thirty years later, and put those folded hands back
along the bottom of this one.

Now the shrub. That dark, spiky bush crowding in behind her head is a juniper.
Juniper in Italian is ginepro. Her name is Ginevra. The plant is a pun on her
name, and Leonardo built the whole background out of it.

The half almost nobody looks for is the back. This panel is painted on both
sides. On the reverse there is a sprig of juniper again, held inside a wreath of
laurel and palm, and a scroll with six Latin words: VIRTVTEM FORMA DECORAT.
Beauty adorns virtue.

Here is what makes that sentence interesting. Laurel and palm were not her
emblem. They were the personal emblem of Bernardo Bembo, the Venetian ambassador
to Florence, a married man who admired her and whose circle wrote poems to her.
When conservators put the panel under infrared, Bembo's own motto, Virtue and
Honor, turned up underneath the paint of the reverse, painted over. So the back
of this picture was altered, and the man behind the commission is probably him.

She was real, and young. Born in 1457, daughter of Amerigo Benci, a Florentine
banker close to the Medici. Educated, which in that house was possible. Married
at sixteen, in 1474, to Luigi Niccolini. Poets dedicated verses to her, Bembo
among them, and so did Lorenzo de' Medici.

Of her own writing, one line survives. The opening of a sestina. It reads: I ask
your forgiveness and I am a mountain tiger.

Stand where you can see her eyes and hold there for a moment. She is not
softened. The mouth is set, the gaze goes past your shoulder, and the skin is
built up in thin layers with no visible brushwork at all, which is the young
Leonardo already doing the thing he became famous for.

Last, how it got here. In February 1967 the National Gallery bought it from
Franz Joseph II, Prince of Liechtenstein, out of the princely collection. The
price was somewhere between five and six million dollars, a record for any
painting at the time. Every other painting anyone attributes to Leonardo
sits outside the Americas. This one is fifteen minutes from the Capitol.
