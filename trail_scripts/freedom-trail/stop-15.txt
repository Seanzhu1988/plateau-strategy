You have crossed the water into Charlestown, and there in the old navy yard, masts rising over the docks, is a wooden warship that has been afloat for more than two centuries. This is the USS Constitution, the one they call Old Ironsides, launched in seventeen ninety seven, and she is the oldest commissioned warship still floating anywhere in the world. Not the oldest in a museum. The oldest still on the active list of a navy. She is technically still a United States warship, and the sailors who show you around are real active duty Navy, which is why, before you get too excited, you should know you will need to bring a photo identification to get aboard. This is a working military base, not a monument, and that is the whole point of her.

Start with the nickname, because it is the best story she has. In the War of Eighteen Twelve, this ship fought a British frigate called the Guerriere out in the Atlantic. The two ships hammered each other at close range, and something extraordinary happened. British cannonballs struck the Constitution's hull and bounced off. They did not punch through. The ship was built of live oak, an incredibly dense southern hardwood, with a hull nearly two feet thick in places, and the shot simply could not get through it. A sailor watching it supposedly cried out that her sides were made of iron, and the name stuck. Old Ironsides. She was not actually armored. She was just built so stoutly out of the right wood that she might as well have been.

And she won. Over and over. In a war that went badly for the young United States in many places, this ship was almost pure good news, undefeated in battle, taking British warships at a time when the Royal Navy was thought to be untouchable. For a new country badly in need of proof that it could stand up to the greatest naval power on earth, the Constitution was that proof, floating and firing and coming home victorious. She was a morale machine as much as a warship, and the country loved her for it.

There is another Boston thread here, because of course there is. She was built right here in Boston, in a shipyard in the North End, and the copper that fastened her together, the bolts and spikes and fittings, came from the foundry of Paul Revere. That name again. His copper is in the State House dome, his bell is in King's Chapel, his engraving inflamed the Massacre, and his metal holds this ship together. The man got everywhere.

Now the part that should sound familiar, because it is the theme of this entire trail. By the eighteen thirties, the Constitution was old and worn, and the Navy planned to scrap her, to break up the undefeated ship for salvage. And what saved her was not a battle. It was a poem. A young writer named Oliver Wendell Holmes read that they meant to destroy her and wrote a furious poem called Old Ironsides, and it spread across the country and stirred up such public outrage that the Navy backed down and rebuilt her instead. A ship that survived every British broadside was nearly killed by a budget, and was rescued by a piece of writing. You have heard that shape all day. The thing survives because at the last moment somebody refused to let it go.

She is still cared for the way a living ship is cared for. Every so often they turn her around in the harbor so that she weathers evenly on both sides, and now and then, on her own power under sail, she still goes out onto the water, more than two hundred years old, moving under the wind. Active duty sailors consider it a plum assignment to serve aboard her, to wear the old uniform and walk the deck and keep the oldest ship in the Navy alive for the next generation.

So go aboard if the line is not too long, mind the low beams below deck, and let a sailor tell you about the guns. Then look up at the masts and the rigging against the sky and understand what you are seeing. This is not a replica and it is not a relic. It is the same ship, still commissioned, still crewed, still afloat, that beat the Royal Navy when the country was brand new. From here only one stop remains, the tall grey obelisk on the hill above you, where the battle happened that made everyone believe this whole improbable thing might actually work. Follow the brick up the hill. It is the last climb.
