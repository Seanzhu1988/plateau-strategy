You have crossed to a small traffic island in the middle of the street, and set into the pavement is a ring of cobblestones. That is all it is, a circle of stones in the road, with cars going around it. But this is the Boston Massacre site, the spot where five men were killed on the fifth of March, seventeen seventy, and it is one of the places on the trail where the marker is deliberately humble because the event was not. Look back up at the balcony of the Old State House behind you, because the two places are one story, and now you are standing in its second half.

Here is what happened on this ground. It was a cold night with snow on the streets. A single British sentry stood guard outside the custom house, right about here, and a crowd began to gather and taunt him, the way crowds had been taunting soldiers all winter, because the town was sick of being occupied. The mood turned. People threw snowballs, then ice, then chunks of wood. A squad of soldiers came out to support the lone sentry, and they found themselves ringed by an angry mob in the dark, shouting at them to fire, daring them. Someone was knocked down. A gun went off, then more, and when the smoke cleared, men were lying in the snow. Five of them would die.

Learn their names, because the trail is easy to walk without ever hearing them. Samuel Gray. James Caldwell. Samuel Maverick, only seventeen, an apprentice. Patrick Carr, an Irish immigrant who lived a few days and then died of his wounds. And the first to fall, at the front of the crowd, a man named Crispus Attucks, a sailor and dockworker of African and Wampanoag descent, a man of both African and Native American blood, who had once escaped slavery and made his life on the waterfront. The first person to die in the long chain of events that led to American independence was a Black and Native man, and that fact sits at the very center of the story, in the pavement, at the front of the crowd.

Now here is the part that makes this spot more than a grievance, the part that shows you what kind of revolution this was going to try to be. The soldiers were arrested and put on trial for murder. And the lawyer who stood up to defend them, the British soldiers who had fired on his own townspeople, was John Adams. Adams was as much a patriot as anyone on this trail. He would help write the Declaration and become the second president. And he took the case for the defense because he believed, fiercely, that even these men, even now, deserved a fair trial, and that a town which lynched its enemies in the street was no better than the tyranny it was resisting. He said the trial was one of the best things he ever did for his country. He believed the rule of law mattered more than the verdict, that if the new world was going to be worth building it had to hold to its principles exactly when it was hardest.

He won, mostly. Most of the soldiers were acquitted. Two were convicted only of manslaughter. A Boston jury, in a town boiling with rage, looked at the evidence and refused to simply convict men because the crowd wanted them convicted. It is one of the most quietly radical things on this whole trail. Not the shooting. The trial.

At the same time, Paul Revere, silversmith and propagandist, made an engraving of this night, a vivid picture of a line of soldiers firing on peaceful citizens, and it was not accurate, and it was not meant to be. It was designed to inflame, and it did. So this one spot holds both halves of how a revolution actually runs. The cold fury of the propaganda that rallied people to the cause, and the cold discipline of the courtroom that insisted on being fair even to the enemy. Both were Boston. Both were necessary. Neither one alone would have built a country worth having.

Stand a moment on the stones, mind the traffic, and look from the ring in the street up to the balcony above, from the place where five men died to the place where the Declaration was read six years later. Then follow the brick on toward a great brick hall with a golden grasshopper on top, where the case for the whole revolution was argued out loud, and where, fittingly, they have been buying and selling groceries the entire time.
