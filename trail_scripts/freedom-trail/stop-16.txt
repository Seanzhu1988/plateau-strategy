This is it, the end of the trail. The tall grey granite obelisk in front of you is the Bunker Hill Monument, and it marks the battle that, more than any single event, convinced both sides that the American Revolution was a real war that the colonists might actually win. It is two hundred and twenty one feet of solid granite, and there are two hundred and ninety four steps to the top, and there is no elevator, so decide now how badly you want the view.

First, a correction that Bostonians enjoy pointing out. This is the Bunker Hill Monument, but it does not stand on Bunker Hill. The battle was mostly fought on Breed's Hill, which is where you are standing, right next to the smaller hill it got named after. The names got tangled in the retelling and never got untangled, so the most famous monument of the battle sits on the wrong hill, and everyone has just agreed to live with it. It is a fitting last lesson for this trail, that history is messier than the plaque, and the story we remember is not always exactly the story that happened.

Now the battle, in June of seventeen seventy five, because the outcome is not what you would guess from the size of the monument. The colonists lost. Let me say that plainly, because it matters. The Americans dug in on this hill overnight, the British attacked up the slope in the heat of the day, and it came down to the American militia running low on gunpowder. Powder was so scarce that the officers gave the order that became legend, do not fire until you see the whites of their eyes, not out of bravado but out of arithmetic. They could not afford to waste a single shot at long range. So they held, and held, and let the redcoats come terribly close before they fired, and the first two British assaults were shredded and thrown back down the hill. But on the third assault the Americans were finally out of powder, and they had to retreat, and the British took the hill.

So the British won the ground. But look at the cost. Over a thousand British soldiers were killed or wounded taking this one small hill from a ragtag colonial militia that most professional officers had assumed would break and run at the first volley. They did not break. They stood, and they made the finest army in the world bleed for every step. And that changed everything, on both sides. The British suddenly understood they were in for a long, brutal, expensive war, not a quick police action against a rabble. And the Americans understood something even more important. They understood that they could stand against regular troops, that ordinary farmers and tradesmen behind a dirt wall could hold their ground against the best in the world. They lost the hill and won their own belief. After Bunker Hill, the war felt winnable, and a war you believe you can win is a war you are willing to keep fighting.

A good man died up here too. Doctor Joseph Warren, one of the leaders of the Boston patriots, the man who had actually sent Paul Revere out on his ride, refused the safety his rank allowed him and fought here as an ordinary soldier, and he was killed on this hill. The people who started the thing did not just make speeches in warm halls. Some of them died in the dirt on this slope.

The monument itself took a long time and a lot of struggle to build, which by now will not surprise you. The cornerstone was laid fifty years after the battle by the Marquis de Lafayette, the Frenchman who had fought for the Revolution as a young man and came back as an old hero. The money kept running out, and the project was rescued at one point by a fair organized largely by women who refused to let it fail. It took nearly twenty years to finish. Nothing about American memory has ever been simple or cheap.

So here you are at the end. If your legs have anything left, climb the two hundred and ninety four steps, and know that most people lose count somewhere around two hundred and arrive at the top gasping and quietly proud. The view is the whole city you just walked across, laid out below you, from this hill all the way back to the Common where you started. Two and a half miles, sixteen stops, almost four hundred years of a city that never stopped using its own history. You followed a line of red brick through the middle of it, and it brought you here, to the hill where a defeated militia proved a country was possible. Well walked. This is the end of the Freedom Trail.
