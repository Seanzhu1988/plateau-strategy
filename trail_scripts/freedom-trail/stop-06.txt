This next stop asks you to look down, not up, because the thing you came to see is set into the pavement under your feet. There is a colorful mosaic in the sidewalk, right in front of the old city hall, and it marks the site of the Boston Latin School, the first public school in America. The schoolhouse itself is long gone. What is left is the ground it stood on and this mosaic laid into the walk to say, here, it started here.

It started in sixteen thirty five. Think about how early that is. The town of Boston was five years old. There was barely a Boston yet, a rough cluster of houses on a peninsula at the edge of a continent, and one of the first things these people did, before they were comfortable, before they were safe, was found a school and open it to the sons of the town, free. That instinct, that a community owes its children an education as a public duty and not a private luxury, is one of the oldest ideas this city gave the country, and it is written into the sidewalk here because the building could not survive but the idea did.

Now, who sat in that schoolroom. This is where it gets remarkable for a spot most people walk straight over. Five of the men who would go on to sign the Declaration of Independence were students at Boston Latin. Samuel Adams learned his Latin here. So did John Hancock. So did Robert Treat Paine and William Hooper. And so, most famously, did Benjamin Franklin, who has a statue standing right here on the spot, the first portrait statue ever raised in the city of Boston. Look at Franklin's bronze face and enjoy the irony, because Franklin is the one who did not finish. He was a brilliant boy from a poor family with too many children, and the money for schooling ran out, so he was pulled from Boston Latin before he graduated and put to work. The other boys stayed and collected their credentials. Franklin left early, taught himself everything, and became the most famous American in the world. The statue of the dropout stands on the ground of the school he dropped out of.

So the spot holds a small joke and a large truth at the same time. The joke is that the most celebrated student of the first public school is the one who never graduated from it. The truth underneath is that this scrappy town school, free and open, was turning out the men who would argue the colony into a country. You could get a first rate education here without a penny of family money, and the Revolution was in no small part an argument written by people who had.

The school did not die when the building did. Boston Latin still exists, still public, still free, still one of the best schools in the city, though it moved off this spot long ago to a larger home. It has been running, in one place or another, since sixteen thirty five, which makes it the oldest school of any kind in the country. When you stand on this mosaic you are standing at the root of something that never stopped growing.

Take a moment for the building behind the statue too, the grand old pile with the steep French roof. That is the old city hall, where Boston governed itself for a century, and it is a reminder that this short stretch of School Street was for a long time the civic heart of the city. The school, the seat of government, the churches you just passed, all within a couple of blocks. This was where Boston did its serious business of running itself and teaching its young, side by side.

Then look one more time at the pavement, at the mosaic bright against the grey stone, and appreciate that the trail asks you to honor a school by looking at the ground. There is no grand hall to enter here, no relic to file past. There is a good idea, marked where it was born, and a bronze schoolboy who proves that what you make of an education matters more than whether you finish it. From here the trail carries on to a small old brick building on the next corner, a bookstore that became the beating heart of American literature, and very nearly became a parking lot. Keep following the brick.
