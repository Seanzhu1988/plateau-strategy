Just down the block you come to a heavy grey stone building with a squat, square tower, and this is King's Chapel. It looks solid and settled now, part of the furniture of the city, but it arrived in Puritan Boston as an unwelcome guest, and the story of how it got built is the story of the whole religious quarrel that shaped this town.

Remember why Boston existed. The Puritans came here to get away from the Church of England. They crossed an ocean specifically so they would not have to worship the king's way, and they built a city where their way was the only way. So when the royal governor decided, in the sixteen eighties, that Boston would have an Anglican church, a Church of England church, right in the middle of town, it landed like an insult. Which it partly was. It was the crown reminding the colony who was in charge.

And here is how deep the resentment ran. When the governor went to build the church, no one in Boston would sell him the land. Not a single plot. A whole city quietly closing its doors. So the governor simply took a corner of the town's oldest burying ground, ground the town did not want to give, and put the Anglican church on top of the graves. That is why King's Chapel sits where it does, hard against the oldest cemetery in Boston. It was built on the one piece of land the colony could not refuse because the governor did not ask.

The first church here was a small wooden one. The grand stone building you are looking at came later, in the seventeen forties, and the way they built it is my favorite piece of engineering on the trail. The congregation was still using the old wooden church and did not want to stop holding services while the new one went up. So they built the new stone church around the outside of the old wooden one. The old church kept running, services and all, inside the shell of the new one rising around it. And when the stone walls were finished, they took the wooden church apart from the inside and passed the pieces out through the windows of the new building. A church born around another church and delivered through its own windows.

Look at that tower again, because it is telling you something too. It stops square and flat and plain, with no spire, and it looks unfinished, because it is. A tall steeple was drawn for the top of it, the proper crown for a church like this, and the money simply ran out before it could be built. So the tower just stops. And here is the Boston twist. The city grew rather fond of it exactly as it is. A plain honest tower with no airs, no gilded spire reaching for heaven, suited the place, and no one ever finished it. Sometimes the version you can afford becomes the version you love.

There is a Revere here as well, because of course there is. High in that tower hangs a bell cast by Paul Revere, and it is the largest bell his foundry ever made. The first one cracked, as bells do, and Revere recast it, and it still rings today. His name is on this trail as a rider, a coppersmith, and a bell founder, and this is the biggest voice he ever gave a building.

Step inside if it is open, and notice the pews. They are box pews, little wooden enclosures with doors, and families rented them, the good ones near the front, the way you might hold a season ticket now. The Revolution changed the inside as much as the outside, because after independence this congregation broke from the Church of England for good and became the first Unitarian church in America. The building that came as the king's church ended up as one of the most freethinking congregations in the new country.

Before you leave, look at the little burying ground beside it, because it is older than the church that crowds it. This is the oldest cemetery in Boston, and the first governor of the colony is buried here, along with the first woman to step off the Mayflower onto these shores. Kings and Puritans, the crown and the graveyard, all pressed together on one corner. Take it in, then follow the brick a little further, because your next stop is not a building at all. It is a spot on the sidewalk, and you will have to look down to find it.
