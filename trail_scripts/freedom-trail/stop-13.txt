Rising above the North End rooftops is a tall white steeple, and this is the Old North Church, built in seventeen twenty three, the oldest church building still standing in Boston. Its full name is Christ Church, but nobody calls it that. Everybody knows it as the Old North, because of one line every American schoolchild learns. One if by land, two if by sea. This is the steeple those lanterns hung in.

Set the scene. It is the night of the eighteenth of April, seventeen seventy five, the same night Paul Revere is about to ride. The patriots know the British are going to move, but they do not know how, whether the soldiers will march out over land by the neck of the peninsula, or cross the river by boat. So Revere arranges a signal, a backup, in case the messengers cannot get out of the town. Lanterns in the highest steeple in Boston, visible for miles. One lantern if the British go by land. Two if they go by water. And that night the answer was two. The soldiers were crossing the Charles River by boat.

Now, who actually climbed the steeple, because it was not Revere. Revere was busy getting himself across the river to begin his ride. The man who went up into this tower was the church sexton, Robert Newman, along with a friend, and think about what that took. The town was crawling with British soldiers. To be caught hanging signal lanterns in a church steeple was to be caught committing treason, and treason got you hanged. Newman climbed up into the dark of this very steeple, held the two lanterns in the window just long enough to be seen across the water, less than a minute, because any longer risked a soldier below looking up and wondering. Then he came down and slipped out a back window of the church to get home unseen through the streets. An ordinary church caretaker, risking his neck for under a minute of lantern light. Look up into the dark where he stood.

The church itself is worth your attention beyond that one night. High in the tower hang eight bells, cast in England back in the seventeen forties, and they are among the oldest church bells in America. Here is a lovely thread. As a boy, before he was anybody, Paul Revere earned pocket money as a bell ringer right here, pulling the ropes in this tower. The man of the midnight ride learned the feel of this steeple as a child hauling on its bells. Boston is small, and the threads keep tying back on themselves.

Step inside if you can, and notice the box pews again, those little wooden pens with doors, each one rented by a family and marked with their name. The light comes in pale and the brass chandeliers hang low, and it is one of the most beautiful colonial interiors in the country, still an active church three centuries on. This steeple, by the way, has come down twice in great storms and been rebuilt each time, so what you see is faithful but not all original wood. The signal it sent, though, has never needed rebuilding.

Out front is a long open mall lined with trees, and at the far end stands a bronze statue of Paul Revere on his horse, mid stride, with the Old North steeple rising behind him. It is one of the most photographed views in Boston, and for once the postcard and the history line up, the rider and the steeple that sent the signal, in a single frame. Stand where you can see them together and the whole night of the eighteenth of April is in front of you at once, the lanterns in the tower and the man on the horse.

Here is the thing to take away. The famous line makes it sound like a grand gesture, one if by land, two if by sea, ringing down through history. But at the time it was two lanterns held in a window for under a minute by a frightened church custodian who then fled out the back. History is very often like that up close, small and quick and risky and done by people whose names we barely remember. Robert Newman, sexton. Say his name once before you go.

From here the trail climbs to a windswept graveyard on the highest ground in the neighborhood, where you can look across the water and actually see the end of your walk. The stones up there still carry the scars of the war. Follow the brick uphill.
