On the next corner stands a modest little building of brick, two stories with a steep roof and small windows, and it is easy to walk right past. Do not. This is the Old Corner Bookstore, the oldest commercial building in downtown Boston, put up in seventeen eighteen, and for a stretch of the eighteen hundreds it was very nearly the center of American literature.

Start with its age, because that alone is a small miracle. It went up in seventeen eighteen, on a lot that had been cleared by one of the fires that used to sweep through this town of wooden houses. It was built in brick precisely because brick did not burn, and it has stood through three centuries of a city that tore down almost everything else. The first business inside was an apothecary, a druggist, and for a while it was a private home. Then, in the middle of the eighteen hundreds, a publishing house moved in, and the corner found its calling.

The publisher was Ticknor and Fields, and for about thirty years this small brick building was where American writing grew up. The list of people who published here, or wandered in to argue and smoke and read proofs, is almost absurd. Nathaniel Hawthorne, whose Scarlet Letter came out of this house. Ralph Waldo Emerson. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, the most popular poet in the country. Henry David Thoreau. Oliver Wendell Holmes. Harriet Beecher Stowe, whose Uncle Tom's Cabin shook the nation over slavery. The Atlantic Monthly, a magazine still going today, was tied to this house. For a generation, if you were an American writer of any importance, your path ran through this corner. Editors sat at desks here deciding what the country would read, and the writers came to them.

Try to hold that picture against what you are looking at. This is not a grand institution. It is a small shop on a busy corner. But the work that came out of it, the novels and poems and arguments, did as much to tell Americans who they were as anything written in a statehouse. The Revolution won the country its independence. Places like this corner gave it a literature to be independent with.

Now the part that should make you grateful the building is even here, because it almost was not. By the middle of the twentieth century the old bookstore had fallen on hard times, the neighborhood had gone shabby, and the wreckers were circling. The plan for this priceless little building, the cradle of American publishing, was to knock it down and pave it into a parking lot. A parking lot. And here is where a handful of Bostonians drew a line. In nineteen sixty a small group put up their own money, bought the building outright, and stopped the demolition. And that fight did more than save one bookstore. The group they formed to do it went on to rescue building after building around this city, a good deal of what you will walk past on the rest of this trail. One stubborn stand over one small brick building started the habit of saving the others.

There is a lesson tucked in that, and it is very Boston. This city has never been sentimental about knocking things down. It has cut down hills, filled in its own harbor, run highways through its neighborhoods. The old things that survive here survived because at some point somebody fought, specifically and stubbornly, to keep them. Nothing on this trail is here by accident. Every building you see standing is a building that somebody refused to let go.

For years the shop level of this literary shrine was, of all things, a burrito restaurant, and locals will tell you about that at length, with a particular Boston mix of outrage and amusement. But look past whatever is selling something on the ground floor today and see the building itself, the brick and the roofline and the small stubborn survival of it. The tenants come and go. The point is that it is still standing to have tenants at all.

Take one more look and then move on, because the trail is about to turn serious. Everything so far has been the setting, the schools and churches and graves of a colonial town. Your next stop is the room where the talking stopped being talk. It is the meeting house where five thousand people decided they had run out of patience, and walked out the door to throw a shipload of tea into the sea. The brick continues. Follow it.
