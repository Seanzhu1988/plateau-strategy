You have walked into the North End now, the oldest neighborhood in the city, narrow streets and the smell of good food, and tucked among it is a small, dark, wooden house that looks older than everything around it, because it is. This is the Paul Revere House, built around sixteen eighty, the oldest house left standing in downtown Boston, and Paul Revere owned it and lived in it on the night of the ride that made his name. Of everything on this trail, this is the stop that feels most like stepping directly into the seventeen hundreds.

Duck your head going in, because the doorways were built for smaller people in a smaller time, and low ceilings and dark rooms are part of what the house has to tell you. It went up not long after a great fire cleared this part of town, which is why it survived when older houses did not. By the time Revere bought it, in seventeen seventy, it was already about ninety years old, a secondhand home for a busy tradesman with a growing family. That is the first thing to let go of here. Revere was not a general or a gentleman. He was a working craftsman, a silversmith, and this is a craftsman's house, cramped and practical and real.

And it was full. Revere raised an enormous family in these few small rooms, across two marriages. His first wife died, and he married again, and between the two he fathered sixteen children. Not all of them lived, and not all of them were under this roof at once, but picture even half of them here, in this handful of low dark rooms, with a workshop to run and a city coming apart at the seams outside. The tightness of the place tells you more about the man than any label on the wall. He was ordinary in the way that matters. A tradesman, a husband, a father many times over, trying to make a living, who happened to step into history one April night and do the thing that got remembered.

The ride is the reason the house is on the trail, so let us be clear about what it actually was, because the poem got a lot of it wrong. On the night of the eighteenth of April, seventeen seventy five, Revere was one of several riders sent out from Boston to warn the countryside that the British regulars were marching, and to warn two specific men, Samuel Adams and John Hancock, that the soldiers might be coming to arrest them. He crossed the river, borrowed a horse, and rode toward Lexington shouting the alarm, not, by the way, that the British are coming, since the colonists still thought of themselves as British, but that the regulars were out. He was one of many, the system of alarm was a web of riders, and he was actually captured by a British patrol before the night was over. But he got the warning through in time, and the militia was waiting on the green at Lexington at dawn, and the war began. He was the right ordinary man in the right place, and he did his part, and that is enough.

Remember too that Revere runs through this whole trail like a thread. The copper on the State House dome. The bell in King's Chapel. The engraving of the Massacre. The cornerstone of the capitol. He was a silversmith and an engraver and a bell founder and later a builder of industry, endlessly useful, endlessly busy, his hands on a dozen pieces of the story. This little house is where that busy, capable, overextended man went home at the end of the day.

And like almost everything else you have seen, the house barely survived. By the eighteen hundreds the North End had become a crowded immigrant neighborhood, and this old home was carved up into shops and cheap flats, its history nearly forgotten under the grime. It was saved at the start of the twentieth century, in part by one of Revere's own descendants, and turned into one of the very first historic house museums in the country. So you are able to duck through that low door only because someone, once again, decided an old building was worth more than the money it was sitting on.

Take your time in the small rooms. Notice how low, how dark, how close it all is. This is where a regular man lived a regular crowded life and then rode out into a story. When you step back out into the daylight of the North End, the trail carries on to a church with a famous steeple, where two lanterns hung for about a minute one night and changed everything. Follow the brick.
