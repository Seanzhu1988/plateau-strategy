You are standing where the whole thing starts, on Boston Common, and the first thing worth knowing is that this ground was public before there was a country to be public in. The town set it aside in sixteen thirty four, only four years after the first English families landed on this peninsula, which makes it the oldest public park in America. Older than the republic. Older than the idea of the republic. When you walk onto it you are walking on the first piece of land in the New World that a town decided belonged to everyone at once.

It did not begin as anything you would call a park. For two hundred years this was working ground. The city bought it from a man named William Blackstone, a solitary minister who had the whole peninsula more or less to himself and then sold most of it and moved away when the Puritans arrived and turned out to be exactly as much company as he had been avoiding. After that it was common land in the plain old sense of the word. Every household with a cow had the right to graze it here, and they did, all of them, until eighteen thirty, when the city finally decided that a field of cattle in the middle of a growing town had had its day and sent the cows home for good.

It was not only pasture. This was the town's parade ground, where the militia drilled, and it was the town's stage for its harder business too. Punishments were carried out here in full view, because the point of a punishment then was to be seen. There was a great elm near the center, long gone now, that served for years as the hanging tree. Mary Dyer, a Quaker, was hanged on this Common in sixteen sixty for coming back to a city that had banished her for her faith, and there is a statue of her seated across the street at the State House, put there much later by the same kind of city that once hanged her. Both things are true, and the Common held both.

Then came the part that puts it on this trail. In the years before the war, the British army made its camp right here. Redcoats by the thousand pitched their tents on the grass, from seventeen sixty eight until the day they left, and for a Boston that already resented them, waking up every morning to an occupying army camped on the town's own front lawn was its own daily argument. And on the night the war began, in April of seventeen seventy five, it was from the edge of this Common that the soldiers were rowed across the water to begin the march to Lexington and Concord. The men who fired the first shots of the Revolution started from the ground you are standing on.

Look up the hill from here and you will see a gold dome. That is the State House, and that is your next stop, so the walk begins by climbing. But before you go, take the measure of where you are. Fifty acres, right in the heart of the city, that no developer has ever been allowed to touch, because the same instinct that set it aside in sixteen thirty four has defended it in every century since. Abolitionists held rallies here before the Civil War. Regiments were recruited here during it. A hundred years later Martin Luther King, who had studied just up the road, stood on this Common and spoke to a crowd that filled it. When a city wants to say something to itself, out loud and all together, it still comes here to say it.

That is the thing to carry with you today. The Freedom Trail is not a museum you have entered. It is a working city that never stopped using its own landmarks. The Common is not a preserved thing behind glass. People are crossing it right now on their way to work, the way people have crossed it on their way to everything for almost four hundred years. You are joining a very long line of foot traffic.

The red brick line starts near here and runs for two and a half miles, and you do not need a map to follow it, only your eyes on the ground. Sixteen sites, the first eleven downtown, the last five across the river. The walking itself is short. It is the stopping that takes the day. So settle in, find the brick, and start up the hill toward the dome. Massachusetts built its seat of government on a cow pasture, and there is no more Boston sentence than that.
