You have stepped through the gate into the Granary Burying Ground, and this is one of the places on the trail where you should slow all the way down. It is small. You can cross it in a minute. But almost no patch of ground this size anywhere in America holds more of the Revolution than this one does.

First the name, because it is pure Boston. There is no granary here. The town granary, the building where the city stored its grain, stood right next door, about where Park Street Church is now, and the graveyard took its name from its neighbor. So this is the burying ground by the granary, shortened over time to just the Granary. It was laid out in sixteen sixty, which makes it one of the oldest cemeteries in the city, and by now something like five thousand people are buried here, though there are only a couple of thousand stones. Hold on to that gap between the bodies and the markers, because it matters in a minute.

Now the names. Look for the tall obelisk in the center. That marks the grave of Benjamin Franklin's parents, Josiah and Abiah, and it is worth knowing that Franklin himself is not here. He lived his great life in Philadelphia and is buried there. He put up a stone for his parents in Boston, the city he was born in and left as a teenager, and much later the city replaced it with this obelisk. So the biggest monument in the yard is for the mother and father of a man who made his name somewhere else.

The graves that put this place on the trail are quieter. Three of the men who signed the Declaration of Independence lie here. Samuel Adams, the great agitator, the one who kept the anger lit for years. John Hancock, whose cow pasture you just stood on at the State House, buried now a short walk from where he grazed his cattle. And Robert Treat Paine. Paul Revere is here too, under a stone much smaller than his fame, the way he seems to have wanted it. And in one plot lie the five men killed in the Boston Massacre, including Crispus Attucks, whose story waits for you further down the trail at the spot where he fell. Signers, a midnight rider, and the first men to die, all within a few steps of each other in a yard the size of a garden.

Now that gap between the bodies and the stones. If you look at the rows, they are suspiciously neat, lined up like a filing cabinet. Graves from the sixteen and seventeen hundreds were not arranged in tidy rows. What happened is that in later centuries, to make the ground easier to care for and easier to mow, the caretakers straightened the headstones into rank and file. They moved the stones. They did not move the graves. So the marker you are looking at is very likely not standing over the person whose name it carries. The stones were arranged for the convenience of a lawnmower, and the dead were left where they lay. It is a small, strange, very human thing, and once you know it you cannot unsee it.

While you are here, read the carvings, because they tell their own history if you take them in order. The oldest stones have a grim winged skull at the top, a death's head, all teeth and eye sockets, wings spread. That was the Puritan idea of death, blunt and unsoftened, a warning more than a comfort. As you move into later decades the same winged shape slowly turns into a face, a chubby cherub, the soul rising rather than the body rotting. And later still it becomes an urn and a willow, calm and classical, grief made tasteful. You can watch an entire culture change its mind about death just by walking from the older stones to the newer ones and reading the tops. The carving softens as the century turns.

That is the thing to carry out of here. This is not a solemn field of strangers. It is a neighborhood. These people knew each other, argued with each other, drank and schemed and worshiped together, and now they lie together in the middle of the city they made, with the traffic going by on the other side of the fence. Take one more look at the obelisk, at Revere's modest stone, at the neat dishonest rows, and then step back out onto the street. Your next stop is a stone chapel just down the block, and it arrived in this Puritan town about as welcome as a skunk at a wedding.
