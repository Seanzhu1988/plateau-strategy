Ahead of you now, dwarfed by the glass towers all around it, is a handsome brick building with a stepped gable and, up on the roofline, a gold lion and a white unicorn. This is the Old State House, built in seventeen thirteen, the oldest surviving public building in Boston, and for most of a century it was the headquarters of British power in New England. Everything the Revolution was against was run out of this building.

Look first at those two animals on the gable, the lion and the unicorn, because they are the whole story in miniature. The lion and the unicorn are the royal arms of Britain, the crown's own creatures. Putting them up here was the empire signing its name on the building. Inside, the royal governor's council met, the king's courts sat, and the business of governing the colony in the crown's interest got done. This was the visible seat of the authority that Boston was learning to hate.

And it was here, in this building, years before the shooting started, that one of the first great sparks flew. A lawyer named James Otis stood up in a courtroom in this building and argued for hours against the writs of assistance, the general search warrants that let royal officers enter any home or shop they pleased. He lost the case. But a young John Adams was in the room, and Adams said later that on that day, in that room, the child Independence was born. The argument that a government cannot simply search and seize as it likes was made under this roof, and it went into the bones of the country that followed.

Now step outside the building in your mind, to the street just below that balcony, because the most violent night of the pre war years happened right there, and your next stop is the exact spot. In seventeen seventy, a squad of British soldiers, taunted and pelted by an angry crowd, opened fire and killed five men in the street. The Boston Massacre happened directly beneath this building, the government's own soldiers firing on the town outside the government's own house. The two places, the balcony and the ring of stones in the street, are one story, and you are about to stand in the second half of it.

But first, the balcony itself, because it has a second scene that answers the first. On the eighteenth of July, seventeen seventy six, two weeks after it was signed in Philadelphia, the Declaration of Independence was read aloud to the people of Boston from that balcony. Imagine the crowd packed into the street below, in the very spot where their neighbors had been shot six years earlier, hearing for the first time that they were no longer subjects of a king. And when the reading was done, the crowd did not just cheer. They went up and tore the lion and the unicorn down off the building, and every other royal symbol they could reach, and they carried them into the street and burned them in a bonfire. The crown's own creatures, pulled off the crown's own house, on fire in the crowd. The lion and unicorn you see up there now are later copies, put back once the anger had cooled and the young country could look at the old symbols without wanting to set them alight. To this day the Declaration is read from that balcony every fourth of July.

There is one more thing under your feet that ties the centuries together. Directly below this building, beneath the oldest public building in Boston, runs a subway. One of the busiest stations in the city is carved into the ground right under it, and thousands of people pass under the Old State House every hour without looking up. The eighteenth century sits on top of the modern city, and the trains run on regardless. That is Boston in one image, the old thing kept and honored and completely surrounded, the past standing on a platform full of commuters.

Take a good look up at those two royal animals, knowing they were once dragged down and burned by the very people this trail is about. Then turn your attention to the street just below, to a ring of cobblestones set into a traffic island, and go carefully, because to reach your next stop you have to cross to the exact place where five men fell. Mind the traffic. The story of the balcony and the story of the stones are the same story, and you are walking from one half of it into the other.
