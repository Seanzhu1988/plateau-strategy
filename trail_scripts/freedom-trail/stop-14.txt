You have climbed to the top of the neighborhood now, to Copp's Hill Burying Ground, the second oldest cemetery in Boston, laid out in the sixteen fifties on the highest ground in the North End. It is a windy, open place of leaning slate stones, and it has the best view on this stretch of the trail. Walk to the far corner and look out across the water, because that rise on the other side, with the tall grey obelisk on top, is Charlestown, and that monument is where you are headed. You can see the end of the walk from here. Take a second to appreciate that, because you have earned the view.

But this hill is not just a lookout. Its height, the very thing that gives you the view, is exactly why it has a violent place in the story. During the Battle of Bunker Hill, in June of seventeen seventy five, the British hauled cannon up onto this burying ground and used it as a gun platform. From right here, among these graves, they bombarded Charlestown across the water and set the whole town on fire, to cover their soldiers as they crossed. So this quiet cemetery was, for one day, an artillery position, the dead used as a firing step by an army shelling a town full of the living.

There is a closer scar too, and you can still find it on the stones. British soldiers stationed up here, bored and contemptuous, used the slate headstones for target practice, and the musket ball damage is still there, little pocked craters gouged out of the old markers. There is one stone in particular, belonging to a man named Daniel Malcolm, who in life was a fierce and open patriot, and whose epitaph proudly calls him a true son of liberty and an enemy of oppression. The story goes that the redcoats singled out his stone on purpose and shot at his name, and when you look at the pitting around it, it is hard not to believe. Even the graves were part of the argument. A dead patriot's headstone became a target, which is its own kind of proof that words on a stone can still get under a tyrant's skin.

The people buried up here are a fuller picture of colonial Boston than the graves downtown. The great Puritan minister Cotton Mather and his whole formidable family of preachers lie here, the men whose sermons ruled this town in its earliest, sternest years. And so does Robert Newman, the sexton who hung the lanterns in the Old North steeple the night before this hill became a battlefield. But the most important thing about this ground is easy to miss, and the trail should not let you miss it. Many of Boston's free Black residents are buried on this hill. The North End slope below here was home to a community of free and enslaved Black Bostonians, and a great many of them were laid to rest up here, in a section that the city long overlooked. Among them, by tradition, is Prince Hall, a leader of Boston's free Black community and the founder of Black Freemasonry in America, a man who petitioned this state to end slavery and to educate Black children when almost no one in power would listen.

That is the layer this hill adds to everything you have walked past today. The Revolution you have been tracing, all those speeches about liberty and rights, was being made by a society that held people in bondage, and the people held in bondage were making their own claim on the same words, often more honestly than the men whose names are carved biggest. Prince Hall stood in the middle of that contradiction and pushed against it with everything he had. He is up here, on this windy hill, with the view of the monument.

So do two things before you leave. Find a stone with the musket damage still in it, and put your finger near one of those little craters, and remember that an army once thought a graveyard was worth shooting at. And then walk to the edge and look across the water at Charlestown one more time, at the grey needle of the monument catching the light. Everything left on this trail is over there, across the bridge. The battle that the monument marks is the reason the whole rebellion started to believe it could win. Catch your breath here in the wind, and then head down off the hill toward the water, because you are almost there.
