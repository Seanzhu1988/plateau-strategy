Ahead is a big brick hall with a white cupola and, right at the top, a golden grasshopper turning in the wind. This is Faneuil Hall, built in seventeen forty two, and it has done two jobs at once for its entire life. Downstairs it is a market, where people buy and sell food, and it always has been. Upstairs is the great meeting hall, free to enter, where the argument for the American Revolution was made out loud, over and over, until enough people believed it. They call it the Cradle of Liberty, and the cradle sits directly on top of a grocery.

Start with the grasshopper, because Bostonians love it out of all proportion to its importance. It is a weather vane, hammered out of copper and gilded, made by a local craftsman when the hall was new, and it has sat up there for nearly three centuries. It became such a fixed symbol of the city that there is a story, and it may even be true, that during times of worry it was used as a test to catch spies. If a stranger claimed to be from Boston, you asked him what sits on top of Faneuil Hall, and if he could not tell you it was a grasshopper, you knew he was lying. A city that identifies itself by a bug on a roof is a city with a sense of humor about itself.

Now the man whose name is on the building, because the trail should not hide this. Faneuil Hall was the gift of Peter Faneuil, one of the richest merchants in Boston, who paid to build it and gave it to the town. And Peter Faneuil made a large part of his fortune in the trade of enslaved human beings. That is the uncomfortable truth under the golden grasshopper. The hall that became the cradle of American liberty was paid for with money made from slavery, and in the years that followed, the very same hall became one of the great stages where abolitionists stood up and demanded that slavery end. Both of those things are true at the same time, and the honest way to walk this trail is to hold them together rather than choose one. The building is a monument to the gap between what this country said and what it did, and to the long fight to close it.

Because upstairs, in that hall, is where the talking happened. When Britain started taxing the colonies, the Sugar Act, the Stamp Act, all of it, the town gathered here to object, and it was from this room that the arguments and the resolutions came, that a colony had rights, that there should be no taxation without representation. Samuel Adams worked this room like an instrument. Town meeting after town meeting, the case was built here, in public, out loud, until an ordinary Boston shopkeeper understood exactly why he was angry and what he was owed. A revolution is not only muskets. It is first a very long argument, and a great deal of that argument was made in this hall.

And it did not stop with independence. Once the country existed, this became the room where Americans came to shout at their own country to be better. The great abolitionists spoke here, demanding an end to slavery, sometimes to hostile crowds. Later the fighters for women's right to vote stood on this floor. This hall never became a museum piece, never got roped off. It stayed a working forum, a place where whoever had something urgent to say to the public could stand up and say it, and people still hold debates and swearing in ceremonies and rallies here today. The cradle is still rocking.

So go up and stand in the hall if it is open, and look at the enormous painting on the wall and the galleries around you, and feel that it is a plain, serious, working room, not a shrine. Then come back down into the noise and the smell of the market, because that mixture is the whole point. Commerce below, democracy above, both free, both loud, both open to anyone who walks in off the street. That is about as complete a picture of what this country tried to be as you will find in one building.

From here the trail leaves the old civic center behind and heads into the North End, the oldest neighborhood in Boston, all narrow streets and good food, and your next stop is the oldest house of them all, a small dark wooden home that belonged to a silversmith you have been hearing about all day. Follow the brick, and get ready to duck your head.
