Now you are at the top of the rise, and the gold dome is right above you. This is the Massachusetts State House, and it has been the seat of the state government since seventeen ninety eight. Stand across the street and look straight up, because the dome is the thing everyone comes for and it has a longer story than its shine lets on.

Start with the ground it sits on. This was John Hancock's cow pasture. Hancock, the man with the enormous signature on the Declaration, the richest merchant in Boston, lived right next door in a fine stone house, and his family's grazing land became the site of the new capitol. There is that Boston pattern again. The most important building in the state, raised on a field where cattle used to stand. The architect was Charles Bulfinch, a young Bostonian who had toured Europe and come home full of ideas, and this was the building that made his name. Half the handsome old public buildings in New England are downstream of what he did here.

Now the dome itself. It did not always gleam. When Bulfinch finished it, the dome was covered in wooden shingles, and wooden shingles on a dome do exactly what you would expect, which is leak. So in eighteen oh two the state hired a local firm to sheathe it in copper, and the firm belonged to Paul Revere. You will meet Revere all over this trail, as a rider, as a silversmith, as a bell founder, and here he is again as the man whose company rolled the copper that first made this roof watertight. It was not gold for another lifetime. The gold leaf came in eighteen seventy four, real gold hammered thinner than paper and laid over the copper, and it has been regilded every few decades since. What you are looking at is genuinely gold, just an astonishingly small amount of it, spread very thin.

Here is the detail people love, and it is true. During the Second World War, when the whole coast was worried about being lit up for submarines and bombers offshore, the state painted the golden dome grey. A gleaming target on the highest hill in the city was the last thing anyone wanted catching the moonlight, so they dulled it for the duration and gilded it again when the war was won. It is gold once more, and on a clear morning you genuinely have to squint at it.

Walk your eye down to the cornerstone if you can find it, because the men who laid it are the men this trail is about. The cornerstone was set in seventeen ninety five, and it was hauled up the hill by fifteen white horses, one for each state in the young union, and laid in place by the governor of Massachusetts, who at that moment was Samuel Adams, with Paul Revere beside him as the head of the Masons. The agitator who helped start the Revolution had lived to become the governor dedicating the state's new house. That is the quiet reward of walking this trail in order. The same handful of names keep coming back around, at every stage of their lives, because Boston was small and these people all knew each other and kept turning up in each other's stories.

Inside, over the House of Representatives, hangs a carved wooden codfish nearly five feet long. They call it the Sacred Cod, and it is not a joke, or not only a joke. It hangs there to remind every legislator that the wealth of this state was built on fishing, on the cod pulled out of the cold Atlantic by the ton, and that the comfortable people making laws under a gold dome got here on the back of that. When the chamber moved once, they carried the cod over in a procession. Massachusetts takes its fish seriously.

Before you move on, turn around and look back the way you came, down over the Common you just crossed and out toward the harbor. This is Beacon Hill, and it was named for exactly what it sounds like. A beacon stood up here, a pole with a barrel of tar on top, ready to be set alight to warn the countryside if danger came by sea. The hill has been cut down and built over since, but the logic of the place has not changed. It was always the spot you climbed to see trouble coming and to signal the rest of the world. The state simply built its government on the lookout.

From here the trail heads back downhill and the stops come close together. Three of them almost in a row. Find the brick and keep going.
