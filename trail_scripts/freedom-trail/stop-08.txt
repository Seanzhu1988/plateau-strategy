Now you come to the building where the arguing turned into action. This is the Old South Meeting House, a tall brick church with a white steeple, put up in seventeen twenty nine, and if you stand in the middle of it and look up at the galleries you are standing in the room where the Boston Tea Party began.

First, understand what this building was for. It was built as a Puritan meeting house, a place of worship, but a meeting house in colonial Boston was more than a church. It was the largest indoor space in town, and that made it the room where the whole community gathered when something big had to be decided. When a town meeting outgrew the official halls, it came here, because this was the only room that could hold the crowd. So the biggest arguments of a hot decade happened under this roof, not because it was official, but because it was the only place big enough for everyone who wanted to shout.

Now the night itself. The sixteenth of December, seventeen seventy three. There were three ships in the harbor loaded with East India Company tea, and the tax on that tea had become the thing the whole quarrel with Britain had boiled down to. The colonists did not want the tea landed, because landing it meant paying the tax, meant accepting that Parliament could tax them as it pleased. The royal governor would not let the ships leave without unloading. It was a standoff, and it came to a head in this room. Something like five thousand people crammed inside and spilled out the doors, in a town of only sixteen thousand, so nearly a third of Boston was here or trying to be. They argued all day into the evening by candlelight, waiting for a final word from the governor. When the answer came back that the ships would not be allowed to leave, Samuel Adams rose and said that this meeting could do nothing more to save the country. It was a signal. A war whoop went up from the back, and the crowd poured out of these doors and down to the wharf, and men thinly disguised as Mohawks boarded the ships and threw three hundred and forty chests of tea into the harbor. It started right here, in this room, with those words.

The British did not forget where it started. When their army occupied Boston a couple of years later, they took a specific, spiteful revenge on this building. They came into the Old South Meeting House, ripped out the pews, the pulpit, the galleries, all the fittings of a church, hauled in loads of dirt, and turned the sanctuary into a riding ring for their cavalry. Officers exercised their horses inside the very room where the rebellion had been lit, and they knew exactly what they were doing. It was an insult aimed at the heart of the thing. Stand in here and picture that too, the horses turning in circles on the packed dirt, the deliberate desecration of the room, because it tells you how much this space frightened them.

The building survived the war and then had to survive its own city, which is often the harder fight. After a great fire tore through downtown Boston in the eighteen seventies, the meeting house was in the path of the wreckers again, its congregation gone, the land valuable. And a group of citizens, many of them women, organized to save it, one of the very first historic preservation campaigns in the whole country. They won. That is why you can walk in today. The room was saved by people who understood that some buildings are worth more than the lot they sit on.

One more thread before you go. Benjamin Franklin was baptized in this congregation as an infant, born just across the street. And Phillis Wheatley, an enslaved young woman who became the first African American to publish a book of poetry, worshiped here. The room held the whole span of the colonial town, the printer's son who became a founder and the enslaved poet whose brilliance argued against the system that held her, under one roof.

So take a slow look around. This is not a preserved decoration. It is the room where a town talked itself all the way to the edge and then walked out the door and went over it. From here the trail heads to the seat of the government they were rebelling against, and to the spot just outside it where the first blood was spilled. Follow the brick.
