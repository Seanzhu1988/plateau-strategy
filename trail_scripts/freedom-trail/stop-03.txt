Coming down off the hill, the first thing you meet is a tall white steeple on the corner of Park and Tremont, and that is Park Street Church. Pick it out from a few streets away, because for years it was the tallest thing in Boston and the first sight a traveler had of the city, riding in from the west. People arriving here saw that white spire before they saw anything else, so you can arrive at it today the way they did, letting it grow as you get closer.

The congregation built it in eighteen oh nine, and the steeple rises two hundred and seventeen feet, which does not sound like much now, standing among taller things, but for the first half of the nineteenth century this was the landmark. Ships took their bearings from it. The story goes that a famous English visitor was told to notice the church, and the corner it stands on, and here is where the corner earns its nickname, because locals call this Brimstone Corner, and there are two reasons and they fit together perfectly.

The first reason is literal. During the War of Eighteen Twelve, when the country was fighting Britain again and Boston was worried about attack, the militia needed somewhere safe to store gunpowder, and the sturdy stone crypt under this new church was exactly the cool dry vault they wanted. So for a time the basement of Park Street Church held barrels of gunpowder, which is brimstone, which gave the corner its name. The second reason is the one the churchgoers preferred. The preaching from this pulpit was fiery, full of sin and salvation and the fires below, so brimstone in the sermon as much as brimstone in the cellar. A corner named for gunpowder in the basement and hellfire from the pulpit. Boston enjoyed the double meaning too much to ever let it go.

But the reason this modest white church stands on a national trail is what got said and sung inside it. On the fourth of July, eighteen thirty one, a hymn was performed here in public for the very first time, sung by a children's choir, and the hymn was America, the one that begins, my country, tis of thee, sweet land of liberty. A young seminary student named Samuel Francis Smith had written the words earlier that year, setting them to a tune he found in a German songbook, a tune that happened to be the same one Britain used for God Save the King. So the song that a generation of American schoolchildren would sing as their own was, underneath, the British national anthem with new American words laid over it, first sung out loud in this building. That is either a wonderful accident or a very pointed joke, and either way it started here.

And it was not the only thing that started here. Two years before the hymn, on the fourth of July, eighteen twenty nine, a young man named William Lloyd Garrison stood up in this church and gave one of his first great public speeches against slavery. Garrison would go on to publish The Liberator and become the loudest, most uncompromising abolitionist voice in the country, hated for it in his own city, once dragged through the streets of Boston by a mob. But early, when almost no one wanted to hear it, he said it here. This corner has a habit of being where new American ideas get spoken before the rest of the country is ready for them.

That is really the theme of Park Street. It looks like a pretty New England church, the kind you could walk past without a second glance, and it is one of the places where the American conscience learned to raise its voice. A hymn about liberty and a speech about slavery, both from the same white building, within two years of each other, both on the fourth of July, both aimed at what the country said it believed and had not yet lived up to.

Stand a moment and take in the steeple, then look just past the church, because right beside it is a low iron fence around a small crowded yard full of old slate stones. That is your next stop, and it is one of the most extraordinary small places on the whole trail, a single graveyard holding more of the Revolution than almost anywhere else in America. So when you have had your fill of the spire, step down to the burying ground, and mind the gate.
