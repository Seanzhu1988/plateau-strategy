This is the Split Button, by Claes Oldenburg and Coosje van Bruggen, installed in nineteen eighty one, and it is sixteen feet across, made of white painted aluminium, lying on the grass in front of the library as though somebody dropped it.

Oldenburg was the great American sculptor of enormous ordinary objects: a clothespin, a shuttlecock, a spoon with a cherry on it, a giant lipstick on caterpillar tracks. He made the clothespin downtown by Philadelphia City Hall, forty five feet high, in nineteen seventy six, and this is the companion piece across the river.

Look at it properly. It is a four hole button, split down the middle, with the two halves slightly apart and tilted, resting on its edge. The holes are big enough to sit in and students do.

Now turn around and look behind you, at the standing bronze figure of Benjamin Franklin on the plinth in front of College Hall. That is by John Boyle, eighteen ninety nine, and it stood in front of the city's post office downtown before it was moved here in nineteen thirty nine.

Now look at the button again, and at Franklin, and the joke arrives. Oldenburg said the button had popped off Franklin's waistcoat and rolled down the lawn and split when it landed. That is the whole piece: a monumental public sculpture whose subject is a founding father's missing button.

There is a second reading he offered, which is that the split button is Penn's own emblem, since the university's shield has a chevron and the split suggests division and joining. Take whichever you like. The first one is better.

This is a good moment to talk about what Penn does with art, because it is unusual. In nineteen fifty nine Philadelphia became the first American city to pass a percent for art ordinance, requiring that one percent of the construction cost of a new building be spent on public art. Penn adopted the same principle on its own campus, and the result is that there is serious sculpture all over it, not gathered in a garden but standing where people walk. Within a few hundred yards of here there is work by Alexander Calder, by Tony Smith, by Robert Indiana, and more.

That family connection is worth knowing while you are in this city. Alexander Milne Calder carved the two hundred and fifty sculptures on Philadelphia City Hall and the William Penn on top of the tower. His son Alexander Stirling Calder made the Swann fountain on the Parkway. His grandson, Alexander Calder, invented the mobile. Three generations of one family, three works in a straight line along the Parkway, and pieces by the third of them on this campus.

Sit in the button for a moment if it is free; everyone does, and it is comfortable in a way a sculpture has no business being.

One more thing about the percent for art idea while you are standing next to its results. Philadelphia's ordinance of nineteen fifty nine has produced hundreds of works across the city over sixty five years, and it was copied by other cities and by the federal government. It is why this city has more public sculpture than almost any other in the country, and why a walk from the museum on the hill down to Independence Hall passes a dozen serious pieces without trying.

While you are here, look north across the Walk at the low modern building with the glass front. That is Van Pelt, the main library, from nineteen sixty two, and the six million volumes of Penn's collection are mostly behind it. It is not a beautiful building and nobody claims otherwise, but its ground floor is open and it has a reading room and a cafe, and the special collections upstairs hold Franklin's own papers, including letters in his hand.

That is the standard shape of an American university library by now: a beautiful nineteenth century one that has become something else, and a large plain twentieth century one with the books in it. You saw the same pair at Columbia an hour up the coast, and at Princeton, and at Harvard.

Then look at the building behind Franklin, the green stone one with the towers. That is where we are going next, and its stone is falling apart on purpose.
