There is a name you will see everywhere on this campus, so it is worth explaining once. Wharton is the business school, founded in eighteen eighty one by Joseph Wharton, an industrialist who made his money in nickel and iron, and it was the first of its kind anywhere: before it, business was learned by doing business. Its undergraduate programme is unusual too, because most business schools take only graduate students. Roughly a fifth of Penn's undergraduates are in it, and the effect on the culture of the place is a running joke among the other four fifths.

On your way south you cross the middle of the campus, so this is a good place to stand for a moment and talk about what Penn actually is, because it is different from the other seven on this tour.

Look around you. Within a few minutes of this spot are the Wharton School, which is the oldest collegiate business school in the world, founded in eighteen eighty one; the Perelman School of Medicine, which is the oldest medical school in the country, founded in seventeen sixty five; the first university teaching hospital in America; a law school from eighteen fifty; a school of engineering that built the first electronic computer; a school of nursing; a school of veterinary medicine; a school of dental medicine; and a design school. All of them on one continuous campus, all within a fifteen minute walk.

That is Franklin's idea, carried out. He argued in seventeen forty nine for a school that would teach useful things to people who were going into the world rather than into the church, and Penn is the American university that took that furthest. It has always been professional, practical, and slightly commercial, and Penn people say so with pride while people at the other seven say it with a raised eyebrow.

There is a phrase for it here: the One University policy, adopted in the nineteen seventies, which means that a student in any school may take courses in any other. An undergraduate in the college can take a Wharton course. A nursing student can study in the design school. It sounds administrative and it changes what the place feels like.

You will also notice, walking south, that the campus does not stop. Penn owns and operates a very large amount of West Philadelphia, and the boundary between university and city is deliberately soft: shops, restaurants, a cinema, apartment buildings. In the nineteen nineties Penn made a decision, under its president Judith Rodin, to invest heavily in the surrounding neighbourhood rather than wall itself off, funding a public school, improving streets and lighting, and buying and renovating housing. It is studied in urban planning courses as an example of a university doing this well, and it is also, inescapably, a university with enormous resources shaping the neighbourhood around it, which is what the nineteen sixties clearances did in a cruder way.

Keep walking south and east. You will cross a wide street, and ahead of you will be a long low building of brick with a tiled roof, an ornamental gate, and a garden in front of it with a pool.

One more number, because it explains the feel of the place. Penn has around twenty two thousand students in total and about four thousand faculty, and the university and its health system together employ more people than any other private employer in Philadelphia. A university that is also the largest employer in its city is a particular kind of institution, and it is why the relationship with the neighbourhood is never simple and never only about education.

Notice as you walk that the buildings get newer and taller toward the south and east. That is the medical and research campus, and it is where most of Penn's money and most of its growth are.

One more thing on the medical side, since it is a few minutes from here and it is the oldest of its kind. The Perelman School of Medicine was founded in seventeen sixty five by John Morgan and William Shippen, who had trained in Edinburgh, and it was the first medical school in the thirteen colonies. The Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania followed in eighteen seventy four as the first teaching hospital built by an American university, meaning a hospital that existed to train doctors as well as treat patients.

Between them these institutions changed how medicine was taught in this country, from apprenticeship to a formal course with a hospital attached, which is the model everywhere now.
