This is College Hall, from eighteen seventy three, and it is the oldest building on this campus and the first one Penn built here after it moved across the river from downtown.

The colour is the thing. It is built of serpentine stone, a green rock quarried in Chester County, and there is nothing else quite like it in American university building. In the eighteen seventies serpentine was fashionable in Philadelphia and it is beautiful, a soft grey green that changes with the light, and it is also a disaster as a building material. Serpentine holds water, and when the water freezes it shatters the surface, so the stone spalls, flakes and crumbles. Penn has been repairing this building continuously for a hundred and fifty years, replacing stone piece by piece.

The architect was Thomas Webb Richards, and the style is Collegiate Gothic in an early, Victorian form: towers, pointed windows, and originally two tall spires that were removed in nineteen fourteen when they became unsafe. So the building you see is lower than the one designed.

There is a piece of popular lore attached to it that Penn neither confirms nor kills. Charles Addams, the cartoonist who created the Addams Family, was a Pennsylvanian and studied here briefly, and it is widely said that this building, with its towers and its gloom, was a model for the family's house. Addams drew his house from several sources and never settled the question. The university is happy to let people believe it.

Inside is the office of the president and the provost, and a good deal of the College of Arts and Sciences, so it is not open to visitors.

Now, the thing that happened in the basement of the building next door and in this one, because Penn's biggest claim on the twentieth century is a machine.

In the nineteen forties, in the Moore School of Electrical Engineering, a few blocks south of here, John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert built ENIAC, the Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer, for the United States Army, to compute artillery firing tables. It was switched on in nineteen forty five and unveiled in nineteen forty six. It filled a room, weighed thirty tons, and had around eighteen thousand vacuum tubes. It was the first general purpose electronic computer, and it was programmed by six women, Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas and Ruth Lichterman, who worked out how to make it do things by physically rewiring it, and who were left out of the story for decades. Parts of ENIAC are still on this campus, in the Moore Building, and can sometimes be seen.

Look back at the green stone once more before you walk on, and notice how much of it has been replaced: you can see the newer pieces because they are a different shade. A building that has been mended for a hundred and fifty years is a fair emblem for a university.

It is worth saying what Penn was before it came here. The university spent its first century downtown, in the old city, on Ninth Street, near where the historic district is now, and it moved out to West Philadelphia in eighteen seventy two because it had run out of room and land here was cheap and open. That move, across the Schuylkill, is why Penn has a campus and the older colleges downtown do not. Every American university that moved out of a city centre in the nineteenth century got a campus out of it; the ones that stayed got a set of buildings on streets.

Look at the front of College Hall once more and count the storeys, then look at the towers. The building originally had two tall spires that made it considerably more dramatic than what stands now, and photographs from the eighteen eighties show a silhouette much closer to a cathedral. They came down in nineteen fourteen because the serpentine could not carry them safely. The building has been quietly getting shorter and plainer for a century, which is what happens to a fashionable stone that does not work.

The lawn in front of it, with Franklin on his plinth, is called College Green, and it is the one proper lawn on this campus and the place where commencement happens.

Walk east along the Walk, and the next building on your right, red sandstone with a great chimney of a tower, is by a Philadelphia architect who was hated in his own time.
