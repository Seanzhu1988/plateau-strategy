Start here, at Thirty Seventh Street and Locust Walk, and sit down on the bench, because there is room and that is the point of it.

The bronze man reading a newspaper beside you is Benjamin Franklin, by George Lundeen, put here in nineteen eighty seven. He is at ease, legs crossed, paper open, and he has left space on the bench for you. It is the most photographed spot at the University of Pennsylvania and there is usually a small queue, so take your turn.

Look at what he is reading. It is the Pennsylvania Gazette, the newspaper Franklin bought in seventeen twenty nine when he was twenty three and turned into the best paper in the colonies, and the sculptor has cut the type so that you can read the front page over his shoulder. Franklin made his money in print, and everything else in his life, the science, the diplomacy, the founding of this university, was paid for by a printing business he built himself after arriving in this city at seventeen with almost nothing.

Now, the university. Penn dates itself to seventeen forty, which is the earliest of these Ivy claims and also the most argued about, and the argument is worth two minutes because it tells you how these institutions think.

In seventeen forty a group of Philadelphians raised money for a building on Fourth Street intended as a charity school and a preaching hall for the evangelist George Whitefield. The building went up and the school did not really happen. In seventeen forty nine Franklin published a pamphlet, Proposals Relating to the Education of Youth in Pensilvania, arguing for a new kind of school: not a seminary for training ministers, which is what Harvard, Yale and Princeton were, but an academy teaching English, mathematics, history, science and practical skills to boys who would go into business and public life. He gathered trustees, they bought the unused building from seventeen forty, and the Academy of Philadelphia opened in seventeen fifty one.

For a long time Penn dated itself to seventeen forty nine, then seventeen fifty one. In eighteen ninety nine the trustees voted to move the founding date back to seventeen forty, on the strength of that first building, which conveniently made Penn older than Princeton. Historians have raised eyebrows ever since. Penn's better claim is not age but novelty: it was the first American institution to call itself a university, in seventeen seventy nine, and Franklin's idea of a school teaching useful things to people who were not going to be clergymen was genuinely new in the English speaking world.

So the man on the bench is not a symbolic founder in the way John Harvard is. Franklin wrote the plan, raised the money, chaired the trustees, and argued with the headmaster about the curriculum for years. This is his school in a way none of the others are anyone's.

One practical note before we walk. Penn's campus is in West Philadelphia, it is open, and the spine of it is the brick path you are standing on, Locust Walk. Everything on this tour is on or within a minute of that path. It is about a mile from end to end.

One more thing about the bench, since you are on it. Lundeen made several of these seated Franklins and there are versions in other cities, but this one is at the university he founded, which is the right place for it. Notice that he is not on a plinth and not looking into the distance. He is at your height, doing something ordinary, which for a founding father is unusual and was deliberate. Franklin spent his life insisting he was a printer, and his own epitaph, written when he was young, describes his body as the cover of an old book with the contents torn out.

Two more things about Franklin and this university before you get up. He wanted the school to teach in English rather than Latin, which sounds trivial and was radical: the ancient languages were the whole of a gentleman's education, and Franklin thought a boy who was going to run a business or a farm needed arithmetic, accounts, history and natural philosophy more than he needed Cicero. He lost that argument to the classicists on his own board of trustees and complained about it for the rest of his life, in writing, at length.

And he never went to any university himself. He had two years of school, total, ending at ten, and he taught himself by reading and by argument in a club he founded called the Junto. The man who founded this place was almost entirely self educated, which is worth sitting with on his bench.

Now stand up, and look east down the Walk. That is where we are going.
