This is Houston Hall, opened in eighteen ninety six, and it is the first student union building in the world.

That is a real claim and it holds up. Before this, American colleges had chapels, libraries, classrooms and dormitories, and nowhere for students to simply be. The idea of a building given over to undergraduate life, with common rooms, a dining hall, games rooms, meeting spaces and a place to sit, was invented here, and every student union on every campus you have visited is descended from it.

It was paid for by Henry Howard Houston, a railway executive, in memory of his son Henry Junior, who died of typhoid at twenty three while travelling in Italy shortly after graduating from Penn. The building was designed after a competition among Penn architecture students, which is a nice detail: the first student union was designed by students. William Kelsey and Milton Bennett Medary won it.

Go in. It is open to the public and there is food in it.

The ground floor has the Hall of Flags and a great room with a fireplace, panelled, with a beamed ceiling, which was the original common room and still functions as one. Look at the woodwork and the fireplaces. In eighteen ninety six this was a startling amount of domestic comfort to hand to undergraduates, and it was meant as a statement: that the university had a duty to their life outside the classroom.

The building has been extended and remodelled repeatedly, and it now forms part of the Perelman Quadrangle with Irvine Auditorium and two other buildings around a court.

There is a piece of Penn history attached to this spot that is not architectural. In the nineteen sixties and seventies this was where the arguments happened, as Hamilton Hall was at Columbia and Massachusetts Hall at Harvard. In February nineteen sixty nine, students occupied College Hall for six days over Penn's plans to expand into West Philadelphia and its research contracts, and the negotiations that ended it produced a commitment from the university about community housing. Every campus on this tour has this story, in the same years, with the same shape.

Now, a practical suggestion, because you are about halfway along this walk and standing in a building with a food court in it. This is the best place on the route to sit down for twenty minutes. There is nothing else on Locust Walk that will let you.

There is one more thing worth knowing about the union idea, because it did not come from nowhere. Penn students had been agitating for a building of their own for years, and the model in the founders' minds was partly the debating unions at Oxford and Cambridge and partly the settlement houses and clubs of the eighteen nineties, which were then a new idea about how buildings could shape social life. Houston Hall took an idea about improving the poor and applied it to undergraduates, which is a very Victorian move and, judging by the last hundred and thirty years, a durable one.

Look at the plaque about Henry Junior if you can find it. The building exists because a young man died of typhoid on holiday, which is how a surprising number of these buildings exist.

There is a piece of Penn student vocabulary worth knowing while you are standing in the middle of it. Undergraduates here talk about the Quad, which is the Quadrangle, a very large group of Collegiate Gothic dormitories a few minutes west of this spot, built between eighteen ninety five and nineteen twenty nine, where most first year students live. It is one of the largest collegiate Gothic complexes in the country, arranged around a series of connected courtyards, with carved grotesques on the gates that repay a look: musicians, athletes, scholars, and a few jokes.

If you have twenty spare minutes at the end of this walk, the Quad is the thing on this campus most people miss, and the gates are usually open in daytime.

When you come out, look across the court at the building with the tall Gothic roof and the strange silhouette. That is Irvine Auditorium, and there is a story about it that Penn students have told for ninety years.
