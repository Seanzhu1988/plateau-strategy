This is Franklin Field, and it is the oldest stadium still in use for football in the country, and rather more has happened here than a university normally gets.

The site has been Penn's athletic ground since eighteen ninety five. The horseshoe of brick and concrete you are looking at was built in nineteen twenty two, with the upper deck added in nineteen twenty five, and it holds about fifty two thousand people.

Here is the list, and it is the reason to end a walk here.

The first scoreboard in American sport was put up at this ground. The first radio broadcast of a football game came from here, in nineteen twenty two. The first television broadcast of a football game, in nineteen thirty nine. It was the first stadium with two tiers of seating. The Penn Relays, the oldest and largest track and field competition in the country, have been run here every April since eighteen ninety five, and they bring around a hundred thousand people over three days.

The Philadelphia Eagles played here from nineteen fifty eight to nineteen seventy, and in nineteen sixty they won the National Football League championship on this field, beating Vince Lombardi's Green Bay Packers, which remains the only postseason game Lombardi ever lost. Philadelphia did not win another until two thousand and eighteen.

And in nineteen forty eight the Republican, Democratic and Progressive parties all held their national conventions in this city, and events connected to them used the stadium, in the first conventions ever televised.

Now the thing that Penn students will tell you about, which is the toast. At the end of the third quarter of a home football game, the crowd sings a song called Drink a Highball, and on the last line, here's a toast to dear old Penn, everybody throws toast onto the field. Thousands of slices. There is a machine, a modified Zamboni, nicknamed the Toast Zamboni, which comes out and sweeps it up. The tradition is said to have started in the nineteen seventies, when the university banned alcohol at games and students, unable to drink a toast, threw one instead.

That is a fair place to end a walk round Penn, because it is exactly the character of the institution: practical, slightly commercial, sceptical of ceremony, and willing to make a joke out of a rule.

Turn round and look back the way you came. In a mile you have gone from a bronze Franklin reading his own newspaper on a bench, along a street that was closed to cars and turned into the spine of a campus, past a giant button that popped off his waistcoat, a green stone building that has been crumbling for a hundred and fifty years, the finest Victorian library in America, the first student union ever built, a concert hall designed by a man whose name was left off it, a museum with a twelve ton sphinx in the hall, and a stadium where they throw bread.

One more thing about the Penn Relays before you go, because it is the largest event this campus holds and most visitors have never heard of it. It runs over three days in late April, it has been going since eighteen ninety five, and it brings together high school, college and professional athletes from around the world, with tens of thousands of competitors. The Jamaican teams have a following here that fills the stands. If you are in Philadelphia on the last weekend of April, this stadium is the place to be, and tickets are cheap.

One more thing about the field itself before you go. Franklin Field's track is where the modern American track and field calendar effectively begins each spring, and the surface has been rebuilt many times, but the horseshoe of brick around it is the nineteen twenties structure, unreconstructed. Stand at the open end and look down the length of it. Very few American stadiums of that age survive at all; almost all of them were replaced in the sixties and seventies by concrete bowls out by a motorway, and then those were replaced in turn.

This one survived because a university needed a stadium the same size it had always needed, which is the great advantage of not being a professional franchise.
