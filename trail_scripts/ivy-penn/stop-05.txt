This is the Fisher Fine Arts Library, finished in eighteen ninety one, by Frank Furness, and it is the best building on this campus and one of the best in the country.

Look at the outside first. Red sandstone and brick and terracotta, in a style nobody else was working in: pointed and Gothic in outline but industrial in feeling, with a great blunt tower like a factory chimney, a huge arched entrance, and a gable end with an enormous window. It is heavy, it is asymmetrical, and it does not look like a library. It looks like a machine for holding books, which is what Furness meant.

Frank Furness was a Philadelphian, a Union cavalry officer who won the Medal of Honor at the Battle of Trevilian Station, and he built about six hundred buildings in and around this city, most of them railway stations and banks. He was the most original American architect of his generation and by nineteen hundred his style was despised: too rough, too loud, too Victorian. Most of his work was demolished. Louis Sullivan, who trained in his office and went on to build the first skyscrapers in Chicago, said Furness made buildings out of the fullness of his imagination, and Sullivan's pupil was Frank Lloyd Wright, so the line runs from this man to the twentieth century.

Now go in, and it is open to visitors during the day, and stand in the reading room.

It is a great hall four storeys high with a skylight, ringed by galleries, with terracotta ribs rising and meeting overhead like the vault of a church built by an engineer. The colour is red and warm. The detail is everywhere: leaf carving in the terracotta, iron work, tiles.

Then look at the back of the building, from outside if you can. Furness put the book stacks in a separate structure behind the reading room, a tall block of iron and glass, and he designed it to be extended: the intention was that as the collection grew, the stack could simply be lengthened backwards. That is a nineteenth century architect thinking about growth, which almost none of them did, and it is why architects come here.

The building was nearly lost. By the nineteen fifties Furness was out of fashion and the university proposed demolishing it and putting up something modern. It survived, was restored in the nineteen nineties, and is now a National Historic Landmark. Louis Kahn, who taught at Penn for twenty years and whose two Yale buildings are on this tour, loved it and defended it, and you can see why: the honesty about structure, the willingness to let a building show what it is made of, runs straight from Furness to Kahn.

It is now the fine arts library, so it is full of architecture students, and it is a working reading room. Visitors are welcome to look. Speak quietly.

There is one more thing to look at inside, and it is easy to miss. Furness designed the reading room so that the readers sit under a skylight and the books sit behind them in the stack, which reverses the usual arrangement of a nineteenth century library and puts the people in the best light rather than the collection. It is a small decision and it tells you what he thought a library was for.

Look also at the terracotta detailing around the great fireplace and the stair. Furness had it made specially, and the leaf forms are unlike anybody else's; they are blunt and fleshy and slightly ugly, and they are unmistakably his.

One more thing about Furness's reputation, because it is a lesson that applies to half the buildings on this tour. In nineteen fifty eight, when this library was under threat, an architectural historian could still write that Furness's work was an embarrassment to Philadelphia. Twenty years later it was being called the greatest architecture the city produced. Nothing about the buildings changed. The people looking at them did, and roughly two thirds of Furness's six hundred buildings were demolished during the decades when the opinion was low.

If there is a single practical lesson in walking eight old campuses, it is that the buildings people want to knock down are usually the ones the next generation crosses an ocean to see.

From here, walk a little further east along the Walk to the large building on the corner with the arched entrance.
