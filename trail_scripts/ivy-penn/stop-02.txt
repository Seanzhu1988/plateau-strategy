This is Locust Walk, and it is the spine of the university: a brick path running east and west through the middle of the campus, with the older buildings along it, and every student at Penn walks it several times a day.

Here is the thing to know about it, and you can see the evidence under your feet. This was a street. Locust Street ran straight through here with cars on it, and the university sat on both sides of a public road, cut in half. In the nineteen sixties Penn persuaded the city to close it, and the paving was taken up and replaced with brick, and the trees were planted, and the road became a walk. That single decision, more than any building, made Penn a campus instead of a set of buildings in a city.

It was part of a much larger and more contested programme. In the nineteen fifties and sixties Penn expanded enormously, and it did so by acquiring and clearing blocks of West Philadelphia, including a neighbourhood called Black Bottom, a predominantly Black community whose residents were displaced by urban renewal in which the university was a participant. Thousands of people lost their homes. The university has, in recent decades, acknowledged this directly; there is a substantial body of work by Penn's own historians on it, and former residents hold reunions. When you walk a pleasant brick path through a beautiful campus in an American city, it is worth knowing what was on the ground before.

Now look around at what lines the Walk. Some of these houses are genuine nineteenth century Philadelphia villas that were here before the campus swallowed the street, and they are now fraternity houses and university offices. Others are purpose built. The mix is why Locust Walk feels like a street rather than a quadrangle: the buildings are different sizes and set at different distances, because they were never planned together.

Look up at the trees. They are mostly London plane and oak, and they were planted when the street was closed, so they are about sixty years old, which is why they arch over the path now.

And look at the people. This walk is the social life of Penn: club tables, flyers, a cappella groups, protests, people selling things for charity. At class change it is genuinely crowded. Penn has about ten thousand undergraduates and twelve thousand graduate students, and most of them pass along here.

There is one more thing to notice as you go east. The Walk crosses Thirty Sixth Street on a bridge, over the traffic below, which is the most obvious sign that this was carved out of a city street grid. Stand on the bridge and look down at the cars, and then look back along the Walk, and the whole idea of the campus is visible in one turn of the head.

While you are on the Walk, look for the plaques and the benches with names on them, and at the flagstones. Penn has a tradition of marking the Walk with class gifts, so the path is a record of graduating years going back decades. Universities are unusual among institutions in that they ask everyone who passes through to pay for a piece of the ground, and then write their name on it.

And notice the width. The Walk is about the width of the street it replaced, which is why it can hold a crowd. Campus paths built as paths are almost always too narrow.

Look at the buildings on the north side of the Walk as you go, the older brick and stone houses. Several of these are fraternity and sorority houses, and Greek letter organisations are a much larger part of student life here than at Harvard or Princeton, where they either do not exist or are called something else. Penn has a few dozen of them, and the ones on Locust Walk are the visible face of a system that shapes a great deal of the social calendar.

And notice the bicycles. Penn is flatter and more spread out than the New England campuses, and it is a genuinely bicycle scaled place in a way that Harvard Yard, which you can cross in six minutes, never needed to be.

Keep walking east. In two minutes, on your left, you will see a large aluminium button, split down the middle, lying on the grass.
