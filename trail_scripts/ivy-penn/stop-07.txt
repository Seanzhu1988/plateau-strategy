This is Irvine Auditorium, finished in nineteen twenty nine, and there is a legend about it that every Penn student is told and that is not quite true, which is the best kind of legend.

Look at it first. It is Gothic, in grey stone, with a steep roof, pinnacles, and a silhouette that does not resolve into anything obvious: it is not a church, not a hall, not a tower, but bits of all three piled together. The architect was Horace Trumbauer, who was one of the great society architects of the age and who built mansions for the very rich, the Philadelphia Museum of Art, and Widener Library at Harvard, the one you saw with the columns and the steps.

The legend is that Trumbauer's design was a student project that had received a failing grade, or that it was drawn by a student, or that the whole thing was a joke played on the university. It is repeated constantly.

The truth is more interesting. The chief designer in Trumbauer's office was Julian Abele, the first Black graduate of Penn's architecture school, class of nineteen oh two, and one of the most accomplished American architects of his generation. He designed, in Trumbauer's name, the Philadelphia Museum of Art, Harvard's Widener Library, and most of the campus of Duke University. Because of the racial conventions of the time his name did not appear on the drawings, and Duke did not publicly credit him until the nineteen eighties. Irvine came out of that office in the years when Abele was its chief designer.

So a building at the university where he was the first Black graduate, in the office where he was the designer, is remembered by students as a joke drawing that failed a class. Penn has since named a room in Fisher Fine Arts after him and there is a portrait of him on this campus.

Now what it is for. Inside is a concert hall seating around thirteen hundred, and in it is the Curtis Organ, which is one of the largest organs in the world: it was built for the Sesquicentennial Exposition in Philadelphia in nineteen twenty six, an enormous instrument with over ten thousand pipes, and Cyrus Curtis, the publisher of the Saturday Evening Post, bought it and gave it to Penn. The auditorium was designed around it.

The organ is maintained by a volunteer society and there are free recitals during term. If you can catch one, do, because an instrument like this in a room built for it is a rare thing and the sound is physical.

The interior was completely renovated in the late nineteen nineties, when the acoustics, which had always been poor, were rebuilt.

Look up at the roofline once more as you go. The building is a fifteenth century French silhouette with a nineteen twenties American concert hall and an exposition organ inside it, designed by a man whose name was not on it.

One more note on the legend, because it is worth being precise about why it survives. Buildings that look strange attract stories, and a Gothic pile with a lumpy silhouette in the middle of a campus is going to collect one. What makes this case sad rather than funny is that the real explanation, a brilliant designer whose name was kept off his own work because he was Black, was available the whole time and less interesting to students than a tale about a failing grade.

Abele designed the Philadelphia Museum of Art, which you can see from this campus on a clear day, and Harvard's Widener Library, which is on this tour. He is on three of these walks and his name is on none of the buildings.

There is one more thing to know about the organ, because it very nearly went. Instruments of this size are enormously expensive to maintain, and the Curtis Organ sat unplayable for long stretches of the twentieth century while the auditorium's acoustics were bad and nobody wanted to spend the money. It was saved by a volunteer society of enthusiasts who raised funds and did the work, which is how most great organs in this country have survived. When you hear one, you are usually hearing the result of somebody's forty year hobby.
