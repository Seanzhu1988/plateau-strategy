This is the Penn Museum, properly the University of Pennsylvania Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology, founded in eighteen eighty seven, and it holds around a million objects.

The building opened in eighteen ninety nine and it is worth a look before you go in: brick and tile in a free Romanesque manner, by Wilson Eyre and others, with a walled garden and a reflecting pool in front and a rotunda behind. It is domestic in scale at the front and enormous behind, which is the shape of a museum that kept growing.

Go in, and go straight to the sphinx.

The Sphinx of Ramses the Second sits in the main entrance hall. It is red granite, about twelve and a half tons, carved around three thousand two hundred years ago, and it is the largest sphinx in the western hemisphere. It came from the temple of Ptah at Memphis in Egypt, excavated by the British archaeologist Flinders Petrie, and Penn acquired it in nineteen thirteen. When it was found it was buried to the shoulders in sand, which is why the head is weathered and eroded and the lower body is comparatively crisp: the part that was above ground took three thousand years of wind and the part below did not.

For most of a century it stood in a lower gallery. In twenty nineteen the museum moved it, which took a specialist team and a great deal of engineering, up into the main hall, so it is now the first thing you meet.

Then the rest. The strengths of this museum are extraordinary for a university and they come from the fact that Penn ran its own excavations for over a century. The Royal Tombs of Ur, in what is now Iraq, were excavated in the nineteen twenties and thirties by Leonard Woolley in a joint expedition with the British Museum, and Penn's share includes the great gold and lapis headdress of Queen Puabi, the Ram in the Thicket, and the Great Lyre with the bull's head. These are among the most important objects to survive from Mesopotamia.

There is a Chinese rotunda under a nineteen twenties dome, with monumental Buddhist sculpture and a crystal ball that belonged to the Dowager Empress. There are galleries of Africa, the Americas, the Mediterranean and Asia.

And there is a serious, visible reckoning going on here about how the objects came, which the museum does not hide. The Penn Museum has been among the more active American institutions in repatriation, returning human remains and objects, and it has publicly apologised for its historical holdings of skulls collected by Samuel Morton in the nineteenth century for the purposes of racial pseudoscience, and for its handling of remains from the nineteen eighty five MOVE bombing in this city. There are gallery texts about all of it. If you want to see a museum thinking out loud about its own history, this is one.

There is an admission charge, free for Penn people, and it is closed on Mondays. Ninety minutes is the minimum.

One practical tip about the sphinx, since people miss the best view. Walk round behind it. The back of the piece carries hieroglyphic inscriptions naming Ramses the Second and, cut later, two of his successors, which is an ancient habit: a new pharaoh would take an existing monument and add his own name to it. You are looking at three kings claiming the same lion.

If you have children with you, the Egyptian galleries and the mummies are the draw, and the museum knows it; there is a good deal of material aimed at them.

Two more objects worth finding if you have the time. There is a colossal granite architrave from the palace of Merenptah, and there are the Ur objects in a gallery of their own, and if the Middle East galleries are open you should give them twenty minutes on their own: the material from Ur is what most people have seen in photographs without knowing where it lives.

The museum also runs one of the largest archaeological conservation laboratories in the country, and parts of it are visible to visitors, which is the same idea as the glass walled labs at Harvard's art museums. Universities have worked out that the most interesting thing in a museum is often the person mending something.

When you come out, walk east a few minutes toward the large brick horseshoe of a stadium.
