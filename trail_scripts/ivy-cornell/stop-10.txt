You have walked north from the museum, past the buildings on the northern edge of the campus, and the ground has been falling away on your right the whole time. That drop is the gorge, and this is where you cross it.

This is the Fall Creek suspension bridge, and you should walk out to the middle of it and stop.

It is a footbridge, built in nineteen sixty by the university's own engineers, replacing an earlier one, and it hangs across Fall Creek gorge, about a hundred and forty feet above the water, connecting the main campus to North Campus where most first year students live. It moves a little when people walk on it, which is what a suspension bridge does and which alarms visitors.

Look down. Below you the creek runs through a rock gorge with sheer sides, and upstream is Beebe Lake and Triphammer Falls, and downstream the water falls away toward Ithaca Falls and the town. This is inside the campus. Students cross it four times a day on the way to lectures.

Now look at the geology, because it is genuinely remarkable and it is the reason for all of this. These gorges were cut through shale and sandstone laid down in the Devonian period, roughly four hundred million years ago, when this was the bed of a shallow sea. The rock is full of marine fossils. The gorges themselves are young: they were cut in the last twelve thousand years, since the glaciers retreated, as meltwater tore down the hillsides toward the new lake. Cayuga Lake is a finger lake, gouged by ice, and it is deep enough that its floor is below sea level.

So a university sits on a hill between two chasms that were cut by a melting ice sheet through the floor of a Devonian ocean, and its students walk across them on a swaying bridge to get to breakfast.

There is a hard thing to say about these gorges and this university says it, so I will. The bridges here have been sites of suicide, and after a cluster of deaths in two thousand and ten Cornell put barriers and nets on the gorge bridges, which was controversial because they alter the experience of the crossing, and which the university judged worth it. The means restriction has been studied and it works. If you are struggling, the university and the town both have services, and so does anyone reading this: there is help.

One more thing about the bridges, because there are several and they are part of how this campus works. There are footbridges over both gorges, and they are the only ways across, so a student living on North Campus crosses a chasm to get to a lecture and crosses back for dinner. In an ordinary university a path is a path. Here the geography imposes a route, and everybody uses the same few crossings, which is why you meet everyone you know on them.

One more thing about the engineering, since this is a university that teaches it. The bridge is a true suspension bridge in miniature: two towers, two main cables, hangers dropping to a stiffened deck. The reason it moves under your feet is that a light deck on flexible cables has very little mass to resist you, and the same physics on a larger scale is why the Tacoma Narrows bridge tore itself apart in nineteen forty in a wind, which every engineering undergraduate in the country is shown film of in their first year.

Cornell's engineers built this one, and generations of their students have crossed it on the way to the lecture where they learn why it wobbles.

One more thing about what the gorges did to this university's engineering department, which is a nice piece of local history. Cornell's early civil engineering programme used the gorges as a laboratory: students surveyed them, measured the flows, and designed structures for them, and the hydraulic laboratory on Fall Creek used the fall of the water for real experiments. A university that happened to own two chasms and a set of waterfalls taught its engineers with them, which is exactly the practical instinct the place was founded on.

The dam upstream at Beebe Lake was built for water power, and Cornell generated its own electricity from the creek for years.

Cross to the far side and look back at the bridge and the gorge together. Then come back, because the last stop is upstream.
