Before the building, a note about the man it is named for, because he is a complicated figure to have a humanities building named after. Goldwin Smith was a distinguished historian and a genuine believer in Cornell's founding principles, and he was also, in his later writing, an outspoken antisemite. Cornell has discussed this publicly and the name remains. Every one of these eight campuses has at least one building in this position, and how each institution has handled it is worth noticing as you walk.

This is Goldwin Smith Hall, from nineteen oh six, and it is the humanities building and the largest thing on the Arts Quad.

It is Beaux Arts classical, in grey limestone, with a portico of Ionic columns, and it was designed by Carrere and Hastings, the firm that built the New York Public Library and Woolsey Hall at Yale. It is named for Goldwin Smith, an English historian who had been Regius Professor at Oxford and who came to Cornell in eighteen sixty eight as one of its first professors, drawn by White's promise of a university with no religious test.

Inside are the departments of literature, classics, history and philosophy, along with lecture halls and the Temple of Zeus, which is a cafe.

The cafe is worth explaining because the name is a joke that outlived its own premise. When the building opened, one of its halls held the university's collection of plaster casts of classical sculpture, which every serious university owned in the nineteenth century: full size copies of the Laocoon, the Venus de Milo, the Elgin marbles, made from moulds and used for teaching, because no American student was going to see the originals. Students called the room the Temple of Zeus.

Then in the twentieth century plaster casts went entirely out of fashion. Universities everywhere threw them out, broke them up or put them in basements, and Cornell was no exception. The casts went. The name stayed, and it is now attached to a cafe that serves soup and where people argue about poetry, which is arguably closer to a temple of Zeus than a room of plaster ever was.

Go in and have a coffee. It is open to anyone.

Now, while you are in the humanities building, the thing about Cornell's teaching that follows from the founding idea.

Cornell has fourteen colleges and schools. Four of them are contract colleges, supported by New York State: Agriculture and Life Sciences, Human Ecology, Industrial and Labor Relations, and the veterinary college. The rest are private. A New York resident in a contract college pays much less than a private student in the same university, sitting in the same lectures.

And the range is genuinely unlike anywhere else on this tour. Cornell teaches classics and it teaches dairy science. It has a world leading hotel school, founded in nineteen twenty two, the first anywhere, which runs an actual hotel on campus. It has the largest agriculture college in the Ivy League and a plant breeding programme that has released varieties you have eaten. It has a veterinary school. It teaches Sanskrit and it teaches viticulture, and its founder would have regarded that as the entire point.

One more thing about the casts, since they have vanished from every campus. In the nineteenth century a plaster cast collection was standard equipment for teaching art history, and the great museums had halls of them: the Metropolitan Museum in New York had thousands. Then modernism arrived, originality became the value, and copies became embarrassing. Most collections were destroyed outright in the nineteen forties and fifties. Scholars now regret it, because the casts were made from originals that have since weathered, been damaged or been destroyed, and in some cases the plaster copy is a better record of the surface than the marble is today.

One more thing about the hotel school, since it is the most Cornell institution in the university. It was founded in nineteen twenty two, the first of its kind anywhere in the world, at a moment when the idea that hotel keeping was a subject fit for a university was faintly ridiculous. It runs the Statler Hotel on this campus, a real working hotel where students do the jobs, and you can book a room in it. Its graduates run a large share of the world's hospitality industry.

That is Ezra Cornell's any study, taken literally by a university that has never been embarrassed about the practical.

From here, walk south east, out of the quad and down toward the chapel with the mosaics.
