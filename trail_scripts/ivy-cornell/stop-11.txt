Before you walk on, look back at the bridge you have just crossed, from the bank, so that you see it in profile against the gorge. That is the photograph, and almost everyone takes theirs from the middle of the deck instead, where you cannot see the bridge at all.

This last stop is the gorge itself, and it is the answer to why anybody puts up with an Ithaca winter.

From the suspension bridge, walk east and upstream along the path, and within a few minutes you come to Beebe Lake, which is a small artificial lake made by damming Fall Creek in the nineteenth century for water power, and below the dam is Triphammer Falls, which is named for the forge hammer the water once drove.

There is a path all the way round Beebe Lake, about a mile, and it is the walk Cornell students take when they need to not be at Cornell for half an hour. In autumn it is the best half mile in New York State. In winter it freezes.

Now, the wider point, since this is the end of the walk.

Cornell's campus has a gorge on each side, a lake below it, waterfalls inside its own boundaries, and a botanic garden and a four hundred acre nature preserve attached to it. The Cornell Botanic Gardens hold arboretums, a plantation of native trees, wetlands and gorges, and they are free and open and most visitors never find them. The university owns and manages an extraordinary amount of land, because it began as a farm on a hill and never stopped being partly agricultural.

That is the thing this university has that none of the other seven do. Harvard has a yard. Yale has courtyards. Columbia has six blocks of Manhattan. Cornell has weather, and geology, and forty miles of lake, and it built its library so that its reading room looks out over the whole of it.

Look back up the hill toward the tower. If it is the right hour, you may hear the chimes from here.

That is the end of this walk and of the Ivy League tour, if you have done all eight. You have gone from Harvard Yard in Cambridge, the oldest college ground in the country, through New Haven and Princeton and Morningside Heights and Locust Walk and College Hill and a green in the woods of New Hampshire, to a hill above a lake in western New York where a carpenter who laid the first telegraph wire founded a university on the principle that any person could study anything.

Of the eight, this is the youngest by a century, the largest, the least tidy, and the only one whose founder had almost no formal education. It is worth ending here for that reason.

One more practical note about the gorges, since people get hurt every year. Swimming in the gorges is prohibited in most places and the rocks are slippery and the currents under the falls are stronger than they look. Stay on the marked trails, do not climb the walls, and take the closures seriously; the trails shut in winter and after heavy rain because rock falls. The waterfalls are best seen in spring, when the melt is running.

One more thing about the botanic gardens, because they are genuinely one of the best things here and they get almost no visitors. They cover something over three thousand acres in total across the university's holdings, including a formal arboretum, native plant collections, a herb garden, wetlands and the gorges themselves. Admission is free. The arboretum in autumn is the equal of anything in the north east.

If you have a car and half a day, Taughannock Falls is eight miles north along the lake and drops two hundred and fifteen feet, which is higher than Niagara, into a gorge with walls four hundred feet high, and there is a flat path to the base of it.

One more thing about the lake, since it is in every view on this campus. Cayuga is the longest of the Finger Lakes, about thirty eight miles, and it is deep: over four hundred feet in places, which puts its bed below sea level. The lakes were cut by the ice sheet gouging out old river valleys and then dammed by the debris it left, and they are the reason this whole region has the shape it has, including the vineyards on the slopes, because the water moderates the climate enough for grapes to survive a New York winter.

There are wineries the whole length of both shores, and a great many Cornell graduates have ended up running them.

The town is at the bottom of the hill. If you have the time and the knees, go down through Cascadilla Gorge rather than by the road. Thank you for walking it with me.
