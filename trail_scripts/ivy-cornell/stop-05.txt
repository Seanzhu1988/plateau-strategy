You have come up from the slope, so you are entering the quad from its open western end, which is deliberate: this is the way the founders intended people to arrive, with the view behind them and the university ahead.

This is the Arts Quad, and it is the oldest part of Cornell and the closest this campus comes to a plan.

It is a large rectangle of grass with buildings on all four sides, and at the west end, where you came in, it opens toward the slope and the view. The buildings around it are the original stone halls of the university, and if you look along the row on the west side you will see three that were built first, in a line, in local grey stone.

Those are Morrill, McGraw and White halls, from the late eighteen sixties, and they are Cornell's founding buildings. Morrill Hall, the southernmost, was the first building the university opened, in eighteen sixty eight, and it is named for Justin Morrill, the Vermont senator whose Land Grant Act made Cornell possible.

Now look at the two seated bronze figures facing each other across the middle of the quad.

At one end, Ezra Cornell, by Hermon Atkins MacNeil, nineteen nineteen. At the other, Andrew Dickson White, seated, by Karl Bitter, nineteen fifteen. The founder who had the money and the founder who had the ideas, placed to look at each other across the grass they founded.

There is a story every Cornell student is told: that at midnight, when the chimes strike, if a virgin walks between the two statues, they get up, meet in the middle, and shake hands. Since nobody has ever reported it happening, the joke tells you what students think of each other. It is the same shape of joke as Harvard's shoe and Yale's Woolsey toe, and every campus has one.

Now, this is the right place to say what Ezra Cornell actually did, because he is the least conventional founder of the eight.

He was born poor in eighteen oh seven, a Quaker, and he was a carpenter and a millwright. He worked for Samuel Morse laying the first telegraph line, and he solved the practical problem of stringing wire on poles, which was harder than it sounds and which nobody had done. He took payment in stock, the stock became Western Union, and he became very rich. He was a state senator, and he had no formal education beyond a country school.

He gave five hundred thousand dollars and his farm on this hill to found the university, on the condition that it teach anybody anything, and he then spent the last decade of his life fighting the state legislature and the newspapers about it. He died in eighteen seventy four with the university six years old and in financial trouble.

His farm is under your feet.

One more thing about the buildings on this quad, and it is a warning against tidy stories. The three original halls are grey Ithaca stone, quarried locally, and they were built plainly because there was no money for anything else. The later buildings around the quad are limestone, brick, and concrete, from every decade since. Cornell has never had a governing architectural style and has never pretended to want one, and the quad is a record of a hundred and fifty years of building whatever the money and the fashion allowed.

Set that beside Columbia's single controlling plan of eighteen ninety four and you have the two opposite ways an American campus can be made.

One more thing about the Arts Quad's geography, because it explains the shape of the whole campus. The quad sits at the top of the slope with its open end facing west, so the founders deliberately left the best view unbuilt. Every proposal since to close that end has been rejected. It is the one piece of formal planning at Cornell that has held for a hundred and fifty years, and it is a decision to keep a hole in a wall.

Look also at the paths across the grass. Some of them are the paved routes and some are the lines students actually walk, worn into the turf, and the university has periodically given up and paved the worn ones.

From here, walk east across the quad toward the building on the far corner with the columns.
