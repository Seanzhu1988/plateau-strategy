Start on Ho Plaza, outside the student union, because this is the crossroads of Cornell and because you need to understand the ground before you look at any building.

Cornell is on a hill. Not a gentle rise like College Hill at Brown, but a real hill, four hundred feet above Cayuga Lake, with gorges cutting down either side of the campus, and the town of Ithaca laid out on the flat land below. Students say Ithaca is gorges, which is a pun they have made for a century and are not going to stop making. The two gorges, Cascadilla to the south and Fall Creek to the north, have waterfalls in them, inside the campus, and you can walk to both from where you are standing.

That geography decides everything: which buildings are where, why students are always going up or down, and why the university has bridges rather than paths in places.

Now the university, and it is different from the other seven on this tour in a way that shows in every building.

Cornell was founded in eighteen sixty five, a hundred years after most of the others, by Ezra Cornell and Andrew Dickson White. Ezra Cornell was a farmer and a carpenter from upstate New York who had made a fortune building telegraph lines for Samuel Morse and who was the largest shareholder in Western Union. White was a young academic and politician who had studied in Europe and had ideas.

They founded it under the Morrill Land Grant Act, which Congress passed in eighteen sixty two, giving states federal land to sell to endow colleges teaching agriculture and the mechanic arts. Cornell is New York's land grant university, which means it is a private university with four state supported colleges inside it, an arrangement that exists nowhere else and that nobody would design on purpose.

And the founding idea is on every piece of paper this place produces. Ezra Cornell said: I would found an institution where any person can find instruction in any study.

Read that again, because in eighteen sixty five every word of it was radical. Any person meant that it admitted women from eighteen seventy two, and Black students from the start, when most of the colleges on this tour did neither. Any study meant that agriculture and engineering and hotel management would be taught alongside classics, as equals, when the others thought a gentleman studied Greek. It was nonsectarian at a time when that was genuinely shocking, and White was attacked for years as an atheist.

The campus you are about to walk is that idea in stone: enormous, various, unmatched, with no single style, because it was never meant to be one thing.

One more thing about the founding, because the coeducation part was not smooth. Cornell admitted its first woman in eighteen seventy and opened properly to women in eighteen seventy two, when Henry Sage gave the money for a residence hall on the condition that the university genuinely commit to it. The trustees argued. Faculty predicted disaster. Sage Hall was built, women came, and Cornell became the first Ivy League institution to admit women as undergraduates on the same terms as men, nearly a century before Yale and Princeton did in nineteen sixty nine.

It is also worth saying that the promise of any person was more complete on paper than in practice, and Cornell's own historians have documented the gaps: quotas, exclusions from housing, and the ordinary prejudices of the period, applied here as everywhere else.

A word about how this place got its land, because the Morrill Act deserves it and because Cornell's own historians have made the point forcefully. The federal land the act distributed was not empty. It was taken from Indigenous nations, by treaty, by coercion and by outright seizure, and the sale of it endowed the land grant colleges of which this is one.

Ezra Cornell was unusually shrewd about the mechanism: rather than sell New York's land scrip cheaply as most states did, he bought it himself and used it to acquire prime timber land in Wisconsin, held it, and sold it over decades for far more, which is why Cornell's endowment from the act dwarfed every other state's. That shrewdness is why this university is rich, and the land it was made from was Native land. Cornell published research on exactly this in twenty twenty, naming the parcels and the nations they were taken from.

Walk north from here, up the slope toward the clock tower.
