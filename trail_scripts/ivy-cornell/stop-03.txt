Before you go in, a word about the architect, because he is all over this town and almost unknown outside it. William Henry Miller was in the first class to enrol at Cornell, in eighteen sixty eight, studied architecture under Charles Babcock, and then spent his whole career in Ithaca. He built this library, several other buildings on the campus, and a great many houses in the town, and if you walk the residential streets below the hill you will pass a dozen of them.

This is Uris Library, from eighteen ninety one, and inside it is the most beautiful room at Cornell.

The architect was William Henry Miller, who was the first student to enrol in Cornell's architecture programme and who then designed a great deal of Ithaca. The style is Richardsonian Romanesque, in stone, heavy, with round arches.

Go in and ask for the A. D. White Library. It is up a stair, and it is a two storey reading room in cast iron and wood: tall bookcases with a gallery running round at first floor level on iron brackets, spiral stairs in the corners, arched windows at the end, dark wood, green lamps. It was built to hold Andrew Dickson White's own library, which he gave to the university, and it looks like the platonic idea of a nineteenth century library because it is one.

Students study in it. It is not a museum room; it is in use, and it is quiet, and you may go in and look as long as you are quiet too.

Now, the lawsuit, because it began in this building and it is the great scandal of Cornell's early history.

Jennie McGraw was a Cornell trustee's daughter and one of the richest women in the state. She married Willard Fiske, Cornell's first librarian, in eighteen eighty. She was already ill with tuberculosis. She died in eighteen eighty one, a year later, and left the bulk of her fortune, something around two million dollars, to Cornell to build a great library.

Her widower Fiske contested the will. The university fought him. The case ran for years and went to the Supreme Court of the United States, and in eighteen ninety the Court ruled against Cornell on a technicality: under its charter the university was limited in how much property it could hold, and it could not take the bequest. The money went to Fiske, who left Cornell, moved to Florence, and spent the rest of his life collecting books.

Cornell got the tower, built with money already committed, and lost the library. Fiske, in the end, left his own collections to Cornell when he died, including the world's best collections on Dante and on Iceland, so the university got something back after all. But it lost thirty years of endowment, and the affair split the trustees and the faculty for a generation.

One more thing about Willard Fiske, since he comes out of the lawsuit looking like the villain and the story is more complicated. He was a serious scholar and a genuine eccentric: he had learned Icelandic, corresponded across Europe, and was the university's first librarian. After the case he settled in Florence in a villa, collected obsessively, and when he died in nineteen oh four he left Cornell his collections after all: the Dante collection, the Petrarch collection, and the Icelandic collection, each among the best in the world. They are in this library system now.

So the man who took the money away spent the rest of his life buying books and then gave them to the university that sued him.

One more thing about the reading room itself, because it is worth looking at properly rather than photographing. The iron gallery, the spiral stairs and the shelving are all original nineteenth century library engineering, from the period when cast iron let builders stack books higher than a wooden floor could carry. Look at the brackets under the gallery and at the openwork of the stair treads. Iron stack construction like this was invented for libraries in the middle of the nineteenth century and it is the direct ancestor of the steel stack towers at Yale and Harvard.

There is also a rather good tradition attached: the room is open late, and generations of students have claimed particular desks in it as their own for a whole term.

Come out and stand in front of the tower, and look west, and you are at the top of a long slope.
