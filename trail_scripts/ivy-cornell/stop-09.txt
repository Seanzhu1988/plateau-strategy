One thing before you look at it: the name. Herbert Fisk Johnson Junior was a Cornell alumnus and the head of the family firm S. C. Johnson, the wax company in Racine, Wisconsin, whose headquarters Frank Lloyd Wright designed in the nineteen thirties. So the family that commissioned one of Wright's masterpieces paid for a Pei building here, which is a good pedigree for a small museum in upstate New York.

This is the Herbert F. Johnson Museum of Art, from nineteen seventy three, by I. M. Pei, and it stands on the edge of the hill at the north west corner of the Arts Quad, where the ground falls away toward Fall Creek gorge.

Pei was not yet famous when he took this. He became famous shortly afterwards for the East Building of the National Gallery in Washington and then, in the nineteen eighties, for the glass pyramid at the Louvre. This is early Pei and it is concrete: a tower of stacked horizontal slabs with a great void punched through the upper part, cantilevered out over the slope.

Students call it the sewing machine, because from the right angle that is exactly what it looks like. Pei is said not to have minded.

Go in, and here is the instruction: take the lift straight to the fifth floor before you look at anything else.

On the fifth floor is the Asian art collection, which is the museum's greatest strength, and it is arranged in a single long gallery with, at the end, a wall of glass. Through it you are looking north west over the gorge, over the lake, and down forty miles of water between the hills. It is the best window in any museum I know. The Chinese and Japanese objects are extremely good, and half the people up there are looking out of the window instead, and the museum has never tried to stop them.

Then work down. The collection runs to something over thirty five thousand objects: American painting, European prints and drawings, a strong contemporary holding, and a good deal of photography. It is a teaching museum in the same way as Harvard's and Princeton's, and the study room shows objects to anyone who asks.

Admission is free, always, and it is closed on Mondays.

There is one more thing about the building. Because it hangs on the edge of the slope, it is much taller on the downhill side than the uphill side, and the entrance is high up in it, so you go in near the top of a building that continues a long way below you. Walk round the outside afterwards and look at it from the north, from the lower ground, and it is a different building entirely.

Now, from here, we are going to the edge of the campus, because the last two stops are the reason people who love Cornell love it.

One more thing about the building's reception, since it followed the usual pattern. When it opened in nineteen seventy three, plenty of people at Cornell disliked it: too raw, too big, wrong for the quad, blocking the view. Half a century later it is a listed part of the campus's identity and appears on the merchandise. That is now the fourth or fifth time on this tour that a building was hated on arrival and loved later, and it should make anybody cautious about their own first reaction to a new building.

One more thing about the collection worth knowing, because it is unusual for a university museum. The Johnson has a very strong holding of Asian art, built up over a century, and it was one of the first American university museums to treat Asian art as central rather than as a supplement to a European story. That is partly Cornell's own history: the university has taught Asian languages and history since the nineteenth century and has long had large numbers of students from Asia.

The prints and drawings study room will bring out works on paper for anyone who asks, which is the same open door policy you met at Yale and at Dartmouth.

One more practical note. The museum has a small cafe and, more usefully, a terrace, and the fifth floor gallery with the window is the coldest place on campus to sit on a hot day and the warmest place to look at the lake on a cold one. Students use it as a reading room, quietly, and nobody minds.

Walk north from the museum, past the buildings, toward the bridge.
