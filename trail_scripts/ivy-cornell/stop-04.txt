You are standing at the top of Libe Slope, which is the great lawn falling away west from the library toward West Campus and the town, and it is where Cornell sits down.

Stand here for a full minute before you read any more, because the view is the reason this stop exists and it does not need commentary.

Look at the view. On a clear evening you are looking over the roofs of Ithaca, down the length of Cayuga Lake, which runs forty miles north, with the hills on both sides. It is the best view from any campus on this tour and it is not close.

The slope is steep. In winter students sled down it on cafeteria trays, which the university periodically discourages and has never stopped. In spring they lie on it. At sunset in warm weather there will be a hundred people on this grass.

Here is the practical thing about Cornell that this slope explains. The campus is on top of the hill, and a large part of the undergraduate housing, including most of the first year dormitories, is at the bottom of it on West Campus. So students go up and down this hill several times a day, in all weathers, including an Ithaca winter that runs from November to April and is genuinely severe. There are buses. Most people walk. The phrase for it is well known here and the slope is the reason.

Now, since you are looking at the town, the town deserves a paragraph.

Ithaca has about thirty two thousand people, and about half of them are students, between Cornell up here and Ithaca College on the hill on the other side. It is a small city in a rural county, four hours from New York and five from Toronto, and it is famous for being extremely liberal, extremely outdoorsy and extremely far from anywhere. The gorges, the waterfalls and the state parks around it are why people who love it love it.

Below you, at the bottom of the slope, is Collegetown, which is the student neighbourhood: bars, cheap food, apartments. Down in the flat part of town is the Commons, a pedestrian main street, and the farmers market by the lake on Saturdays, which is one of the best in the country.

And the two gorges are the boundaries of this campus, north and south, each with waterfalls in them and trails along them. Cascadilla Gorge Trail climbs from the town to the campus past six waterfalls, and it is the most beautiful way to arrive at a university anywhere in America. If you have the legs and it is open, come up that way rather than by bus.

One more thing about the winter, because visitors from milder places do not believe it. Ithaca sits at the bottom of a valley in the snow belt east of the Great Lakes. It gets something over sixty inches of snow in an average year, the cloud sits in the valley for weeks, and there is a local joke that the sun is a rumour between November and March. The university does not close for snow.

Students adapt in visible ways: the tunnels and covered walkways on parts of the campus, the layering, the enthusiasm for anything happening indoors, and the almost religious response to the first warm day in April, when the slope fills within an hour.

One more thing about the slope, which is the single most photographed piece of ground at Cornell. At the top of it, near where you are standing, is where the university holds its convocation and where students gather for the last night of term. At the bottom is West Campus, rebuilt in the two thousands into a set of residential houses with their own dining halls, on the Harkness model that Yale and Harvard adopted in the nineteen thirties, which Cornell came to seventy years later.

So the hill is not only a view. It is the daily physical connection between where students sleep and where they learn, and the university has spent a great deal of money on both ends of it.

Now turn around, walk back past the tower, and go north east into the open quadrangle.
