Look up before you go in, and notice that the tower is not free standing: it grows out of the corner of the library beside it, which is why it looks slightly odd from some angles, as though a cathedral tower has been attached to a house.

This is McGraw Tower, a hundred and seventy three feet of stone with a clock and twenty one bells in it, finished in eighteen ninety one, and it is the thing you see from the road, from the lake, and from the far side of the valley.

The tower is attached to Uris Library and was given by Jennie McGraw, whose family money came from lumber, and whose bequest to the university became one of the longest and most damaging lawsuits in the history of American higher education, which is a story for another stop.

Now the bells, because this is where Cornell differs from every other campus on this tour.

The Cornell Chimes have been rung since the university opened in eighteen sixty eight. There were originally nine bells and there are now twenty one. They are played three times a day during term: a morning concert, a midday concert and an evening one, roughly at quarter past seven, quarter past one and six o'clock, though the times shift. They have been played nearly every day for over a hundred and fifty years, which makes this one of the longest running daily traditions at any American university.

They are played by students. Every year the Cornell Chimesmasters hold a competition, and it is genuinely hard: candidates spend weeks learning the instrument and are judged, and only a handful are chosen. It is one of the most competitive things a Cornell undergraduate can try for, and the winners then get up in the dark in February to climb a tower and play for the campus.

And you can go up. This is the point. During the concerts, visitors may climb the tower, a hundred and sixty one steps up a stone spiral, and stand in the playing chamber a few feet from the chimesmaster while they play. There is no ticket and no charge. You just turn up, at the right time, and climb.

The instrument is not a keyboard in the ordinary sense. It is a rack of wooden levers connected by wires to the clappers, and the player strikes them with closed fists and works the pedals with their feet, and it is physical work. Watch their hands.

The repertoire is anything. There is a traditional programme, the Cornell alma mater and the Jennie McGraw Rag, and then whatever the player has arranged that week: Bach, folk tunes, television themes, pop songs. Requests are taken.

At the top, above the playing chamber, is an observation level, and from it you look out over the whole campus, down the length of Cayuga Lake, and across the valley to the hills.

If you can arrange your visit around a chimes concert, do it, because it is the single best free thing on any of these eight campuses.

There is a practical warning about the climb. The staircase is a stone spiral, narrow, with a rope handrail, and there are a hundred and sixty one steps. It is not for anyone unsteady, and there is no lift. If you cannot climb it, stand on the quad below during a concert instead: the bells are loud enough to be heard across the campus and down into the town, and the sound is better outdoors than in the chamber anyway.

The tower is also, less romantically, the university's clock, and it has kept the time on this hill since eighteen ninety one.

There is a second bell tower story worth knowing, because it involves a pumpkin. In October nineteen ninety seven somebody put a pumpkin on top of the spire of McGraw Tower, a hundred and seventy three feet up, on a spike, overnight, and nobody has ever admitted to it. It stayed there through the winter, freeze dried in the wind, visible from the whole campus, and became a minor national news story. It was finally removed the following March with a crane. Nobody knows who did it or how, and the university's official position is amused ignorance.

It is on this campus in the collection, or what is left of it. Ask a Cornellian about the pumpkin and watch them light up.
