You have walked east and slightly downhill from the chapel, and you are now near the edge of the original campus, where the university's buildings give way to the slope down toward the gorge.

This is the Andrew Dickson White House, from eighteen seventy one, and it is the private house of a man building a university, standing in the middle of the university he built.

White was Cornell's first president and he designed this house with his architect while the campus was going up around him, and he lived in it. It is brick, Gothic in feeling, with steep roofs and a tower, and it looks like a well off Victorian family house because that is what it was.

Inside is a library room built for his own books, which were the foundation of the university's collection, and that room survives.

The house is now the Society for the Humanities, which is a research institute that brings scholars from around the world to Ithaca for a year at a time to work on a theme, and it holds lectures that are open to the public.

Now, White is the one to talk about here, because he is the intellectual half of this university and he is underrated.

He was born in eighteen thirty two to a wealthy family in Syracuse, went to Yale, then spent years in Europe, studying in Berlin and Paris and serving as an attaché in St Petersburg, which was unusual for an American of his generation. He came back with a conviction that American higher education was narrow, sectarian and backward compared with the German research universities, and he wanted to build something else.

When Ezra Cornell had the money and the land and the will to found a college, and the New York legislature had the land grant to allocate, White was a state senator on the education committee and the two men found each other. Cornell provided the fortune. White provided the plan: a university, nonsectarian, coeducational, teaching every subject, hiring on merit, with no test of belief.

He then spent eighteen years as president making it work, and afterward he was the American minister to Germany and to Russia and led the American delegation to the first Hague peace conference.

The two founders are buried a few hundred yards from here in the chapel you have just seen.

Look at the house once more. A university president in eighteen seventy one lived on his campus in a family house with his own library, and students came to him. The presidents of these institutions now live in official residences off campus with security, and the change happened everywhere within a century.

One more thing about the house's second life. The Society for the Humanities was founded in nineteen sixty six and it invents a theme each year, brings in fellows from around the world to work on it, and holds seminars and public lectures. That model, an interdisciplinary institute with a rotating theme and visiting fellows, was new when Cornell did it and has been copied widely.

So a Victorian president's private house now holds a permanent rotating conversation between scholars from different fields, which is closer to what White wanted from a university than most lecture halls manage.

One more thing about the house, which is easy to miss from outside. Behind it is a garden and beyond that the ground drops toward the gorge, so the site White chose for himself was the one with the view, on the edge of the campus he was laying out. Presidents choose their own plots.

The building was also, for a period in the twentieth century, the university's art museum, before the Johnson was built, so it has been a house, a museum and a research institute, which is the usual life of a good building on a campus that keeps growing.

There is one more Cornell first worth attaching to White, because it followed directly from his hiring policy. Cornell appointed the first professor of American history at any university, and it created the first four year degree in journalism, the first university course in electrical engineering, and the first degree in veterinary medicine in the country. It hired non resident lecturers, including Louis Agassiz and James Russell Lowell, which was White's idea for getting eminent people to a hill in western New York without asking them to move there.

Whether every one of those firsts survives close scrutiny is the sort of thing universities argue about, but the pattern is real: a new university with no traditions to protect could do things the old ones could not.

Now turn back north west, and walk down toward the museum on the edge of the gorge.
