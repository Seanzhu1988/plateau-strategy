Before you go in, note where it sits: down the slope from the Arts Quad, off the main axis, near the centre of the campus but not at the head of it. Compare that with Princeton, where the chapel is enormous and central, and with Columbia, where it is tucked to one side. The position of a chapel on a campus is a statement about how central worship was meant to be, and Cornell's is exactly as central as its charter required, which is to say present and not governing.

This is Sage Chapel, from eighteen seventy five, and it is where Cornell's founders are buried, and it is not what you would expect from a university that was attacked for being godless.

The building is by Charles Babcock, who was Cornell's first professor of architecture and an Episcopal priest, and it has been extended several times, so it is a long low building with a tower, in brick and stone, and inside it is a surprise: dark, richly painted, with a mosaic apse, stencilled walls and a wooden ceiling covered in decoration.

It was given by Henry Sage, a lumber millionaire and trustee, and it was built as a nonsectarian chapel, which was the whole argument. Andrew Dickson White had insisted that Cornell would have no religious affiliation and no compulsory worship, and he was denounced across the country for it. Sage, who was religious, gave the chapel on the understanding that it would serve all denominations and none, and Cornell has kept that: services here are nondenominational and the chapel is used by many faiths.

White wrote a two volume book about the row, published in eighteen ninety six, called A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom, which argued that religious authority had obstructed scientific inquiry for centuries. It was enormously influential and it is still argued about by historians, many of whom think White overstated it considerably. He wrote it out of this fight.

Now go into the memorial antechapel, which is off the entrance, because the founders are in it.

Ezra Cornell is buried here, and Andrew Dickson White and his wife, and Henry Sage, and Jennie McGraw, whose contested will you heard about at the library. They are in sarcophagi, in a small vaulted room with mosaics, and the whole founding generation of the university is in one room.

Stand in it for a moment. The man who laid telegraph wire and the man who wrote about the warfare of science and theology, buried a few feet apart in a chapel that neither of them would have described the same way.

The chapel is open daily and it is free, and there are organ recitals and concerts. Look up at the ceiling and at the apse mosaic before you leave, and look at the memorial windows, several of which are by the best American studios of the period.

One more note on White's book, because its reputation matters. The warfare thesis, the idea that religion and science have been in permanent conflict, was enormously influential in the twentieth century and is now largely rejected by historians of science, who point out that the actual history is far messier: many of the founders of modern science were devout, and many of the disputes White described were about authority and institutions rather than about belief. His book was a weapon in a fight he was losing at the time, and it worked, and it has outlived its own accuracy.

One more thing about the chapel's own architecture, because it changed. Babcock built it in eighteen seventy five and it was extended twice, in eighteen ninety eight and nineteen forty, and each extension was done in keeping, so the building reads as one thing and is not. The apse mosaic and the painted decoration are largely from the later work.

Look for the memorial windows down the sides, several of which commemorate individual Cornellians, and one of which is by the Tiffany studios. A chapel that is nonsectarian by charter has ended up as the place where this university remembers its own, which is what a chapel does whatever it says over the door.

From here, walk east and downhill, and there is one more thing worth seeing on this side before we turn back.
