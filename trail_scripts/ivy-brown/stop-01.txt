Start on Prospect Street, in front of the iron gates, and do not walk through the middle one. I will explain in a moment, and it is the best superstition on this whole tour because it works in two directions.

These are the Van Wickle Gates, given in nineteen oh one under the will of Augustus Van Wickle, a coal fortune, in memory of his time here. They stand at the top of College Street, and if you turn round and look back down the hill you will see the reason this spot exists: a long straight view down College Street to the west, over the roofs of Providence, ending at the gold dome of the Rhode Island State House about half a mile away. That is one of the best views in New England and almost nobody photographs it, because everyone is facing the other way, at the gates.

Now the rule. The centre gate is opened twice a year. In September, at convocation, it swings inward and the entering class walks up the hill and through it into the university. In May, at commencement, it swings outward and the graduating class walks down through it and out. Those are the only two times it opens.

And the superstition attached is precise. A student who walks through the centre gate at any other time, in either direction, will not graduate. Students take it seriously enough that you will see them step round to the side openings without breaking stride. There is a second version, that walking out through the middle more than once means you will not graduate on time, and a third, that a student who passes through it a third time will marry someone from Brown.

You are a visitor, so nothing binds you, and you may walk through the middle. But look first at how the gates work: a wide central opening with a pair of smaller ones either side, which is exactly the arrangement of a triumphal arch, and that is not an accident. The nineteen oh one design is a ceremonial arch for a procession of about four hundred people twice a year, and it stands closed the rest of the time.

Here is the university you are walking into. Brown was founded in seventeen sixty four as the College in the English Colony of Rhode Island and Providence Plantations, which is a mouthful and was the seventh college in the colonies. It was Baptist, and its charter did something none of the others had done: it guaranteed that no religious test would be applied to any student, and that members of other denominations would sit on its governing boards. In a century when Harvard was Congregationalist and Princeton Presbyterian and everyone examined everyone else's theology, Rhode Island, which had been founded by Roger Williams as a refuge for people the Puritans had thrown out, wrote religious liberty into its college's charter.

The name came in eighteen oh four, when Nicholas Brown Junior gave five thousand dollars. His family's money, and the university's beginnings, come partly from the transatlantic slave trade, and Brown has confronted that more directly than almost any American university. We will come to it.

One more thing before you go through, about the view behind you. That gold dome down College Street is the Rhode Island State House, finished in nineteen oh four by McKim, Mead and White, the same firm that laid out Columbia's campus and built Harvard's main gate. It has one of the largest self supporting marble domes in the world, and on top of it stands a gilded figure called the Independent Man, which is as good a summary of this state's opinion of itself as anything. Rhode Island was the first colony to renounce allegiance to the crown, in May seventeen seventy six, and the last of the thirteen states to ratify the Constitution, in seventeen ninety, holding out until it was threatened with being treated as a foreign country.

A word about the size of this place, because it shapes everything you are about to see. Brown has around seven thousand undergraduates and about eleven thousand students in total, which makes it the smallest of the eight on this tour after Dartmouth. It has no business school and no law school. It has a medical school, founded properly in nineteen seventy five, which is very young by these standards, and an engineering school that is the oldest in the Ivy League.

The consequence is that Brown is an undergraduate college with some graduate work attached, and it says so, and the undergraduates are the centre of the institution in a way they are not everywhere.

Now, through the gates, and stop on the green.
