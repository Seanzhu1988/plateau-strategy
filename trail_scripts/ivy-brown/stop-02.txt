This is University Hall, and it is the oldest building at Brown, finished in seventeen seventy, four years before the Revolution began and six years after the college itself.

Look at it. Brick, four storeys, plain, with a gambrel roof and a small cupola, and a shape you have met twice already on this tour: it is a close cousin of Nassau Hall at Princeton and of the colonial halls at Harvard, because it was built for the same purpose by people looking at the same models. The builders here explicitly used Nassau Hall as their pattern. For its first decades this building was the entire college: dormitory, classrooms, chapel, library, dining hall, and the president's residence.

Now the war, because this building had a hard one.

In December seventeen seventy six the college closed. American troops occupied University Hall as a barracks, and then it became a military hospital, and then in seventeen eighty French troops under Rochambeau, marching to join Washington, were quartered here on their way south to Yorktown. The building was used by soldiers for most of the war and was badly damaged. Classes did not resume until seventeen eighty two. There is a plaque about the French troops.

So the same story you heard at Massachusetts Hall in Cambridge and at Nassau Hall in Princeton: the college building emptied of students and filled with soldiers, and the college billing the government afterwards for the damage.

It is the president's office now and the offices of the corporation, so it is not open, and like Massachusetts Hall at Harvard that makes it the building where Brown's arguments arrive. Students have occupied it more than once.

Now walk out onto the grass in front of it, because the green is the thing here.

Brown's campus sits on top of College Hill, on the east side of Providence, and the main green is a rectangle of lawn with the oldest buildings round it. It is smaller and more domestic than Harvard Yard and considerably less grand than anything at Yale or Columbia, and that is the character of the place. Brown has never had the money of the others and it has never wanted the pomp, and the campus reflects it: brick, wood, modest scale, a lot of trees.

Look at the buildings on the far side of the green. The temple fronted one in the middle is Manning Hall, from eighteen thirty four, which is a Greek revival chapel. Beside it, the Romanesque one is Sayles. The plain brick range to the north is Hope College, a dormitory from eighteen twenty two. All of them face this lawn, and the lawn is where the university gathers.

There is a tree on this green worth finding, and any student can point you at it. Brown's commencement procession comes down through the gates and along this green, and the whole ceremony happens outdoors in the street below, which is unusual and which we will come to at the end.

Look at the roof line and the cupola as you take it in. Both have been rebuilt: the building was extensively remodelled in the eighteen thirties and again, more importantly, in nineteen forty and forty one, when the university took it back to something closer to its eighteenth century appearance after a century of alterations. That is a common story with colonial buildings in America. What you are usually looking at is a nineteen thirties or forties idea of what the seventeen seventies looked like, done carefully, on original walls.

There is a plaque about the French troops on the exterior. Rochambeau's army marched from Newport through Providence in June seventeen eighty one on its way to join Washington, and the campaign that ended at Yorktown three months later effectively went past this door.

It is worth pausing on the charter for a moment, because it is genuinely unusual and it was written here. Brown's charter of seventeen sixty four says that no religious test shall be applied to any student, that the faculty shall be open to all denominations, and that the governing boards shall be shared out among Baptists, Quakers, Congregationalists and Anglicans in fixed proportions. That is written into the founding document, in a decade when Harvard required a religious profession and Yale examined its students on doctrine.

Rhode Island had been founded by Roger Williams in sixteen thirty six after Massachusetts banished him for arguing that the state had no business in a man's conscience, and the colony took in Quakers and Jews and Baptists whom the Puritans would not have. The oldest synagogue building in America is at Newport, thirty miles from here. The college's charter is that colony's idea applied to education.
