This is the John Carter Brown Library, from nineteen oh four, and it is one of the great collections in the world sitting quietly on a college green in Rhode Island.

The building is Beaux Arts, in white marble, with a columned front, by Shepley Rutan and Coolidge, and it is deliberately more formal than anything around it. That is because it is not really a university building. The John Carter Brown Library is independent: it has its own board, its own endowment and its own director, and it sits on Brown's campus by arrangement. It was given by the family, and the collection was begun by John Carter Brown in the eighteen forties.

What it holds is this: books, maps and manuscripts about the Americas, printed before eighteen twenty five. Not about America the country, but about the Americas as a whole, north and south, from the moment Europeans first wrote about them. It is one of the two or three best collections of early Americana anywhere, and scholars fly here from other continents to use it.

Think about what that means. Somewhere in this building is a copy of the letter Columbus wrote in fourteen ninety three describing what he had found, printed within months and read across Europe. There are the first printed accounts of Mexico and Peru. There are the earliest maps that show the coastline being worked out, decade by decade, as ships came back with more information, and you can watch the shape of the continents assemble across a century of printing.

There are books printed in Mexico City in the fifteen thirties, which is decades before anything was printed in English North America. There is material in indigenous languages, printed by missionaries, which is sometimes the earliest written record of those languages.

The reading room is for scholars, but the library runs exhibitions in its gallery that are free and open to anybody, and there is usually something remarkable out. Ask at the desk. The staff are used to people wandering in and they are proud of the place.

Here is the thing to hold on to as you stand in front of it. This library and the report on slavery you just heard about are two halves of the same subject. The books inside describe the European arrival in the Americas, written by the people doing the arriving. The report describes what that became, and how the money from it built the college around this building. Brown has both on one lawn and does not pretend they are unconnected.

The library was also, in a small way, the beginning of a discipline: the systematic collecting of Americana as a scholarly field started with private collectors like John Carter Brown in the nineteenth century, and their libraries became the foundation of American historical scholarship.

One more thing about how a collection like this is used now. Almost all of the John Carter Brown's holdings have been digitised and are freely available online, so a student in Lima or Lagos can read a sixteenth century account of the conquest without leaving home. That is the quiet revolution in rare books of the last twenty years: the objects still need a temperature controlled room and a scholar's appointment, and their contents no longer do.

The building is worth two more minutes of looking at from outside. It was designed to hold a private collection given to a university, which is an odd brief, and the result is a small, formal, self contained thing with its back to the campus and its front to the green, sitting slightly apart from everything, exactly like the institution it houses.

One more thing about what makes a collection like this valuable, since to a visitor it is a room of old books. Rarity is only half of it. The other half is completeness: because John Carter Brown and his successors set out to acquire everything printed about the Americas before eighteen twenty five, a scholar can come here and know that what is absent is genuinely absent, rather than merely not collected. That is what makes a research library different from a rich man's shelf, and it is why the discipline of the history of the book grew up around collections like this one.

Come out and turn north, walking up past the green, and the tall thin brick tower on the corner is next.
