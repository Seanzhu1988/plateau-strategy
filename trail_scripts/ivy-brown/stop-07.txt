This is the John Hay Library, from nineteen ten, and it holds the strangest collection on this tour.

The building is white marble, classical, by Shepley Rutan and Coolidge again, and it was the university's main library until nineteen sixty four. It is named for John Hay, Brown class of eighteen fifty eight, who was Abraham Lincoln's private secretary as a young man, later ambassador to Britain, and then Secretary of State under McKinley and Roosevelt, in which job he ran the Open Door policy toward China and negotiated the treaty that led to the Panama Canal. Andrew Carnegie paid for the building at Hay's request.

It is now the special collections library, and here is what is in it.

The Lincoln collection, one of the largest anywhere, built on Hay's own papers: he was in the room for a great deal of the Lincoln presidency and he kept everything.

The papers of H. P. Lovecraft, who was born in Providence in eighteen ninety, lived most of his life on this hill, and wrote the horror fiction that has shaped the genre ever since. He died poor and largely unpublished in book form in nineteen thirty seven and is now read everywhere. The library holds his manuscripts, letters and books. His work is also, and the library says so plainly, deeply racist, and the collection is used to study that as much as the fiction.

The Anne S. K. Brown Military Collection: about half a million items on the history of soldiers and how they dressed, which is the largest such collection in the world and looks eccentric until you realise it is one of the main sources for the material history of European armies.

The Harris Collection of American Poetry and Plays, which aims to hold every book of American poetry ever published, and comes remarkably close.

And then the ones people come for. There are books bound in human skin, three of them, which the library has publicly reckoned with and now handles under a formal policy; anthropodermic bibliopegy was a real if rare nineteenth century practice. There is a substantial collection on magic and the occult. There is a collection of American sheet music. There is one of the largest collections of toy soldiers in existence.

The exhibition gallery is free and open on weekdays and there is nearly always something out. Ask at the desk what is on show.

Look at the front of the building on your way past, at the names carved along it. Nineteenth and early twentieth century libraries did this everywhere: a list of the great, cut in stone, chosen by a committee. You have seen it at Columbia on Butler and at Harvard. It is the same impulse and the same slightly narrow list.

One more thing about the building's own history. When the John Hay opened in nineteen ten it was the main library, and by the nineteen sixties it had been outgrown, so the university built the concrete tower you will see shortly and moved the working collection into it, leaving this building to the rare books. That is the same story as Low at Columbia and Chancellor Green at Princeton and the Fogg at Harvard: the beautiful old library becomes something else, and the ugly new one gets the books. It happens to every university within about eighty years of building a library, and no institution on this tour has escaped it.

While you are here, a word about what a special collections library is actually for, because it is easy to see one as a treasure house. The Lovecraft papers are used by scholars studying race and American culture as much as by people who love the stories. The military collection is used by historians of dress, of technology, and of the state. The human skin bindings are used to teach the ethics of collecting. None of that is decoration: a library like this exists so that arguments can be settled by looking at the object rather than by repeating what somebody said about it.

The building is open on weekdays and the exhibition space is free.

From here, walk south along the west side of the green, and the plain building on the corner opposite the gates is where we started, so instead cut east across the green toward the modern one with the arch.
