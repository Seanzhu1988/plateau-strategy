This is Sayles Hall, from eighteen eighty one, and it is the room where Brown keeps its own face.

It was given by William Sayles in memory of his son, who died at twenty, and it was designed by Alpheus Morse in a heavy Romanesque manner: rough faced stone in bands of colour, round arches, a squat tower, deep window openings. If it reminds you of Sever Hall at Harvard or the Fisher library at Penn, that is the same decade and the same taste for weight and mass after Richardson.

Go inside if you can. The great hall on the first floor is one long room with a high wooden ceiling, and hanging on the walls, in rows, are painted portraits of every president of Brown University since seventeen sixty five, along with benefactors and professors. There are dozens of them, in gilt frames, looking down at whatever is happening in the room. Lectures, concerts, meetings and examinations happen here, and generations of students have sat under those faces.

At the far end is the Hutchings Votey organ, installed in nineteen oh three, a large instrument that is played for concerts and for one particular tradition.

That tradition is the reason to mention this building at all. On the night before Halloween, the university holds an organ concert in this hall at midnight, in the dark, with the portraits lit. It has been going for decades, it is packed, and the programme is exactly what you would expect. It is the single most Brown thing on this campus: an eighteenth century institution with a nineteenth century hall and a twentieth century organ, using all three to have a party in the dark.

Now, while you are standing in front of these portraits, this is the right place to say the thing about Brown's own history that the university itself has said at length.

In two thousand and three, Brown's president Ruth Simmons, who was the first Black president of an Ivy League university and the great granddaughter of enslaved people, appointed a Steering Committee on Slavery and Justice and asked it to investigate the university's own relationship to the transatlantic slave trade and to report publicly. No American university had done this.

The report came in two thousand and six. It found that the Brown family, whose name the university carries, were deeply involved: John Brown owned and outfitted slave ships, including the Sally, whose voyage in seventeen sixty four killed more than a hundred of the people aboard. It found that enslaved people worked on the construction of University Hall, the building you have just looked at. It found that a substantial part of the money that founded and sustained the college came from that trade, and it also found that Moses Brown, John's brother, became an abolitionist and freed the people he had enslaved, and that the family argued with itself for decades.

Brown published all of it, built a memorial on this campus, funded work in the Providence public schools, and endowed a centre for the study of slavery and justice. Other universities have since done similar work, and Brown's report is the document that started it.

One more note about the portraits, since they are the memorable thing in the room. They run from seventeen sixty five to the present, which means the room contains a continuous visual record of what the head of an American university was supposed to look like across two hundred and sixty years: Baptist ministers in black, then nineteenth century men with beards, then twentieth century men in academic gowns, and then, from two thousand and one, Ruth Simmons. The room tells the story of the institution simply by being hung in order.

There is one more part of the slavery and justice story that belongs here rather than in a footnote, because it is about this ground. The report established that enslaved and free Black labourers worked on the construction of University Hall in seventeen seventy, the building you looked at ten minutes ago. In two thousand and twenty the university dedicated a memorial on this campus, a sculpture by Martin Puryear near the front green, in the form of a broken shackle and chain partly buried in the earth, with an inscription about the trade and the university's part in it.

Go and find it after this walk if you have ten minutes. It is deliberately not grand, and it is placed where students cross rather than in a corner.

Walk out and go east, downhill a little, toward the building with the columns and the reading room.
