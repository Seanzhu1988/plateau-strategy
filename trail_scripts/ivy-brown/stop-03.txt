This is Manning Hall, from eighteen thirty four, and it is a Greek temple standing on a New England college green, which needs explaining.

Look at the front: four Doric columns, a plain pediment, no ornament, painted stone. It is a copy, and a deliberate one, of a specific ancient building: the Temple of Diana Propylaea at Eleusis in Greece, scaled up to double the size of the original. The architect was James Bucklin, working with the amateur enthusiasm of the moment, and the moment was the Greek Revival, which swept America in the eighteen twenties and thirties.

Here is why. Greece was fighting its war of independence against the Ottoman Empire through the eighteen twenties, and Americans, who had recently fought their own, were passionately for it. Lord Byron died at Missolonghi in eighteen twenty four and became a martyr on both sides of the Atlantic. At the same time American builders were discovering accurate measured drawings of Greek buildings, published by Stuart and Revett, for the first time. The result was that for about twenty years every bank, courthouse, church and college hall in the United States was a Greek temple, and towns across the country were named Athens, Sparta, Ithaca and Troy.

You saw this on the Philadelphia walk, if you did it, at the Second Bank of the United States, which is a Parthenon on Chestnut Street. This is the same enthusiasm applied to a chapel.

It was given by Nicholas Brown Junior in memory of James Manning, the first president of the college, a Baptist minister who ran the place from seventeen sixty five and who taught the whole curriculum himself in the early years.

It has been the university's chapel, its library, and a lecture hall, and it is now the chapel again, used for services and meetings of many kinds. Look inside if the door is open: a single plain rectangular room with a gallery, entirely without decoration, which is Baptist as much as Greek.

Now step back and look at the three buildings in a row along this side of the green: Manning in the middle, and either side of it Hope College from eighteen twenty two and Slater Hall from eighteen seventy nine. Three buildings, three generations, and the university simply added them along the line as it could afford them. There is no master plan here of the sort McKim drew for Columbia. Brown's campus grew the way a village grows.

That is worth holding on to as you walk the rest of this, because it is Brown's whole character. The other seven campuses on this tour were, at some point, designed: someone drew a plan and the buildings were made to fit it. Brown was assembled.

One more thing about the Greek Revival, since you are standing in front of the best example on this tour. The style was not only about liberty and Byron. Accurate archaeology was new: Stuart and Revett's measured drawings of Athens, published from seventeen sixty two onward, gave builders in Rhode Island exact dimensions of buildings they would never see, and for the first time an American carpenter could reproduce a Greek order correctly rather than from a pattern book copy of a copy. The precision is part of the appeal. This is not a building in the Greek taste; it is a specific temple, measured, doubled and rebuilt in Providence.

Look at the material too. It is not marble; it is painted, and much of the Greek Revival in New England is wood and painted brick pretending to be stone, because stone was expensive and carpenters were skilled. Tap a column on a Greek Revival house in this state and it will often sound hollow.

Look at the pediment and the columns and notice what is missing: there is no sculpture in the pediment, no carving on the capitals beyond the plain Doric echinus, and no decoration on the frieze. That is partly cost and partly creed. This was built as a Baptist chapel for a denomination with a long suspicion of ornament in worship, so the plainest of the three Greek orders was the natural choice, and the building is austere inside for the same reason.

There is a practical note. Manning is used for services, meetings and student events, so its doors are open irregularly. If it is shut, the front is the thing anyway.

From here, walk east across the green toward the large Romanesque building with the round arched windows.
