This is Faunce House, now formally the Stephen Robert Campus Center, from nineteen oh four, with an arch through it onto the green, and it is where Brown students actually are.

It was built as a student union, six years after Penn invented the idea at Houston Hall, and it has been rebuilt and extended several times since. Inside is the Blue Room, which is the cafe everybody means when they say meet me at the Blue Room, along with common spaces, offices for student organisations, and a theatre.

Go through the arch and stand in it for a moment, looking out at the green. This is the doorway most students come through several times a day, and the arch frames the whole main green with University Hall on the far side.

Now, standing here, this is the place to explain the thing that makes Brown different from the other seven universities on this tour, because it is not architectural.

In nineteen sixty nine, Brown adopted what is called the New Curriculum, and it is still the most radical undergraduate structure in the Ivy League. It came out of a student proposal, written mainly by two undergraduates, Ira Magaziner and Elliot Maxwell, after a year of study, and the faculty adopted it.

Here is what it does. There are no distribution requirements. None. A Brown undergraduate does not have to take a science, or a language, or a quantitative course, or anything at all outside their concentration. There is no core curriculum of the kind Columbia is famous for. Students design their own course of study with an adviser, and may create their own concentration if the existing ones do not fit.

And any course may be taken satisfactory or no credit, which is pass or fail, with the failure simply not appearing. There are no grades of D or F on a Brown transcript: a course you fail is a course you did not take.

The argument for it is that students learn best what they chose freely, and that a person who is made to sit through a subject they resent has not learned it anyway. The argument against it is obvious and has been made continuously for fifty five years: that eighteen year olds do not know what they need, and that a degree should mean something specific.

What is not in dispute is that it changed who applies here. Brown draws students who want that freedom, and it has a reputation for the creative and the self directed, and the university's own culture, its politics, and its relationship with its students all follow from a decision made in this building's decade by undergraduates who wrote a paper.

There is one more Brown particularity worth knowing while you stand here. The university has no fraternity houses on the green and a comparatively small Greek system, and its residential life is arranged around dormitories and programme houses rather than clubs. That, and the curriculum, and the fact that the campus is stitched into a residential neighbourhood rather than walled off, all pull the same way.

One more consequence of the New Curriculum worth knowing, because it is measurable rather than atmospheric. Because there are no distribution requirements, Brown students take a noticeably higher proportion of their courses in departments outside their concentration than students at universities that mandate breadth, which is the opposite of what the critics predicted. The university publishes the figures. Whether that is because free choice works or because the sort of person who applies to Brown was always going to wander is not a question the data can settle.

There is one more thing about nineteen sixty nine at Brown, and it happened a few months before the curriculum was adopted. In December nineteen sixty eight, the Black students of Brown and of Pembroke, the women's college, walked out of the university altogether, about sixty five of them, in protest at the tiny number of Black students being admitted. They stayed away and negotiated. The university agreed to raise Black enrolment substantially and to fund it. Brown's Black student population went from a handful to a real presence within a few years as a direct result.

So two of the things that define this university, the curriculum and the composition of its student body, were both changed by students within twelve months of each other, and both changes stuck.

Come out of the arch and walk east, down the hill a little, toward the tall concrete building.
