This last stop is not a building but a street, and it is two minutes from the gates, and it is the reason many people come to Providence at all.

Walk out of the Van Wickle Gates, down College Street, and take the first proper turning to your right. You are on Benefit Street, and you are standing in a mile of eighteenth and early nineteenth century houses, one after another, on both sides, running along the side of the hill.

There are more than two hundred of them. Colonial, Federal, Greek Revival, in wood and brick, painted in the colours of their period, with fanlights over the doors and granite steps up from the pavement. It is one of the densest concentrations of original colonial architecture in America, and unlike the reconstructions at other historic sites, these are the real houses, lived in continuously.

Here is how it survived, and it is a lesson about cities. Providence was rich in the eighteenth century, on shipping and the China trade and, honestly, on slaving voyages. Then in the nineteenth century the money moved: the mills went up the river valleys, the commercial centre moved downhill, and College Hill became a backwater. Nobody had a reason to knock these houses down and build something bigger, so they simply stayed, subdividing into cheap flats and slowly rotting.

By the nineteen fifties they were slums, and the city planned to clear a good deal of the hill. What stopped it was the Providence Preservation Society, founded in nineteen fifty six, largely by women of the neighbourhood, who fought the plan, raised money, and pioneered a method: identify the historic buildings, get the area surveyed, buy the worst houses, restore and resell them with covenants. The College Hill study they produced in nineteen fifty nine became a national model, and it is one of the founding documents of the American preservation movement.

So the street you are standing on is not a museum. It is a working residential street that was saved by its residents at the moment American cities were demolishing their old centres wholesale.

Walk a few hundred yards in either direction. Look at the doorways, which are the best thing: the Providence door surround, with its columns and its fanlight, is a local speciality and no two are identical. Look at the granite kerbs and steps, cut from local stone.

The John Brown House, which belonged to the slave trading brother from the Sayles Hall story, is on Power Street two blocks south, and it is a museum and it does not hide what paid for it. The First Baptist Church, where the commencement procession ends, is at the north end. The RISD Museum is at the bottom of the hill and it is free on Sundays and it is genuinely one of the best small art museums in the country.

H. P. Lovecraft walked these streets at night and put them in his fiction almost unchanged, and his grave is in Swan Point Cemetery to the north east.

One more practical thing about walking this street. Benefit Street runs along the contour of the hill, so it is flat, and the streets crossing it are extremely steep. If you walk down to the river and want to come back up, choose your street: some of them are close to a staircase. The easiest return is by College Street, the one you came down, which is steep but straight.

One more thing about this street and the university above it, because the two are not separate. A great many of these houses are owned by Brown and by the Rhode Island School of Design and used as offices, seminar rooms and faculty housing, which is part of why they are maintained. A colonial house is expensive to keep and an institution with an endowment can do it where a family often cannot. The preservation of College Hill is partly a story about neighbours organising and partly a story about two universities needing space and buying old buildings instead of building new ones.

Whether that is preservation or a slow institutional takeover of a neighbourhood is an argument you can hear in Providence at any dinner table.

That is the end of the walk. You have gone from a gate that opens twice a year, across a green that grew by accretion rather than plan, past a library of the first books written about the Americas and a report about where the money came from, to a street that survived because it was too poor to be worth destroying. Thank you for walking it with me.
