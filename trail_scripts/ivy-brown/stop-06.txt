Before you look up at it, notice where you are: this is the corner of the green nearest the town, and the tower was put here rather than in the middle of the campus because it was meant to be seen from the street. A private memorial placed at the public edge of a university is making a point about who it is for.

This is Carrie Tower, from nineteen oh four, and it is a hundred and ten feet of brick and white marble on the corner of the green, with a clock in it, and it is a love letter.

Read the inscription at the base. It says: Love is strong as death. That is from the Song of Songs.

The story is this. Carrie Mathilde Brown was a granddaughter of Nicholas Brown Junior, the man the university is named for. She married a man named Paul Bajnotti, an Italian diplomat from Turin, and she died in eighteen ninety two. Bajnotti, her widower, spent a great deal of money in Providence commemorating her. He built this tower at her family's university. And down the hill in the middle of the city, in Burnside Park, he built the Bajnotti Fountain, also for her.

So there are two monuments to the same woman in this city, put up by a grieving Italian husband a hundred and thirty years ago, and the university one has a clock in it and tells the time to students hurrying to class.

Look at the tower itself. It is Italian in feeling, a campanile, with the brick laid in bands and marble trim, and a clock face high up. The clock ran for most of the twentieth century, stopped, and was restored.

Now, this is a good place to stop for a moment on the corner and take in the shape of this campus, because you have now seen most of it.

Brown sits on top of College Hill, which rises steeply from the Providence River below. The main green is behind you, the older buildings around it, and the campus spreads north and east into the residential streets of the hill in a way that has no edge: university buildings, then a house that is a department, then an ordinary house, then another department. Brown owns a great many nineteenth century houses on this hill and uses them as offices and centres, which is why the place feels domestic.

That hill is also the reason Providence has one of the best preserved colonial neighbourhoods in America. Benefit Street, two blocks west and downhill from here, is a mile of eighteenth and early nineteenth century houses standing in a row, and it survived because the city's money moved elsewhere in the nineteenth century and nobody bothered to redevelop it. It was nearly lost in the nineteen fifties and was saved by a preservation society founded by women of the neighbourhood. If you have an hour after this walk, Benefit Street is what to do with it.

There is a second reason to stand at this corner. Look at how the university simply stops and the neighbourhood begins. There is no wall here, no gate, no line on the ground. A university building, then a nineteenth century wooden house that is a department, then a house that is a house. Brown owns and uses dozens of these, and the effect is that the campus has no edge at all on this side, which is unlike every other campus on this tour except Penn.

That is partly philosophy and mostly history: the college simply expanded into whatever came up for sale on the hill around it, one house at a time, for two hundred years.

One more thing about Bajnotti, because the story has an ending. He outlived his wife by more than thirty years, spent much of that time in Providence and Turin, and left money for the upkeep of both monuments. The fountain downtown was restored in the nineteen nineties and the tower here has been repaired several times, most recently after long neglect when the clock had stopped and the brick was in poor condition.

A private grief from eighteen ninety two is now two pieces of public property that a city has to maintain, which is the fate of most memorials and is a reasonable definition of what a memorial is for.

H. P. Lovecraft lived on this hill and set most of his fiction in these streets, and his papers are in the John Hay Library, which is the next stop, fifty yards from here.
