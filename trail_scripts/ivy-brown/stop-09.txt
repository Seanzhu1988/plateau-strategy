You have walked downhill to get here, and that is worth noticing, because the campus falls away eastward from the green and the newer buildings are mostly on the lower ground. Universities put their old buildings on the high ground and their laboratories where the land was cheaper, and you can read the growth of almost any campus by walking downhill from its oldest quadrangle.

This is the Sciences Library, from nineteen seventy one, and it is fourteen storeys of raw concrete standing on top of College Hill, and it is the tallest building at Brown and the least loved and the most photographed from a distance.

Students call it the SciLi. It is Brutalist, which means concrete left as it came out of the formwork, with the structure showing, and it was designed by Warner Burns Toan and Lunde. It is a tower rather than a block because the site is small and the collection is large, and the floors are stacked with the stacks on the upper levels and study space below.

Here is why it deserves a stop on a walk full of eighteenth century brick.

Every campus on this tour has one building from the nineteen sixties or seventies that its own students were taught to be embarrassed by. Harvard has the Science Center. Yale has the Art and Architecture Building. Princeton has the modern insertion in Whig Hall. And every one of those buildings is now, fifty years on, being reassessed by architectural historians as a serious work of a serious period, while the people who use them daily either love them or would still cheerfully demolish them.

Concrete has aged badly in the American climate, which does not help. It streaks and stains and it looks worse in the rain than brick does, and a great deal of Brutalist architecture has been knocked down in the last twenty years, faster than any other twentieth century style.

The SciLi has a particular local fame though, and it is worth knowing. The eleventh floor, which is quiet and high, is where a great deal of Brown's serious studying gets done, and there is a strong tradition attached to it that involves the last night before graduation and will not be described further here. Ask a student.

The other thing about this building is the view. It is the tallest thing on this hill, and from the upper floors you look west across the whole of Providence: the State House dome, the two rivers, the bay beyond. The floors are card access, so most visitors will not get up there, but if you are with somebody who is at Brown, ask.

Look at the ground floor instead, which was renovated to add a cafe and open study space, and which is the current architectural fashion applied to a building of the previous one.

Now turn around and look west, back up the hill toward the green you came from. In about half a mile you have crossed the whole of the old campus.

There is a practical note for anyone who cares about the architecture. The best view of this building is not from underneath it, where it is simply large, but from down the hill to the west, where it stands against the sky above the colonial roofs of Benefit Street. That juxtaposition, a raw concrete tower over a street of eighteenth century clapboard, is either the worst thing that happened to this hill or the most honest picture of what a university is, and Providence has been arguing about it since nineteen seventy one.

There is one more thing about this building that is genuinely interesting rather than architectural gossip. It was built with the stacks on the upper floors and the readers below, which is the reverse of the usual arrangement and was done because the structural engineering favoured putting the heavy load high on a small footprint. Books are extraordinarily heavy: a floor of full shelving weighs several times what an office floor is designed for, and library engineering is mostly the problem of holding up paper.

That is why the tower is the shape it is, and why the lower floors, which carry less, could be opened up into the glass fronted study space you can see from the street.

From here, walk back and north west toward the corner of the campus at the top of Waterman Street, where there is a gate with a name on it.
