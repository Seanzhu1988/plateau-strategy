Stand back from it before you read the names, and look at what a nineteen twenty one memorial gate looks like: restrained, classical, in brick and limestone to match the college rather than in the heroic bronze that an earlier generation would have used. The First World War changed how these things were built. After eighteen sixty five you got a soldier on a plinth; after nineteen eighteen you got a list of names and an arch, because the scale of the loss made individual heroics look wrong.

This is Soldiers Memorial Gate, from nineteen twenty one, at the head of Lincoln Field, and it is the memorial to the Brown men who died in the First World War.

It is brick and limestone, a wide arch with two smaller openings, and the names are cut into it. Read a few. Forty two Brown men died in that war, out of a university that then had well under a thousand students, and the names include undergraduates who left in nineteen seventeen and never came back.

The gate stands at the top of a slope looking down over Lincoln Field, which is the university's old athletic ground and is now a green space with buildings around it, so the memorial has an open view rather than being wedged between walls, which was the intention.

You have now met the same memorial at four universities on this tour, and it is worth saying what that repetition means. Harvard's is a transept in a Victorian hall with the Union dead of the Civil War. Yale's is a marble rotunda that names its German dead as well. Columbia has its war memorials, Princeton has a memorial room in Nassau Hall, and this is Brown's. Every one of these institutions is, among other things, a place that sent its young men to be killed and then carved their names in stone and made the survivors walk past them every day. That is not a small part of what an old university is.

Now, the last thing about Brown, and it happens right where you are about to walk.

Brown's commencement does not happen on a lawn. On the morning of commencement, the graduating class assembles at the top of College Hill, the Van Wickle Gates are opened outward, and the whole procession walks down through them and down College Street, through the city, to the First Baptist Church in America at the bottom of the hill, where the degrees are conferred. It is the oldest Baptist congregation in the country, founded by Roger Williams in sixteen thirty eight, in a meeting house from seventeen seventy five, and Brown has held its commencement there since the eighteenth century.

So the university's most important ceremony is a walk down a public street into a church that is older than the college and that belongs to the city, not the university, and thousands of Providence residents line the street to watch.

That is a good place to end. Walk back to the gates, and this time you may go through the middle of them, because the rule binds students and not visitors, and look down College Street at the State House dome and think about four hundred graduates walking down it in the rain in May.

One more thing about Lincoln Field, which is the slope below the gate. It was the athletic ground, and Brown played the first ever intercollegiate ice hockey game in the United States, and it fielded one of the earliest football teams; Fritz Pollard, who played here, became the first Black player in the Rose Bowl in nineteen sixteen and later the first Black head coach in the National Football League. There is a plaque to him on this campus.

There is one more thing to notice about this gate, and it separates it from the others. Harvard's memorial is inside a building, Yale's is inside a rotunda, Princeton's is a room. Brown put its names on a gate that people walk through, outdoors, in the weather, on the way to a playing field. A memorial you pass under is a different kind of object from one you visit, and it was a deliberate choice by a university that has never had much appetite for interiors of state.

Read the dates on it as well as the names. Some of these men died in nineteen eighteen, weeks before the armistice.

Benefit Street, with its mile of colonial houses, is two blocks down on your right. The RISD Museum, which is the art school next door and one of the best small museums in the country, is at the bottom of the hill. Thank you for walking it with me.
