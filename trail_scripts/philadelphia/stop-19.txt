Walk north from the museum along Twenty Second Street, through a neighbourhood of small row houses and corner bars, and at Fairmount Avenue a wall rises across the whole block: thirty feet of grey stone, with towers at the corners and a gate like a castle's. This is Eastern State Penitentiary, and when it opened in eighteen twenty nine it was the most expensive building in the country and the most famous prison in the world. It closed in nineteen seventy one and was left to rot, and it is kept now exactly as it was found, a ruin with the roofs held up, and it is the best hour of the whole walk.

Here is what it was for. The Quakers of Philadelphia believed that a criminal could be reformed, and that the way to do it was silence and solitude: a cell, a Bible, a skylight, work to do, and no contact with any other prisoner, ever, so that a man would be alone with his conscience and would become penitent. That is where the word penitentiary comes from. It was invented here. The building was designed to make it possible: seven long cellblocks radiating from a central hub like the spokes of a wheel, so that one guard at the centre could see down every corridor, and each cell with its own small exercise yard and a door so low that a prisoner had to bow to enter it. Prisoners wore hoods when they were moved so they would never see a face. The plan was copied by three hundred prisons on six continents.

It did not work. Charles Dickens came in eighteen forty two, on his tour of America, and wrote that the silent system was a slow and daily tampering with the mysteries of the brain, and immeasurably worse than any torture of the body. He was right, and the prison gave up on solitary in nineteen thirteen and became an ordinary crowded jail, and stayed one for sixty years. But the system it invented, the cell, the solitary confinement, the idea that a prison should reform rather than simply punish, is still the system, and every argument about it starts at this gate.

Go in, and take the audio tour, which is included and narrated by Steve Buscemi with the voices of former guards and prisoners, and walk the cellblocks. The early ones are the ones to see: the vaulted corridors with the skylights, the cells with their arched ceilings and the one round window in the roof, the eye of God. The plaster is falling, the paint is gone, there are trees growing in the exercise yards, and the museum has decided, rightly, not to tidy any of it.

Two cells will have a crowd. One is Al Capone's. He served eight months here in nineteen twenty nine and thirty, for carrying a gun in Philadelphia, which was a convenient way of being somewhere safe during a gang war, and his cell has been furnished as it was reported to be: a carpet, a desk, a radio, oil paintings, a cabinet. The other is the cell of Pep, a dog, sentenced in nineteen twenty four to life for killing the governor's wife's cat, or so the story went; the truth is that the governor gave the dog to the prison as a mascot. His mugshot is real.

Here is the thing to do, and it is not comfortable. Find an empty cell in one of the old blocks, one nobody is in, and step inside, and stand for one minute with the door closed behind you. There is the skylight, and the walls, and nothing else. A man was put in here for years with a Bible he might not be able to read and no voice he could answer. One minute is enough to understand the argument.

There is a paid ticket, and the site is open every day. It is the last stop, and it is the right one, because this walk began with a bell that says proclaim liberty to all the inhabitants and ends with the building where the country worked out what to do with the ones it did not mean.

From the gate, the museum is ten minutes back down the hill, the Parkway bus runs from the museum to City Hall, and the old city, where you started, is a subway ride from there. Thank you for walking it with me.
