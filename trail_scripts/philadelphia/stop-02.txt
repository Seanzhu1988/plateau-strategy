Walk in past the exhibition and go straight to the end, where the crowd is, and stand where you can see the bell and the window behind it at the same time. The window is not decoration. It frames the tower of Independence Hall, which is where this bell hung for ninety years, and the people who built this room in two thousand and three put the bell exactly here so that you would see the two together.

Now the bell. It was cast in London, at the Whitechapel foundry, in seventeen fifty two, ordered by the Pennsylvania Assembly for the new State House tower. The Assembly chose the words themselves, from the Book of Leviticus: proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof. Read the line on the bell. Then read the next line, and notice that the colony's name is spelt with one n, Pensylvania. That is not a mistake. It was an accepted spelling at the time, and it is on the Constitution too.

The first time they rang it, it cracked. So two Philadelphia founders, John Pass and John Stow, broke it up and recast it, and added copper to make it tougher, and the second bell sounded so bad that they broke it up and cast it a third time. The bell in front of you is that third casting, seventeen fifty three, Pass and Stow, their names on the front, and it weighs about two thousand and eighty pounds. It hung in the tower and rang for the Assembly, for proclamations, for funerals, and, on the eighth of July seventeen seventy six, to call people to the yard of the State House to hear the Declaration read out loud for the first time. It did not ring on the fourth. Nobody outside the room knew about the Declaration on the fourth.

Look at the crack. There are two, and people confuse them. The wide split that runs up from the rim, the one in every photograph, is not the crack that silenced the bell. That is a repair. In eighteen forty six the city wanted the bell to ring for Washington's birthday, and it had a thin crack by then, so they drilled it out into that wide channel to stop the edges of the crack from buzzing against each other. It worked for a few hours. Then a new hairline ran up from the top of the repair toward the crown, and that thin line, the one you have to look for, is the crack that ended it. It has not rung since.

So why is a bell that does not ring the most famous object in the city? Because of the words on it, and because of who read them. In the eighteen thirties the anti-slavery movement took the line from Leviticus and made this bell their symbol. They called it the Liberty Bell. Before that it was just the State House bell. The name came from people who read proclaim liberty to all the inhabitants and pointed out that the country had not. After the Civil War it went on the road, seven long railway tours between eighteen eighty five and nineteen fifteen, to New Orleans, to Chicago, to San Francisco, stopping in hundreds of towns, and every town turned out. Suffragists made their own copy, with the clapper chained, and said they would not ring it until women could vote. They rang it in nineteen twenty.

It came home from the last tour weaker than it left, and it has not travelled since. For most of the twentieth century it stood in the Hall itself. In nineteen seventy six it moved across the street to a glass pavilion for the bicentennial, and in two thousand and three it moved again, to this room, which was built around it.

Here is the thing to do. Get close, as close as the rope allows, and look at the surface of the metal, not the crack. You will see the seams and the roughness of an eighteenth century casting, the letters slightly uneven, the whole thing a little lopsided. It is a working object that a couple of local founders made in a yard, and it was never meant to be looked at. That is the point of it. The country's most famous symbol is a piece of municipal equipment that broke.

There is no ticket for this room and no time limit, only the security line at the door, which is shortest early and late. If you come back at the end of the day, when the tour groups have gone, you can stand here almost alone, with the tower in the window turning gold.
