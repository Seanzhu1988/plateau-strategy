One block east of Independence Hall, on the south side of Chestnut Street, a Greek temple stands between the brick houses, and it is worth stopping on the pavement across the road before you cross, because from there it is at its best. Eight Doric columns, a plain triangular pediment, white marble gone soft grey, and not one thing on the front of it that a Greek two thousand years earlier would not have recognised. This is the Second Bank of the United States, and it was the most fashionable building in America the year it opened.

The architect was William Strickland, a Philadelphian who had never been to Greece and worked from the engravings in a book. He won the competition in eighteen eighteen with a design copied, quite openly, from the Parthenon, and it went up between then and eighteen twenty four in Pennsylvania blue marble from a quarry up the river. Go and stand at the foot of the columns. They are fluted, they are heavy, and the marble is soft enough that the flutes have weathered to something like driftwood. Strickland went on to build the steeple on Independence Hall, and then the Merchants' Exchange round the corner, and he made this city the first place in the country where Greek Revival was the style, before it spread to every courthouse and bank in the South.

Now the bank. The Second Bank was the national bank, chartered by Congress in eighteen sixteen for twenty years, holding the government's money, issuing notes, and lending, with branches across the country, and it was the most powerful financial institution in the hemisphere. Its president from eighteen twenty three was Nicholas Biddle, a Philadelphia aristocrat who had been to Greece and loved the building. And its enemy was Andrew Jackson. Jackson believed a national bank was a monster that served the rich, and when Congress voted to renew the charter early, in eighteen thirty two, he vetoed it, and then won re-election on the veto, and then pulled the government's money out. The Bank War was the great political fight of that decade, and the bank lost. The charter expired in eighteen thirty six. Biddle tried to keep it going as a state bank and it failed. The building became the Custom House for the port of Philadelphia and stayed one for ninety years.

Which is why you are here, because the Park Service has filled it with faces. Inside is the Portrait Gallery, and the portraits are the ones you already half know, because they are the originals of the paintings on the money and in the schoolbooks: Charles Willson Peale's portraits of the men and a few women of the Revolution, painted from life in this city in the seventeen seventies and eighties. Peale was a painter, a soldier, a naturalist and a showman who opened the first real museum in the country, upstairs in the State House, and he painted everyone who came through Philadelphia. Washington, several times. Jefferson. Hamilton. Franklin. Lafayette. Native leaders who came to treat with Congress. Go in and walk the rooms slowly and look at the eyes.

Here is what to notice. These are not the faces on the money. The money faces were painted later, by Gilbert Stuart and others, when the men were old and famous and knew they were being painted for posterity. Peale painted them in the middle of the war, in their forties, tired, some of them plainly frightened, and he painted them because he knew them. His Washington is a big, ruddy, slightly awkward man who looks as if he would rather be on a horse. His Jefferson is thin and red haired and not yet sure of himself. They are portraits of people, not of monuments, and after the monuments outside they are a relief.

The gallery is free, and it is one of the least visited buildings in the district, so you will have the rooms to yourself, and you can stand as long as you like in front of a face without anyone waiting behind you. Its hours have changed often in the last few years; if the door is shut, the columns are still the point, and the next stop is a hundred yards further east.
