Walk up Second Street and the white steeple comes into view above the roofs before the church does. Stop when you can see the whole of it, tower and spire, and take it in, because for over half a century this was the tallest thing in North America. Christ Church was built between seventeen twenty seven and seventeen forty four, Georgian, red brick, with big arched windows and a great Palladian window at the east end, and it was the finest church in the colonies when it was done. The steeple came later, in seventeen fifty four, and it was paid for by a lottery, which Benjamin Franklin organised, because he wanted a tall place to try out his ideas about lightning rods. It is a hundred and ninety six feet to the top, and nothing in the country stood higher until eighteen ten.

This was the church of the Revolution, in the plain sense that the people who made the Revolution sat in it. Go inside, and the pews are still boxed, with doors, and the doors carry the names. George Washington rented a pew here through the years he was President in this city, and it is marked. So is the pew of Benjamin Franklin, who was not a great churchgoer but kept his seat. Betsy Ross was a member, and so was Robert Morris, the financier, and Benjamin Rush, the doctor, and Francis Hopkinson, who designed the flag, whatever the Ross story says. Fifteen signers of the Declaration worshipped here at one time or another, and seven of them are buried in the church or its burial ground.

Look at the pulpit. It is the original, from seventeen seventy, a wine glass pulpit on a single stem with a sounding board over it, and from it, in the seventeen eighties, the Reverend William White preached to a congregation that had just fought a war against the King whose church this was. That was the problem. Christ Church was Church of England, its clergy had sworn loyalty to the Crown, and after independence it had no bishops and no way to make any, because bishops were made in England. White went to London in seventeen eighty seven and persuaded the Archbishop of Canterbury to consecrate him anyway, and came back the first Bishop of Pennsylvania, and the American Episcopal Church, which is the Church of England with the King taken out, was organised in this building. He was the rector here for sixty years. His house is a few streets south, restored, with his library in it.

Then go out and walk two blocks west to the corner of Fifth and Arch, because the burial ground is not next to the church. The congregation ran out of room in the churchyard in seventeen nineteen and bought a field at the edge of town, and the field is now in the middle of the city behind a brick wall with a gap in it. Look through the gap. The flat stone just inside, worn smooth, covered in pennies, is Benjamin Franklin's grave. He lies beside his wife Deborah, under a stone that says only their names and the year, which is what he asked for; the famous epitaph he wrote as a young man, about the body of a printer being like the cover of an old book, is not on it. The pennies are a Philadelphia habit, from his line that a penny saved is a penny earned, and the church collects thousands of dollars a year in them and uses the money to repair the stone the pennies are wearing away.

Four other signers are in that ground, and the first three Bishops, and the Bache and the Rush families, and a great many people who died in the yellow fever of seventeen ninety three, which killed one in ten of the city in three months while the government fled to the countryside.

The church asks for a donation and the burial ground charges a small fee, both of which go to keeping the buildings up, and both are worth it. Then come back to Arch Street, because the next two stops are on it, and the first is a small house with a big story.
