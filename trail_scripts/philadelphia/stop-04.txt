Turn to the west of Independence Hall, the right hand side as you face it from Chestnut Street, and the smaller brick building at the corner of Sixth is Congress Hall. It was built as a county courthouse between seventeen eighty seven and seventeen eighty nine, and it had been a courthouse for about a year when the federal government arrived in Philadelphia and asked to borrow it. It lent it for a decade. From seventeen ninety to eighteen hundred, this was the Capitol of the United States.

Go in with the ranger, and the ground floor is the House of Representatives. Look at the size of it. When Congress moved in there were sixty five representatives, and by the end of the decade a hundred and six, and they sat here in rows on plain chairs with a fireplace at each end, and the public sat in the gallery you can see above you. This is where the first Congress finished the work of the first Congress: the Bill of Rights was ratified by the states while the House sat in this room, and the first census was counted, and the First Bank of the United States was chartered, and the states of Vermont, Kentucky and Tennessee were admitted to the Union, one after another, by votes taken here.

Then go up the stairs to the Senate. It is the finer room, and it was meant to be. The Senate was the upper house and it got the upper floor, with the carpet, the mahogany desks and the canopy over the presiding officer's chair. The carpet is a reproduction of the one that was here, with the seal of the United States in the middle and the thirteen stars around it, and it is worth a look at the eagle, because the country was still working out what its eagle looked like.

Two inaugurations happened in this building. On the fourth of March seventeen ninety three, George Washington took the oath for his second term in the Senate chamber, upstairs, and gave the shortest inaugural address in American history, a hundred and thirty five words, which is about the length of the paragraph you just heard. And on the fourth of March seventeen ninety seven, John Adams took the oath in the House chamber, downstairs, with Washington standing beside him, and that was the first time in modern history that power passed from one elected head of state to another, peacefully, in front of a crowd, with the man giving it up watching. Adams wrote to his wife that night that Washington looked, he thought, as though he was enjoying it, as if to say: I am fairly out and you are fairly in, and see which of us will be happiest.

Here is the thing to picture while you stand in the House chamber. The room was not solemn. It was hot in summer and cold in winter, the members shouted, the gallery shouted back, and the two parties that were forming, the Federalists around Hamilton and the Republicans around Jefferson, hated each other in a way that would be familiar to anyone who reads a newspaper. In seventeen ninety eight, in this room, a Vermont congressman spat in a Connecticut congressman's face, and the Connecticut man came back the next day with a cane, and the Vermont man defended himself with the fire tongs from that fireplace, and the House spent the next two weeks debating whether to expel them both. It did not.

The building went back to being a courthouse in eighteen hundred, when the government moved to Washington, and it was a courthouse for most of the next century, altered and mistreated until the city and then the Park Service put it back the way it had been. The rooms you see were restored in the nineteen sixties, using the records of what had been bought for them in the seventeen nineties.

No ticket is needed here. Rangers take groups through when the building is open, and the tour is short. When you come out, walk back past the front of Independence Hall to the building on the other side, the twin of this one, at the corner of Fifth. That is Old City Hall, and it was the third branch.
