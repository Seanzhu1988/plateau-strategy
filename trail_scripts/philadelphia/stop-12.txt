Turn off Second Street between Arch and Race, into a lane so narrow that a car can just fit, and stop at the entrance, because this is a place to see all at once before you walk it. Thirty two houses, brick, two and three storeys, shoulder to shoulder on both sides of a cobbled lane a block long, with shutters and boot scrapers and flower boxes and a church steeple at the far end. This is Elfreth's Alley. People have lived on it continuously since seventeen oh three, which makes it the oldest residential street in the country, and they live on it now. Thirty of these houses are private homes.

Walk slowly, then. This is somebody's front step.

The alley was cut through in seventeen oh two by two landowners who each gave up a strip so that a lane could run down to the river. It is named for Jeremiah Elfreth, a blacksmith who owned houses on it in the seventeen thirties and forties and rented them out. The houses you see were built between seventeen twenty eight and eighteen thirty six, and the people who lived in them were the people the grand houses depended on: shipwrights, sailmakers, a pewterer, a glassblower, dressmakers, tailors, a dozen sea captains, and a great many women who ran businesses of their own from the front room. In the seventeen nineties two dressmakers, Mary Smith and Sarah Melton, lived at numbers one twenty four and one twenty six, and those two houses are the museum now.

Look at the houses as you pass. The oldest are the smallest, two storeys with a dormer in the roof, one room wide and two deep, what Philadelphia called a trinity, for the three floors, one room on each. The later ones, from the seventeen nineties on, are three storeys and a little wider. Look for the pent eaves, the small sloping roofs over the ground floor windows that throw the rain off, which are a Philadelphia habit from the Quaker builders who did not like ornament and liked keeping dry. And look up at the second floor windows for the busybodies. A busybody is a pair of mirrors on an iron arm, angled so that a woman sitting upstairs could see who was at her front door without going down, and could see the whole street too. Several are still there. They were the security cameras of seventeen ninety.

The alley very nearly went. By the nineteen thirties it was a slum, the houses subdivided and rotting, the river end industrial, and the city wanted it cleared. A group of residents and neighbours formed an association in nineteen thirty four to buy the worst houses and save the street, and they are still the owners of the museum houses and the reason the rest survived. Preservation in this country, as a thing ordinary people do rather than a thing governments do, started in a few places, and this lane is one of them.

Go into the museum if it is open. It is two of the smallest houses, kept as they were, and the tour is ten minutes and tells you how a dressmaker lived. Then walk to the far end and look back. The steeple you see over the roofs is Christ Church, where you have just been; the sea captains who lived here could see it from their doors and set their clocks by its bell.

Here is the thing to do. Pick one house, any house, and stand in front of it and count the generations. Three hundred years. Fifteen or so families, one after another, in one small brick box, cooking and sleeping and dying and being born, and the box is still here and still lived in. There is nothing else on this walk that is as old and as ordinary at the same time.

Keep your voice down, keep off the steps, and do not photograph through the windows; people are inside. Then come back out to Second Street and walk west along Arch Street, past the burial ground again, to the north end of Independence Mall, where the last building of the old city is the newest.
