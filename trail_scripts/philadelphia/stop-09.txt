Between Third and Fourth on Market Street there is a brick archway in the row of shopfronts, and if you did not know it was there you would walk past it. Go through. It opens into a courtyard, and in the middle of the courtyard stands a house made of nothing: a frame of white painted steel, drawn in the air, the outline of a house with no walls and no roof. This was Benjamin Franklin's house, and this is what is left of it.

He built it between seventeen sixty three and sixty five, a good brick house of three storeys set back from Market Street in its own court, with the printing shop and his grandson's newspaper office fronting the street. He was in London for most of the building and wrote home to his wife Deborah with instructions and complaints, and then he was in Paris, and by the time he came home for good, in seventeen eighty five, he was seventy nine and had lived in the house for only a few years in total. He died in it, upstairs, in April seventeen ninety, and twenty thousand people came to the funeral, which was most of the city.

His grandchildren pulled the house down in eighteen twelve. It was in the way of a street they wanted to open, and it was worth more as building lots, and nobody had thought to draw it properly. So when the Park Service came to this courtyard for the bicentennial in nineteen seventy six, they had the foundations, a few letters, and no picture of the house. Rather than build a guess, they asked Robert Venturi, the Philadelphia architect who was then the most talked about in the country, and he gave them this. The steel frame follows the known dimensions exactly and pretends to nothing else. It is one of the best pieces of modern architecture in the city, and it is a house that says, honestly, we do not know what I looked like.

Stand inside the frame. Then look down. There are concrete portals in the paving with glass tops, and through them you can see the real foundations, the cellar walls and the privy pit that the archaeologists found. Franklin is under your feet, in the only sense that is true; his grave is at Christ Church burial ground, a few streets north.

The museum is underground, entered from the courtyard, and it was rebuilt in twenty thirteen. It is about the man rather than the house, and it is good on the things people forget: that he was a runaway apprentice at seventeen who arrived in this city with a few coins; that the printing shop made him rich enough to retire at forty two; that he then spent forty years as a scientist, a postmaster, a diplomat and a politician and never really stopped being a printer. There is a paid ticket for it. The rest of the court is free.

And the rest of the court is where the fun is. On the Market Street side, in the reconstructed houses that Franklin built as rentals, there is a working eighteenth century print shop, where a ranger sets type and pulls a sheet on a wooden press exactly as his shop did, and it smells of ink. Next door is the office of the Aurora, the newspaper his grandson Benjamin Franklin Bache ran, which spent the seventeen nineties calling George Washington a tyrant in print. And at the corner is the post office. Franklin was the colonies' postmaster before there was a country, and the first Postmaster General after, and this branch is the only post office in the United States that does not fly the flag, because there was no flag when he ran it. It is a real post office. Buy a stamp and have it cancelled by hand with his signature, B. Free Franklin, which is how he franked his own mail.

Here is the thing to do before you go. Go back into the frame, find the spot where the front door would have been, and look out through it toward Market Street, through the arch, to the traffic. That is the view he had. The city has changed entirely and the view has not, and for two minutes you can stand in his doorway.

Christ Church, where he sat on Sundays and where he is buried, is the next stop, three short blocks north on Second Street.
