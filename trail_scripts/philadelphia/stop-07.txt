Turn off Chestnut Street into the little courtyard between the buildings, and the small, elegant brick hall at the end of it, set back from the street with a cupola on the roof, is Carpenters' Hall. Everyone walks past it. It is the reason the whole district exists, and it is not even a government building. It belongs, today, to the same organisation that built it, the Carpenters' Company of the City and County of Philadelphia, founded in seventeen twenty four, a guild of master builders, and they have owned it without a break for two hundred and fifty years.

They built it between seventeen seventy and seventeen seventy four, to a design by one of their own, Robert Smith, who was the best builder in the colonies and had put up the steeple on Christ Church that you will see later. It was their meeting hall, their office, and, because they were the men who actually made buildings, the place where the price of building in Philadelphia was fixed. The Carpenters' Company kept a secret book of prices, so that every master charged the same, and if you wanted a house built you paid the book. Look at the hall as a piece of work: the brick is laid in Flemish bond, alternating the long face and the short, and the Palladian window in the pediment is as fine as anything in the city. It was a builders' advertisement.

And then, in September seventeen seventy four, the delegates arrived. Twelve of the thirteen colonies had agreed to send men to Philadelphia to decide what to do about the closing of the port of Boston, and the obvious place to meet was the State House, one block west. But the State House belonged to the colony of Pennsylvania, whose Assembly was nervous about the whole business and might have said no. So the delegates asked the carpenters, and the carpenters said yes, and on the fifth of September seventeen seventy four the First Continental Congress sat down in the room on the ground floor of this building. Fifty six men, Washington among them, John Adams and Samuel Adams, Patrick Henry, John Jay, and they met here for seven weeks.

Go inside. It is free, and the ground floor room is the one they used, with the chairs and the tables. Stand in the middle of it. What they did in this room was agree to stop trading with Britain, write a petition to the King, and agree to meet again the following spring if nothing changed. Nothing changed, and when they met again, in the State House, it was the Second Continental Congress, and there was a war on. But the choice to act together was made here, by men from twelve colonies who had mostly never met and did not much like each other, and it was made in a room lent to them by a guild of builders because nobody else would lend them one.

Upstairs, at the time, was the Library Company, Franklin's subscription library, which rented the second floor, and the delegates used its books. The room is not open, but it is worth knowing that the first Congress met with a library over its head.

Two other things happened in this building that a guide should mention. In seventeen seventy seven and seventy eight, when the British held the city, they used the hall as a hospital, and the carpenters got it back with the floors ruined. And in seventeen eighty one the Bank of North America, the first bank in the country, opened in this building, because Robert Morris, who was paying for the war out of his own pocket and other people's, needed somewhere to put the money. Franklin's library, the first Congress, and the first bank, in one small building owned by tradesmen. Philadelphia in three floors.

It is closed on Mondays, and on Tuesdays in winter, so check before you count on it. When you come out, walk on down to Third Street. The columned marble building on the corner is the First Bank of the United States, Hamilton's bank, older than the Second and plainer, and beyond it, at the end of the block, is the museum.
