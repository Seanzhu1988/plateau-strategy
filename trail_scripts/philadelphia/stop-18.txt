Stand at the bottom of the steps, on the wide oval where the traffic circles, and look up. The building at the top is the Philadelphia Museum of Art, three Greek temples in golden Minnesota stone joined into one, on a hill called Faircount that used to hold the city's reservoir, at the end of the Parkway, looking back down it at City Hall. It opened in nineteen twenty eight and it was designed, in the end, by Julian Abele, the chief designer at the firm of Horace Trumbauer, who was the first Black graduate of Penn's architecture school and whose name was left off the building for seventy years.

Now the steps. There are seventy two of them, and in nineteen seventy six a boxer in a grey tracksuit ran up them in a film and turned at the top and raised his arms, and since then more people have run up these steps than have gone through the doors at the top of them. The statue of Rocky, the real bronze from the third film, stands at the bottom to the right, and there is a line for a photograph with it at all hours. Run the steps if you want to. Everyone does. The museum has made its peace with it, and there is a set of bronze footprints at the top where he stood.

Then, please, go inside, because the collection is the reason the steps exist.

It is one of the great museums of the country, a quarter of a million objects, and it cannot be done in a day, so here is a guide's list for two hours. Go up the great stair, past the Diana on the landing, the gilded bronze that used to stand on top of Madison Square Garden in New York and was rescued when the building came down. Turn left for Europe. The museum owns Cezanne's Large Bathers, the biggest and last of them, and the Van Gogh Sunflowers that is not the one in London, and a whole room of Marcel Duchamp, more than anywhere else in the world, including the Nude Descending a Staircase that caused a riot in nineteen thirteen and the Large Glass, the cracked one, and, behind a wooden door in a wall with two peepholes in it, the last piece he made, in secret, over twenty years, which you look at through the holes and will not forget.

Then the period rooms, which are what this museum does that others do not. There is a whole medieval cloister from France, arches and garden, reassembled inside. There is a Japanese teahouse, a real one, brought here in the nineteen twenties, with a garden round it. There is a Chinese palace hall with its painted ceiling, and an Indian temple hall, and a room from a Paris hotel, and a Pennsylvania German farmhouse kitchen. You walk out of a gallery and into a building from another continent, and it is the museum's oldest idea, from the nineteen twenties, that a museum should be a walk through the world.

And there is the American wing, which is the one Philadelphians come for: Thomas Eakins, the city's own painter, with the Gross Clinic, the surgeon in the black coat with blood on his hand, which the city bought for itself in two thousand six when it was about to be sold away, and the Pennsylvania furniture, and the Shaker rooms.

Here is the honest note about time. The steps and the view are free and take fifteen minutes. The museum is a paid ticket and needs two hours to be worth it, and it is pay what you wish on the first Sunday of every month and on Friday evenings, when it stays open late and there is music in the great hall. If you are doing this walk in one day you may not have the two hours, and if so, do the steps, look at the view down the Parkway to Penn on his tower, and come back for the museum on its own day. It deserves one.

The last stop is ten minutes north on foot, and it is the opposite of this one.
