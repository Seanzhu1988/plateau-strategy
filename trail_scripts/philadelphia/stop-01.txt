Start here, at the corner of Sixth and Market, because the whole of the old city is laid out from this door. This is the Independence Visitor Center, and it is the front desk for everything you are about to walk through: the Park Service's own desk, with the maps, the rangers, and the one thing you cannot get anywhere else, which is a timed ticket for Independence Hall.

So do that first. Before the bell, before the coffee, before anything. The tickets are free, they are given out for the day each morning, and in summer they are gone by the middle of the morning. A ticket has a time on it, and that time decides the shape of your day. Get it, look at the clock, and then work out how much of this walk you can do before you have to be back at the door of the Hall.

While you are inside, take a minute with the map on the wall. The historic district is small. From this corner you can walk to every building on the first half of this tour in less than fifteen minutes, and most of them are within five. The Liberty Bell is across the street. Independence Hall is one block south. Franklin's court is two blocks east. The buildings are not scattered across a city; they are a village, four or five streets by four or five streets, and in seventeen seventy six that village was the largest town in British North America.

Here is the thing to hold in your head as you walk out. Philadelphia was the capital. Not the first capital, and not the last, but the capital for the ten years that mattered most, from seventeen ninety to eighteen hundred, while the city on the Potomac was still a drawing. Congress sat here. The Supreme Court sat here. The President lived here, two of them, Washington and Adams, in a house whose foundations you will see across the street. Everything the new country decided in its first decade, it decided within a few hundred yards of where you are standing.

And before that, for the two decades before, this same village was where the argument happened. The First Continental Congress met here in seventeen seventy four, in a carpenters' guild hall because the colony's own building was not safe to use. The Second met in the State House the next year and stayed, through the Declaration, through the war, through the Articles of Confederation. The convention that wrote the Constitution sat in the same room in seventeen eighty seven, behind locked doors and shuttered windows, in a Philadelphia summer, and told nobody what they were doing until it was done.

The centre is a good place to get your bearings for the ground too. The streets here are William Penn's grid from sixteen eighty two, the first planned city in the colonies, numbered streets running north and south, tree streets running east and west. Market Street, where you are, was High Street then, and it was the market, with stalls down the middle. Chestnut is the next street south, and that is where the Hall is. Walnut is the one after. If you can count and you know a few trees, you cannot get lost in Philadelphia, which was the point.

One more thing before you go out. The centre has the film, and it is short, and it is worth the twenty minutes if you have come with children or with anyone who is going to ask you who all these people were. It also has the restrooms, and the next ones are a walk away. Use them.

Then cross the street. The building in front of you with the long glass wall is the Liberty Bell Center, and the bell is at the far end of it, in a room with a window that looks straight at the tower it hung in. That is where we are going next.

A note on the order. This walk goes bell, Hall, then the two smaller halls either side of it, then east toward the river and Franklin's neighbourhood, then north to the oldest street, then west across the city to the market, City Hall, the Parkway and the museum on the hill. It is long. Nobody has to do all of it. If your ticket time is early, do the Hall first and come back to the bell after; the bell has no ticket and it is open all day. And if you only have a morning, the first thirteen stops are the ones that matter, and they are all within these few streets.
