Walk west along Arch Street to Fifth, and the low modern building of glass and pale stone that fills the block on your right, facing down the Mall toward Independence Hall, is the National Constitution Center. It opened on the fourth of July two thousand and three, and it sits where it does on purpose: at the far end of the same green from the room where the Constitution was written, looking straight back at it. The Hall is the room. This is the argument.

Go in and do the show first. It is called Freedom Rising, it is in a round theatre with a live actor and a screen that wraps round you, it lasts seventeen minutes, and it starts every visit. Most museums have a film you can skip. Skip this one and you have missed the museum's whole idea, which is that the Constitution is not a document in a case but a fight that is still going on, and that you are in it. The actor will tell you so, and the actor is good.

Then the main hall, which is a circle, because the building is a circle, and it goes round the story from seventeen eighty seven to now: the convention, the fight over ratification, the Bill of Rights, and then the amendments, one by one, each one a moment when the country changed its mind about who counted. There are voting machines you can use, a Supreme Court bench you can sit at, and a great many touchscreens, and it is designed for children and works for them, so if you have brought some this is the place to let them go.

But the room to find is at the end, and it is called Signers' Hall. It is a room full of statues, forty two of them, life size, in bronze, and they are the men who were in the Assembly Room on the seventeenth of September seventeen eighty seven, standing about as if the convention had just finished and nobody had gone home. You can walk among them. You can put your hand on Washington's shoulder. Franklin is sitting down, because he was eighty one and had gout. Madison is very small; he was five foot four. Hamilton is looking at Madison. And they are placed the way they stood, in groups, arguing.

Here is the thing to do in that room. Find the three who did not sign. Thirty nine signed. Three were there on the last day and refused: Edmund Randolph of Virginia, who had introduced the plan and then thought it went too far; George Mason of Virginia, who wanted a bill of rights and was told there would not be one; and Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts, who thought the whole thing would end in tyranny. They are in the room, in bronze, standing apart. And then count the people who are not there at all, which is a longer list: no women, no Black delegates, no Native delegates, and the state of Rhode Island, which refused to send anyone. The room is honest about this. There is a plaque.

Go and stand near Mason. He lost the argument in that room and won it two years later, when Madison, who had opposed a bill of rights, wrote one, because the states would not ratify without it. The first ten amendments are Mason's monument, and the museum's whole second half is about the seventeen that came after.

One more object. The centre holds one of the twelve surviving first printings of the Constitution, the version Congress sent to the states in seventeen eighty seven, and one of the first public printings of the Bill of Rights. They are in a case in the main hall, in low light, and they are worth the walk over. The words are the same as the words in the schoolbook. It is the paper that is different.

This is a paid museum, and it is the second paid stop on the walk. Give it an hour and a quarter; the show and Signers' Hall are the two things not to cut. When you come out, you are at the top of the Mall, and the old city is finished. The walk now turns west across the modern city, and the next stop is a place to eat.
