Cross Fifteenth Street from City Hall and you are in a paved plaza with a fountain in the middle and, at the far side of it, four red letters stacked two by two, ten feet high, with the O leaning over as if it had been pushed. This is LOVE Park, and the letters are the most photographed object in Philadelphia after the bell, and there is a line for those too.

The sculpture is by Robert Indiana, and he did not make it for here. He made the design in nineteen sixty four as a Christmas card for the Museum of Modern Art, then as a painting, then as a print that was pirated all over the world, then as a stamp, and in nineteen seventy he made the first of the big steel ones. This one was lent to the city for the bicentennial in nineteen seventy six and was so loved that when the lender came to take it back in nineteen seventy eight the city objected, and a Philadelphia businessman bought it and gave it to the plaza for good. Its real name, the plaza's, is John F. Kennedy Plaza. Nobody has called it that since the letters arrived.

Stand behind the sculpture, not in front, and look through it. The letters frame the Parkway, and the Parkway runs straight from here, a mile, to the Art Museum on its hill, and everything between was planned to be seen from about where you are. The Benjamin Franklin Parkway is the city's one grand gesture, a boulevard cut diagonally across Penn's grid in the nineteen twenties on the model of the Champs Elysees, by a French planner, Jacques Greber, with the museum at one end as the Louvre and City Hall at the other. It cost the city thirteen hundred buildings, which were demolished to make it, and it gave the city a spine of museums, a cathedral, and a great fountain, and a line of flags of every country in the world down both sides.

Look at the fountain in front of you first. It runs in summer, and it is the place where the city sits, and for about twenty years, from the eighties to two thousand two, this plaza was the most famous skateboarding spot on earth. The granite ledges and steps were perfect for it, skaters came from every country, and the city's answer was to rebuild the plaza so they could not. Then it rebuilt it again in twenty eighteen, into what you see, and the skaters are still not welcome and still come.

Now look up the Parkway. Two blocks on, the round intersection with the big fountain is Logan Circle, one of Penn's five original squares, with the Swann fountain by Alexander Stirling Calder, the son of the man who did the sculpture on City Hall and the father of the man who did the mobiles; the three Calders line up on this street. The domed church on its right is the Cathedral Basilica of Saints Peter and Paul, eighteen sixty four, with almost no windows at street level because it was built in a decade when Catholic churches in this city were being burned. The big classical building on the left is the Free Library, and across from it the Franklin Institute, the science museum, with a twenty foot marble Franklin sitting inside the door like Lincoln in Washington. Beyond them, the Barnes Foundation, and then the Rodin, and then the museum.

Here is the honest advice about distance. From here to the museum steps is a mile and a quarter, mostly uphill at the end, and it is a beautiful walk on a fine day, and a long one on a hot one. The bus runs up the Parkway every few minutes, and there is no shame in it. If you walk, the flags will keep you company, and the Barnes is about two thirds of the way.

The next stop on this tour is the Rodin, which is small and quick and stands right on the Parkway a block before the museum. The Barnes, beside it, is the great collection of Impressionist painting that a chemist assembled in the twenties, and it needs a booked ticket and a morning; if you have the morning, it is the best gallery in the city.

One last thing before you leave the letters. Look at them from the side. Indiana's O leans because he wanted the word to be unstable, a little off, the way the thing itself is. The city has straightened everything else about this plaza twice. It has never straightened the O.
