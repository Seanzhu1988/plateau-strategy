On Arch Street, between Second and Third, a small brick house with a flag over the door stands a little back from the pavement behind a courtyard, and there is usually a line. This is the Betsy Ross House, and it is the place to talk honestly about a story, because the story is the reason the line is there.

The story is this. In the early summer of seventeen seventy six, George Washington, Robert Morris and George Ross, a Pennsylvania delegate and the uncle of Betsy's late husband, called on a young widow who ran an upholstery business, showed her a sketch of a flag with thirteen stripes and thirteen six pointed stars, and asked if she could make it. She said she could, and suggested five pointed stars instead, because she could cut a five pointed star from a folded piece of cloth with one snip, and she showed them. And she sewed the first flag.

Here is what is true. Betsy Ross was real. She was born Elizabeth Griscom in seventeen fifty two, into a Quaker family, apprenticed to an upholsterer, and eloped at twenty one with a fellow apprentice named John Ross, which got her expelled from the Quaker meeting. He died in seventeen seventy six. She kept the business going, and she did make flags; there is a receipt from seventeen seventy seven for ship's colours for the Pennsylvania navy with her name on it. She married twice more, buried two more husbands, ran the business for fifty years, and died in eighteen thirty six at eighty four. She was a working woman in a working street, and she was tough.

Here is what nobody can prove. The flag story was told for the first time in eighteen seventy, thirty four years after she died, by her grandson William Canby, to the Historical Society of Pennsylvania. He said she had told it to him as a child. There is no paper from seventeen seventy six, no diary, no letter, no minute of Congress, and the men who might have written it down did not. Historians, on the whole, do not believe the details. The flag with the circle of stars is more likely Francis Hopkinson's design, and he sent Congress a bill for it. But nobody can prove she did not sew the first one either, and after a hundred and fifty years of telling, the story is a piece of the country's furniture whether it happened or not.

And the house is real, and it is the point. Nobody is even certain she lived in this exact house rather than the one next door, which is gone, but it is a house of the right kind on the right street in the right decade, and that is what you are looking at. Go in. It is small and steep and the stairs are a ladder, and every room is the size of a room in a boat. The front room on the ground floor is the shop, with the cutting table and the bolts of cloth, where an upholsterer took orders for bed hangings, curtains, mattresses, and, when the fleet needed them, flags. Upstairs are the rooms she and her family lived in, and the cellar is the kitchen, with the hearth.

Here is the thing to take away. Stand in the front room, and notice how close the street is. Philadelphia in seventeen seventy six was a city of these houses, thousands of them, two rooms deep, three storeys high, with a shop in front and a family behind and above, and the people who made the Revolution work, the printers, sailmakers, upholsterers, cordwainers, lived and worked in them. The signers had bigger houses. The city ran on these.

Betsy is buried in the courtyard, or at least the courtyard is where her bones were moved to in nineteen seventy five, after two earlier moves, and there is some doubt about whether the right bones were moved. A flag flies over her. On a fine day there is a woman in the courtyard in an eighteenth century gown who will tell you the story as if it were true, and she is very good, and you can decide.

The ticket is a few dollars and the courtyard is free. The next stop is a two minute walk, back toward Second Street and one block north, and it is a whole street, not a house.
