On the left side of the Parkway, a block before the museum, a small stone pavilion stands behind a gateway and a garden with a pool in it, and a bronze man sits on a plinth at the gate with his chin on his hand, thinking. Everyone knows him. He is the Thinker, and this is the Rodin Museum, and it holds the largest collection of Rodin's work anywhere outside Paris. It is the smallest museum on this walk and one of the best, and you can see the whole of it in three quarters of an hour.

The man who built it was Jules Mastbaum, who owned movie theatres, more than anyone else in the country in the nineteen twenties, and who went to Paris in nineteen twenty three, walked into a shop, bought a small bronze by Rodin, and came out a collector. In three years he bought over a hundred pieces, and paid for casts of the greatest ones to be made from Rodin's own moulds, and hired the French architect Paul Cret, who was teaching at Penn, to build a museum for them on the Parkway, as a gift to the city. Mastbaum died in nineteen twenty six, before a stone was laid. His widow finished it, and it opened in nineteen twenty nine.

Start at the gate, with the Thinker. He is a cast from Rodin's own moulds, so he is not a copy; there are about twenty of these in the world, and this is one. Look at his feet, which grip the rock, and his right elbow, which rests on his left knee, which is anatomically wrong and which Rodin did on purpose because it made the body twist. He was made to sit at the top of a doorway and look down at the damned, and he was called the Poet, and he was Dante. He became the Thinker when he was made large and put on his own.

Then walk through the garden to the door, and stop at the door, because the door is the reason to come. The bronze doors set into the front of the building are the Gates of Hell, Rodin's life work, a commission for a museum in Paris that was never built, that he worked on for thirty seven years and never finished, and that was cast for the first time after he died. Mastbaum paid for this cast, and for the one at the Musee Rodin in Paris, so that both cities would have one. Stand in front of it for a full minute before you do anything else. It is twenty feet high and there are nearly two hundred figures on it, falling, clinging, twisting, and almost every famous sculpture Rodin made started as a figure on this door. The Thinker is at the top, in the middle, looking down. The Three Shades are above him. The Kiss was on it and was taken off because it was too happy.

Inside, the rooms are small and calm and the light is good, and the pieces to find are the Burghers of Calais, the six men walking to their deaths with the keys of their city, the marble of the Kiss, the studies for Balzac, and the hands. Rodin made hundreds of hands, and the museum shows them like a collection of portraits, which is what they are.

Here is the thing to notice, and it is the thing about Rodin. Walk round any of the bronzes and look at the surface. It is rough. There are thumbprints in it, and lumps, and places where the clay was pressed on and left. Rodin was the first sculptor who let you see the making, and the critics of his day thought it was unfinished, and it is the reason the figures look alive and the smooth marble men in the Second Bank look dead.

The garden is free. The museum asks you to pay what you wish, and it is closed on Tuesdays. Then walk on up the Parkway, a few minutes, and the yellow stone temple at the top of the steps is the museum, and the steps are the ones you have seen in the film.
