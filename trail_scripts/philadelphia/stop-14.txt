Walk west on Arch Street for about ten minutes, under the Chinatown gate if you drift a block north, and at Twelfth Street a long low building with an iron and glass roof fills the block. Go in through any door. The noise, the smell, and the crowd hit you at once, and you are in Reading Terminal Market, which is the best place to eat in Philadelphia and has been since eighteen ninety two.

It is a market under a railway station. The Reading Railroad built its terminal on Market Street in the eighteen nineties, and the train shed above you, the great arched iron roof, carried the tracks, on the floor above this one. The market was put in the basement of the shed, at street level, because two street markets already stood on this block and the railroad had to house them to build. So the trains ran overhead and the market ran below for ninety years, until the trains stopped in nineteen eighty four and the shed became a convention centre. The market never closed. It is the oldest thing on this block by a century, and the busiest.

Here is how to work it. There are about eighty stalls, in a grid of aisles, and they fall into three kinds. There are the lunch counters, where you eat standing or at a stool: roast pork, cheesesteaks, oysters, hoagies, pretzels, doughnuts, ice cream. There are the grocers, the butchers, the fishmongers and the bakers, who sell to the city and have for generations. And there is the Pennsylvania Dutch corner, at the west end, where the Amish and Mennonite farmers from Lancaster County sell what they make: scrapple, shoofly pie, soft pretzels, chicken pot pie that is a stew and not a pie, and apple dumplings the size of your fist. The Dutch stalls are here Wednesday to Saturday only, because the farmers go home, and they close early.

Now the advice, and this is a guide talking, not a brochure. Do not get in the cheesesteak line. The famous cheesesteak counter has a line round the aisle and the sandwich is fine, and the sandwich you want is the roast pork. Find DiNic's, in the middle of the market, and order the roast pork with sharp provolone and broccoli rabe. It is the sandwich this city should be famous for, and the line moves. If you want the cheesesteak experience, you will get a better one on a street corner in South Philly for the same money, and you will have the story to go with it.

Then walk. Go to the Dutch end and get a soft pretzel from the Amish counter, still warm and twisted by a girl in a bonnet who does not want her photograph taken, so do not. Get a coffee at the counter by the Twelfth Street door, which has had the same owners for decades. Look at the fish at the seafood counter in the middle, the oysters on ice, and at the butcher's cases, which sell parts of animals that supermarkets have forgotten exist. And find the pig. There is a bronze pig by the central seating area, a big one, named Philbert, and people rub his snout for luck and put coins in the trough; the coins go to a food bank.

Sit down at the tables in the middle, if you can find a seat, with your sandwich, and look up at the roof, and listen. Twenty languages, a dozen accents of English, the Amish girls, the fishmongers shouting, the tourists, the office workers from the towers across the street. This is the city eating lunch, and it has been the city eating lunch since before the Liberty Bell moved out of the Hall.

One more counter, because people ask: the ice cream at Bassetts, by the Twelfth Street door, has been sold from this floor since the market opened, the same family, the same name, and it is the oldest ice cream company in the country. Get a cone for the walk to City Hall.

The market is open every day but the Dutch stalls are not; the busiest hour is one o'clock, and the quietest is the first one after it opens. When you have eaten, go out the Market Street side, turn right, and walk one block west. The building filling the end of the street, with the statue on top, is where we are going.
