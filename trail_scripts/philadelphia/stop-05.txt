Now walk past the front of Independence Hall to the other end, and the small brick building at the corner of Fifth and Chestnut is Old City Hall. It is Congress Hall's twin, built at the same time to the same design, in seventeen ninety and ninety one, and it was meant to be exactly what its name says, the city hall of Philadelphia. It was, for sixty years. But for its first decade it was also lent to the federal government, like its twin across the square, and while Congress sat in the one building and the President worked a block away, the Supreme Court of the United States sat here.

So stand for a moment on the pavement between the three buildings and take in what you are looking at. On your left, the Congress. In the middle, the Hall where the Declaration and the Constitution were written. On your right, the Supreme Court. All three branches of the government, within a hundred yards of each other, in three brick buildings on one street, for the whole of the seventeen nineties. Nowhere else in the country can you see that, and in Washington today you would need a car to do it.

The Court that sat here was a small thing. Six justices, appointed by Washington, with John Jay of New York as the first Chief Justice, and they met in a room on the ground floor that you can see, with a bench, a bar, and a jury box, because the court also tried cases. It heard very little in its first years, and the justices spent most of their time riding circuit, travelling the country on horseback to hold court in the states, which they hated. Jay resigned to become governor of New York and said the Court would never have enough dignity or energy to be worth the trouble. He was wrong, but he was wrong in Washington, not here. The case that made the Court what it is, Marbury against Madison, was decided in eighteen oh three, after the move.

But something important did happen in this room. In seventeen ninety three the Court decided Chisholm against Georgia, and ruled that a citizen of one state could sue another state in federal court. The states were so alarmed that they passed the Eleventh Amendment to overturn it, the first time the Constitution was amended to reverse the Court, and the argument about how much power the states had given away, which started in that room, has never really stopped.

Go inside if it is open. The Park Service keeps the ground floor as the courtroom, and the exhibit upstairs is about the Court's beginnings. It is a quick stop, ten minutes, and it completes the set.

Then, before you leave the square, look at the ground behind the Hall. That is Independence Square, the yard of the State House, and it was here, not inside, that the Declaration was first read to the public, on the eighth of July seventeen seventy six, from a platform by the tower door, by a colonel of the militia named John Nixon, with the bell ringing above him. The crowd was not enormous. Philadelphia had a lot of people who thought the whole business was a mistake. But the ones who came cheered, and that night they took down the King's arms from the courtroom wall and burned them in the street.

Two more buildings stand on the square and they are worth knowing as you pass. Philosophical Hall, the small brick building on the Fifth Street side behind Old City Hall, is the home of the American Philosophical Society, which Franklin founded in seventeen forty three and which is the oldest learned society in the country. It has a museum that is free and usually empty. And across Fifth Street from it, the marble building with the columns is Library Hall, a nineteen fifties reconstruction of the Library Company's building of seventeen ninety, the library Franklin also founded, which was the Library of Congress before there was one.

From here the walk turns east, toward the river and the older, smaller streets where the people who sat in these rooms actually lived. The next stop is one block along Chestnut, and you cannot miss it, because it is a Greek temple in the middle of a colonial town.
