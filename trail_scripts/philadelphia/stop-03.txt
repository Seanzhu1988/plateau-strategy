This is the building. Everything else on this walk is here because of this one, and you are about to go into the room where the country was decided, twice.

Stand in front first, on Chestnut Street, and look at it as a building. It was not built for any of that. It was built as the State House of the colony of Pennsylvania, begun in seventeen thirty two and finished in seventeen fifty three, designed by Andrew Hamilton, a lawyer, with Edmund Woolley, a master builder, and it is Georgian, red brick, two storeys, symmetrical, a colonial government office with a tower behind it. The tower is where the bell hung. The wooden steeple on top rotted and was taken down in seventeen eighty one, and what you see now is the replacement William Strickland put up in eighteen twenty eight, which is why it looks a little grander than the building under it.

The ranger will take you in through the door at the tower end, and the first room is the courtroom, on the right. Pennsylvania's supreme court sat here, and the room is set up as it was, with the bar, the dock, and the King's arms gone from the wall. Look at the floor. It is the original brick. Then you cross the hall into the Assembly Room, and that is the room.

It is smaller than you expect. Everyone says so. Forty feet square, grey walls, tall windows on two sides, tables covered in green cloth, and at the far end a raised chair. In this room, on the fourth of July seventeen seventy six, the Second Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. Jefferson wrote it in a rented room three blocks from here. Franklin and Adams edited it. Congress argued over it for two days and cut a quarter of it out, including Jefferson's paragraph against the slave trade. It was signed here, most of it, on the second of August, by men who knew that their names on that paper would hang them if the war went wrong.

Eleven years later, in the summer of seventeen eighty seven, this same room held the Constitutional Convention. Fifty five delegates, four months, the windows shut and the doors guarded, in a Philadelphia summer, because they had agreed that nothing said inside would be repeated outside until they had finished. Washington sat in the raised chair at the end. On the last day, the seventeenth of September, thirty nine of them signed, and Franklin, who was eighty one and had to be helped in, made the remark that every ranger in this room will tell you.

So look at the chair. It is the real one, the one Washington sat in for the whole convention, and it has a sun carved into the top of its back, half a sun, with rays. Franklin said that through all those months, in the ups and downs of the debate, he had looked at that sun and not been able to tell whether it was rising or setting. And that now, at the end, he had the happiness to know it was a rising sun.

The silver inkstand on the table in front of the chair is real too. Philip Syng made it in seventeen fifty two for the Assembly, and the Declaration and the Constitution were both signed with pens dipped in it. Almost everything else in the room is a period piece rather than the actual object, because the furniture was sold off in the nineteenth century when nobody thought this room mattered, and the Park Service has been buying it back ever since. The chair and the inkstand never left.

One more thing about the room. The Second Continental Congress met here for years, not days. It appointed Washington to command the army in this room in seventeen seventy five. It fled twice when the British came. It adopted the Articles of Confederation here. And in seventeen eighty three, when the government could not pay its own soldiers and a mutinous regiment surrounded the building, Congress left Philadelphia altogether and went to Princeton, which is a story for another walk.

The Hall became a World Heritage Site in nineteen seventy nine, the first building in the United States on that list. You need the timed ticket from the Visitor Center, the tour is led by a ranger and takes about twenty minutes, and it is the best twenty minutes in the city. When you come out, do not leave the square. The two smaller buildings either side of the Hall are the next two stops, and they are the other two branches of the government.
