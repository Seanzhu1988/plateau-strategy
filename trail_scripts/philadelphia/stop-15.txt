Walk up Market Street and City Hall fills the end of it, because City Hall is built in the middle of the street, at the crossing of the two main streets William Penn drew in sixteen eighty two. Stand across Market from it, on the Dilworth Park side, and look up, all the way up, to the man on top. That is William Penn, in bronze, thirty seven feet tall, on a tower five hundred and forty eight feet high, and for a hundred years he was the tallest thing in the city by agreement.

The building took thirty years, from eighteen seventy one to nineteen oh one, and it was meant to be the tallest building in the world when it was designed. It was not, by the time it was finished; the Eiffel Tower and the Washington Monument had gone up in the meantime. But it is still the largest city hall in the country, and the largest masonry building in the world, and it is built of stone and brick, all the way up, with no steel frame, which is why the walls at the base are twenty two feet thick. The architect was John McArthur, a Scot, and the style is French Second Empire, the same style as the grey building beside the White House, the mansard roofs, the pavilions, the tiers of columns, the sculpture on every face. There are two hundred and fifty sculptures on the outside, all by one man, Alexander Milne Calder, who spent twenty years on it and finished with Penn on the top. Calder's son made the giant fountain on the Parkway you will see next, and his grandson made the mobiles, and the three generations are in a straight line up the Parkway from here.

Walk into the middle of it. There are four archways, one from each street, and they meet in a courtyard in the centre of the building. Stand in the courtyard and look straight up. It is one of the few places in an American city where the sky is a square. The tower rises from the north side, and if you stand under it you cannot see the top; it is too close.

Now the gentlemen's agreement. When City Hall was finished, the city's builders agreed, without a law, that nothing would be built higher than the brim of Penn's hat. For eighty six years nobody did. Then in nineteen eighty seven a developer built One Liberty Place, the blue glass tower you can see to the west, and it was taller, and the city's sports teams stopped winning championships, and Philadelphians noticed, and the curse of Billy Penn became a thing people said seriously. In two thousand and seven the builders of the Comcast Center, then the tallest, put a small statue of Penn on the top beam, and the Phillies won the World Series the next year, and nobody in this city thinks that was a coincidence.

The tower is the thing to do if you can. There is a small lift, one at a time, that goes up inside the tower to an observation deck just below Penn's feet, and from it you can see the whole city, the two rivers, the Mall you have walked and the Parkway you are about to, and the top of Penn's boots. The tours are ticketed, in small groups, and they sell out, so book ahead if this matters to you. If it does not, the courtyard is free and the ground floor has a visitor centre with the story.

And look at the clock faces on the tower while you are here, one on each side. They are twenty six feet across, bigger than Big Ben's, and they are lit at night, and for a century they were the clock the whole city set its watch by.

One last look at Penn before you go. He is facing north east, toward the spot on the river where he signed his treaty with the Lenape, and his hand is out, and from some angles on the ground it looks as if he is doing something else entirely, which every child in Philadelphia knows and will tell you.

From the courtyard, take the west arch out, cross Fifteenth Street, and the plaza in front of you with the fountain is the start of the Parkway, and the next stop.
