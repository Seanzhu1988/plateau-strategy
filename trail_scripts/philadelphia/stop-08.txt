At the corner of Third and Chestnut, the big brick and stone building with the round front is the Museum of the American Revolution. It opened on the nineteenth of April two thousand and seventeen, which is the anniversary of Lexington and Concord, and it is the newest building on this walk by a long way, and the one to give real time to, because it does the thing the old buildings cannot. They show you rooms. This shows you the war.

Go in and do it in order, because it is built as a story, and the story goes from a coffee house argument in the seventeen sixties to a country in the seventeen nineties, room by room, with the objects to prove it. There is a liberty tree you walk under. There is a full scale privateer's deck. There are the muskets, the uniforms, the letters, and a great many ordinary things, a soldier's shoe, a woman's account book, a slave's manumission paper, that tell you who was actually there. The museum is honest about that, which is its best quality. It gives the war to the people who fought it and lived through it, including the ones the old story left out: the Oneida who sided with the Americans, the enslaved men who fought for the British because the British promised freedom, the women who followed the army and ran its camps, the loyalists who lost everything. It is not a museum of marble men.

But the reason to come, and the reason to save your patience for the end, is the tent.

At the end of the route you sit down in a small theatre for a short film, twelve minutes or so, about one object, and then the screen goes up and the object is there behind glass in the dark. It is Washington's headquarters tent. Not a copy. The actual canvas marquee he lived and worked in for most of the war, from Valley Forge to Yorktown, the tent his officers called his oval office because it was where the decisions were made. It is linen, it is patched, it is faded to the colour of an old sail, and it survived because his step-grandson kept it, and then the step-grandson's daughter kept it through the Civil War, hiding it from the Union army, and then it was sold to pay for a Confederate widows' home, and then a minister bought it for a museum that took ninety years to be built. The film tells that story better than I have. Then the curtain rises.

Here is what to do. When the lights come up, most people take a photograph and leave. Stay. Walk right up to the glass, and look at the seams and the ties and the wear at the door flap, and think about the man who lived in it, six foot two, in a canvas room fourteen feet long, through six winters, while Congress sat in a warm building a few streets from here and argued about his pay. It is the most human object in Philadelphia, and it is the size of a garden shed.

The museum is not free. It is the only paid stop on the first half of this walk, and it is worth what it costs; the ticket is good for two days, so you can come back. Give it an hour and a half. The cafe on the ground floor is the best lunch between here and the market, and the terrace upstairs looks over the roofs of the old city toward the river.

One practical thing. If you have children with you, this is the building that will hold them. The old halls are rooms with chairs in them, and a ten year old sees that in about a minute. This has a ship, a tree, a battlefield in a box, and a film that ends with a curtain going up on a real tent, and I have watched the same ten year old stand in front of that glass for longer than any adult.

When you leave, go back up to Market Street and turn toward the river. Franklin's house was in the middle of the next block, and what is left of it is the next stop.
