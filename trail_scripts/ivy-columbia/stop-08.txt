There is a small confusion to clear up before you go in. There is another St Paul's Chapel in New York, downtown by the World Trade Center site, which is the oldest church building in Manhattan and where Washington prayed after his inauguration, and which became a refuge for rescue workers after the eleventh of September. It has nothing to do with this one beyond the name. If somebody tells you about the shoes and the pews and the recovery workers, they mean the other one.

This is St Paul's Chapel, finished in nineteen oh seven, and it is the best interior on this campus by a distance.

It is not by McKim. It was designed by Howells and Stokes, and Isaac Newton Phelps Stokes was a Columbia trustee's son who was also a serious architect and later wrote a six volume history of Manhattan. The chapel was given by two sisters, Olivia and Caroline Phelps Stokes, in memory of their parents.

From outside it is a compact building in the campus brick, in a northern Italian Renaissance manner, with a portico and a low dome on a drum. It sits deliberately off the main axis, tucked to the side, because McKim's plan put learning at the centre and religion at the edge, which was a decision in itself for a university founded by the Church of England.

Go inside.

The plan is a Greek cross under a dome, and the whole interior is faced in warm pale brick and terracotta, laid in Guastavino tile vaulting. That name is worth knowing. Rafael Guastavino was a Catalan builder who brought a medieval Mediterranean technique to America: thin terracotta tiles laid in overlapping layers in mortar, which makes a self supporting vault that needs no steel and no timber centring, and which is astonishingly strong and fireproof. His firm vaulted Grand Central Terminal's Oyster Bar, the Boston Public Library, the Cathedral of St John the Divine down the road, and the arches under the Queensboro Bridge. Here you can see the tiles themselves, herringboned across the vaults, unplastered.

Look up into the dome. The light is warm because the material is warm, and the acoustics, which follow from the same vaulting, are extraordinary.

That is the chapel's second life. It is one of the best small concert spaces in New York, and there are free concerts and organ recitals in it during term. The organ is a nineteen thirties Aeolian Skinner, rebuilt.

The chapel is interfaith now, and it holds services of several traditions, along with memorials, weddings and lectures. It is normally open in the daytime and it is free and it is almost always quiet, which on this campus is worth something.

There is one more thing to notice, in the windows. The stained glass is by John La Farge's studio and others, and the iconography includes figures from the history of the university as well as saints, which is very much of its moment: an American university in nineteen oh seven placing its own founders in the windows of its chapel alongside the apostles.

Guastavino tile is worth one more sentence because once you have seen it you will see it everywhere in this city. Look for the herringbone pattern of thin flat tiles in any vaulted public space built in New York between about eighteen ninety and nineteen forty: the Registry Room at Ellis Island, the arcade under the Queensboro Bridge, the old City Hall subway station, the Great Hall at the Metropolitan Museum. The firm did around a thousand buildings and then the technique died out because steel and concrete were cheaper.

There is a practical note about visiting. The chapel keeps its own hours, separate from the campus, and it closes for private services. If the door is shut, look at the outside and come back; the interior is worth a second attempt.

Look at the floor as well as the ceiling while you are inside. The paving is laid in a pattern that echoes the vaults above it, and the woodwork of the pews and screens is original. The building has had remarkably little done to it since nineteen oh seven, which is unusual for a chapel that has been in constant use for over a century, and it is why the interior still reads as one designer's single thought.

Sit down for five minutes. Then come out and cross the campus toward the small dome on the west side of Low. That is Earl Hall, your next stop. The physics building comes after it.
