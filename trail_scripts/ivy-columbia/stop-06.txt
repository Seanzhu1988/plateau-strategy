This is Hamilton Hall, home of Columbia College, which is the undergraduate school at the centre of the university. It went up in nineteen oh seven, in the standard campus recipe of red brick and limestone, six storeys, deliberately plain.

Outside it stands a bronze of Alexander Hamilton, by William Ordway Partridge, nineteen oh eight. Hamilton entered King's College, as Columbia then was, in seventeen seventy three or seventy four, an immigrant from the Caribbean in his late teens with a subscription raised by people who had noticed he was extraordinary. He never graduated. The war came, he joined an artillery company, and he was on Washington's staff by twenty two.

Look at the statue for a moment and then think about what the college has made of him. He is on the ten dollar bill, he is the subject of the most successful American musical of the century, and he is claimed by this institution as its most famous undergraduate even though he left without a degree because he went to fight a revolution. That is a good deal to hang on a young man who was here for two years.

Now the building itself, and the reason it matters more than its architecture.

Hamilton Hall is where Columbia's arguments happen. In nineteen sixty eight it was the first building occupied, on the twenty third of April, when students blocked the dean in his office over the gymnasium in Morningside Park and the university's war research. Black students then asked the white students to leave and held the building on their own, renaming it Malcolm X Liberation College, and the two groups ran separate occupations for the rest of the week. That split, and the way the university treated the two buildings differently, is one of the most studied episodes in the history of American student protest.

It was occupied again in nineteen eighty five over apartheid and Columbia's investments in South Africa; that one lasted three weeks and ended with the university divesting. In nineteen ninety six over ethnic studies. And in April twenty twenty four, when students occupied it again during the protests over the war in Gaza, renamed it Hind's Hall after a child killed in the conflict, and the university called in the New York police, who cleared it. More than a hundred people were arrested.

Whatever you think about any of those, notice the pattern: the same door, the same dean's office, five times in sixty years. Universities have buildings where the arguing happens, and everyone on campus knows which one it is.

Inside are lecture halls and the college's offices, and it is not open to visitors.

There is one more thing to look at here. On the corner of the building, notice how ordinary it is: no dome, no tower, no columns of consequence. McKim's plan required that the teaching buildings be a background, and Columbia has stuck to it for a hundred and twenty years, so that a first time visitor cannot tell the philosophy building from the physics building. Whether that is elegance or monotony is the standing criticism of this campus.

It is worth knowing what Columbia College actually is, because the naming confuses visitors. Columbia University contains many schools: the college, the engineering school, the graduate faculties, law, medicine, business, journalism, architecture, social work, international affairs and more. Columbia College is the undergraduate liberal arts school, about forty five hundred students, and it is the oldest part, the direct descendant of King's College. Barnard College, across Broadway, is a separate corporation with its own president and endowment whose students take Columbia classes and receive Columbia degrees, which is an arrangement nobody can explain quickly.

Look up at the top floor of Hamilton before you move on. The building was raised by two storeys in the nineteen twenties, which is why the proportions are slightly heavy for its width, and once you know to look for it you can see the join in the brickwork. Columbia has added floors to several of its buildings over the years, because in Manhattan the cheapest land you own is the air above what you have already built.

From here, walk east across the small plaza, and the building on the far corner has a famous bronze sitting outside it.
