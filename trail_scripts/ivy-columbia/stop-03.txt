This is Low Memorial Library, and the first thing to know is that it has not been a library since nineteen thirty four.

Charles McKim finished it in eighteen ninety seven, and it is the centrepiece of the whole campus: a Greek cross plan under a dome, with a portico of ten Ionic columns, in granite and limestone, raised on a podium at the top of the steps so that it looks down the axis you have just walked. It is modelled on the Pantheon in Rome, by way of every other domed classical building McKim admired, and its dome is the largest all granite dome in the country.

It was paid for by Seth Low, who was president of Columbia at the time and who gave a million dollars of his own money for it, in memory of his father, Abiel Abbot Low, a China trade merchant. Seth Low went on to be mayor of New York. He also, in a decision that tells you something, insisted the building carry his father's name rather than his own.

Go up the steps and in, if the doors are open. What you enter is the rotunda, and it is worth the climb. A great domed room, coffered, with sixteen green marble columns around it, statuary in niches, and the university's ceremonial spaces off it. It was the reading room when this was the library, and the books were on shelves around and below.

The problem was that it was a magnificent room and a bad library. It could hold a fraction of what Columbia needed, it was hard to extend, and by the nineteen thirties the university had built Butler across the way and moved the books there. Low became the administration building, which it still is: the president's office is here, and the trustees meet here, and the Visitors Center is on the ground floor.

Now look up at the frieze on the outside as you go in or come out. Along the entablature are carved the names of the great figures of learning, and the choice is a period piece: Sophocles, Plato, Aristotle, and so on. Every American building of this decade did this, and comparing whose names each institution chose is a small education in what people thought mattered in eighteen ninety seven.

Two things about this building's later history are part of the university's own account of itself. In April nineteen sixty eight, students occupied Low along with four other buildings, in a protest against Columbia's plan to build a gymnasium in Morningside Park, which the neighbourhood of Harlem below saw as a land grab, and against the university's ties to military research during the Vietnam war. The occupation lasted a week. The president's office was held by students, who used his telephone and smoked his cigars. It ended when the New York police cleared the buildings, injuring around a hundred and fifty people and arresting more than seven hundred, and Columbia has never quite finished arguing about that week. The gymnasium was never built.

And in twenty twenty four, students occupied a building on this campus again, and the university again called the police, and the comparisons with nineteen sixty eight were made by everyone including the people making the decisions.

The Visitors Center inside can tell you what is open on the day. The rotunda is usually accessible and it is free.

One more thing about the dome, since it is the object the whole campus points at. It is granite, all of it, and at the time it was built it was the largest all granite dome in the country, an engineering statement as much as an aesthetic one. Inside it is coffered, and the oculus at the top brings light straight down into the rotunda. Stand directly under the middle and look up; on a bright day the effect is the one McKim borrowed from Rome, which is that the room appears to have no roof at all but a hole into the sky.

There is a practical thing about visiting Low. The rotunda is used constantly for university events, and it is frequently closed for a lecture, a dinner or a ceremony, so a shut door is not a rejection. The Visitors Center on the ground floor keeps more reliable hours and it is the place to ask what else is open on the day, including whether the chapel and the libraries are admitting visitors.

Come back out, stand at the top of the steps, and look down the axis at the building facing you across the green.
