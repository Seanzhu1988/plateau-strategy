This is Philosophy Hall, from nineteen ten, and outside it, on a low plinth, sits a naked man with his chin on his hand.

That is a cast of Auguste Rodin's The Thinker, and it is one of the authorised casts made from Rodin's own moulds, not a copy of a copy. Columbia has had it since nineteen thirty. If you did the Philadelphia walk on this tour you met his brother at the gate of the Rodin Museum, and the same explanation applies: he began as a figure at the top of the Gates of Hell, he was Dante looking down at the damned, he was called the Poet, and he only became The Thinker when he was enlarged and set on his own.

Sit on the wall opposite him for a minute. He is placed here, outside the philosophy department, as a joke that has stopped being one: generations of students have walked past a man thinking so hard that his whole body is clenched, on the way to a class about thinking.

Now the building behind him, which has a claim most people never hear.

Philosophy Hall is where FM radio was invented. Edwin Howard Armstrong, a Columbia engineering graduate and later a professor here, worked in the basement of this building through the nineteen twenties and thirties, and in nineteen thirty three he patented frequency modulation, which solved the problem of static that had plagued radio since it began. There is a plaque by the door and it is an official National Historic Landmark of engineering.

Armstrong's story does not end well and it is worth knowing. He had already invented the regenerative circuit and the superheterodyne receiver, which are in every radio ever built. FM was resisted by the broadcasting industry, which had enormous investment in AM, and Armstrong spent the last twenty years of his life in patent litigation, mostly against RCA. In nineteen fifty four, broke, exhausted and estranged from his wife, he wrote her a note, put on his hat and coat, and stepped out of a window of his apartment on the thirteenth floor. His widow continued the lawsuits and won them all.

Every FM radio on earth is descended from work done in the basement of the building you are looking at.

Look at the building's shape too, because there is a small architectural joke in it. Philosophy Hall and Kent Hall opposite were built as a pair, and both are in the campus's standard brick and limestone, but they have a slightly grander cornice and window treatment than their neighbours, because they front the eastern half of the campus and McKim's plan wanted a little more dignity at the corners.

Inside is the philosophy department and the writing programme, and it is not open to visitors.

One more thing to notice while you are sitting with him. This cast is outdoors, unprotected, in New York weather, and the surface shows it: the patina is uneven, there are streaks where rain runs off his shoulders and back, and the bronze is worn bright in the places people touch. Museums keep their casts inside. Columbia has left this one in the rain for nearly a century, which is either careless or exactly right, depending on your view of what a campus is for.

There are more Rodins in New York than you would expect, at the Metropolitan Museum and at the Brooklyn Museum, but this is the one you can sit next to.

While you are here, a word about what else came out of this campus, because the list is long and nobody puts it on a plaque. The laser was conceived by Charles Townes at Columbia. The first American demonstration of nuclear fission was two hundred yards from where you are sitting. Barbara McClintock did the genetics that won her a Nobel Prize here. FM radio, as we have said, is downstairs. It is a very ordinary looking set of brick buildings in which a startling amount of the twentieth century was worked out.

From here, walk north, back up past the east side of Low, and the building set back on your left with the dome and the columns is the next stop, and it is not what its name suggests.
