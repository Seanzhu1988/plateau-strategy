This is Butler Library, and it is the working library of the university: the one with the books in it, the one where students spend their nights, and the one the whole campus flows toward at examination time.

It was finished in nineteen thirty four, by James Gamble Rogers, and if that name is familiar it is because he built most of Yale between the wars, including the cathedral like Sterling library and the acid aged Harkness Tower. Here he was working in a completely different language: Columbia was classical, so Butler is classical, a long limestone front with fourteen enormous columns, facing Low across the green.

Look at the two buildings facing each other and you have the argument of this campus in one glance. Low, on the high ground, domed, ceremonial, and no longer a library. Butler, on the low ground, rectangular, enormous, stuffed with books. Ambition on one side, work on the other.

Butler holds more than two million volumes on fifteen floors of stacks, and it is the largest single building in Columbia's library system, which as a whole holds around fifteen million items. It was named for Nicholas Murray Butler, who was president of Columbia for forty three years, from nineteen oh two to nineteen forty five, which is a reign rather than a term. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in nineteen thirty one. He also, and this is not hidden, presided over a period in which Columbia used quotas to limit Jewish enrolment, a policy that spread to other universities in the nineteen twenties, and he admired aspects of Mussolini's Italy well into the nineteen thirties. He is a difficult figure and the building carries his name.

Now read the frieze. Along the top of the front, cut into the stone, are the names of the great writers of the western canon: Homer, Herodotus, Sophocles, Plato, Aristotle, Demosthenes, Cicero, Virgil. Read them left to right.

Then notice what is not there. Every one is a man. Every one is from Greece or Rome. There is nothing after antiquity, nothing from outside Europe, and no woman anywhere on that wall. In nineteen eighty nine a group of students hung a banner over it with the names of women writers, and the argument about the frieze has recurred at intervals ever since. Columbia's undergraduate Core Curriculum, the shared course in great books that every student takes and that is famous, has been through the same argument repeatedly, and the reading list has changed while the stone has not.

The building is card access only, so you will not get past the lobby, and that is fine. What you want is out here anyway: stand back on the green and look at the whole facade with the names on it, and then turn and look at Low, and understand that a student walking between them passes under that list several times a day.

If you are here in December or May, look at the windows at night. The building stays open around the clock in reading period, and the whole front is lit.

There is one more thing about Butler worth knowing, and it concerns how a library grows. When it opened in nineteen thirty four it was the answer to Low's inadequacy, and it was thought enormous. Within forty years Columbia had run out of room again and had begun sending books off site to storage in New Jersey, which is now standard practice at every research library in the country. A reader requests a volume and it arrives the next day by van. The romance of the open stack, which Princeton built its library around, has been quietly retreating for fifty years in the face of arithmetic.

One more thing to notice about the frieze while you are looking at it. The names are cut in Roman capitals, deeply, and they were chosen by a committee in the nineteen thirties for a building named after a president who was then still in office. Whatever else it is, it is an unusually durable statement of one committee's opinion, and it is why the argument keeps coming back: the university can change a syllabus in a year and it cannot change a wall.

From here, turn and walk east along the south side of the green, toward the sundial and the buildings beyond it.
