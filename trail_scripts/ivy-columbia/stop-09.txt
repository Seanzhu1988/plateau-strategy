Before you look at the building, notice where you are standing. This is the north west corner of the campus, and the gate here opens onto Broadway opposite Barnard College, so this is one of the busiest crossings in the university's day. Students go back and forth across Broadway constantly between the two institutions, and at class change the traffic light here holds up a genuine crowd.

This is Earl Hall, from nineteen oh two, and it is a McKim building, and it is the odd one out on this campus in a way that is worth two minutes.

Look at it. A small domed rotunda with a columned portico, set on the north west side of the campus, facing Low across the way. It is the only other domed building here, and McKim gave it a dome deliberately, as a small answer to Low's large one: the library at the centre, and this off to the side.

It was given by William Earl Dodge, a merchant and philanthropist, in memory of his father, and it was built as the university chapel and centre of religious life before St Paul's was finished five years later. Carved over the entrance is the inscription that Dodge chose: Erected for the students that religion and learning may go hand in hand and character grow with knowledge.

That sentence is the whole nineteenth century American university in twenty words, and it is worth standing under it and reading it out loud.

What the building does now is a good deal more interesting than what it was built for. Earl Hall is the home of Columbia's Office of Religious Life, and it houses the chaplaincies of many faiths together in one building: Protestant, Catholic, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh and others, along with humanist and secular student groups. A building put up in nineteen oh two by a Protestant merchant to make character grow with knowledge is now shared by a dozen traditions and by people with none.

It has another history too. In the nineteen sixties and seventies Earl Hall was where Columbia's student activist groups met, because it was the building that would give a room to anyone. The Student Homophile League, founded here in nineteen sixty six and chartered in nineteen sixty seven, was the first officially recognised gay student organisation at any American university, and it met in this building. There is a plaque about it.

Inside, the rotunda is a plain circular room with a gallery, used for meetings, services, concerts and rehearsals. It is often open on weekdays and there is usually somebody at a desk who will let you look.

Look at the ironwork of the gate beside the building on your way past, the one that opens onto Broadway. Columbia's gates are all in the same design and they are worth a glance: the university's crown, from its King's College days, worked into the ironwork of a school that changed its name to get away from kings.

One more thing about the inscription over the door, because it repays a second thought. Character grow with knowledge is a claim, not a description: it says that the university believes education makes people better, which is precisely the thing that the twentieth century made hard to believe. Every campus on this tour carries a sentence like it, carved in the confident decades between eighteen eighty and nineteen thirty, and reading them in sequence is a short course in what universities used to think they were doing.

One more thing about the building's shape. Because Earl Hall is round inside a square block, the rooms at the corners are awkward, and generations of student groups have been given the odd triangular offices upstairs. Ask anyone who ran a Columbia club in the last fifty years and they will remember a strange room in this building.

Look also at the lawn in front of it, which is one of the few pieces of unpaved ground on this campus. Space in Manhattan is the whole story here: Columbia has about thirty two acres for a university of thirty six thousand people, where Princeton has six hundred acres for fewer than nine thousand.

From here, walk north along the west side of the campus, past the buildings, and cross a hundred and twentieth Street. The plain building on the far side is where the last stop is, and nothing on this campus has changed the world more.
