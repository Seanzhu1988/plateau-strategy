Start on Broadway at a hundred and sixteenth Street, at the gates, and look east through them, because this is the one view that explains the whole design.

You are looking down College Walk, and at the far end the ground rises to a flight of steps with a domed building on top of it. That is the axis Columbia was built on, and everything else is arranged around it.

First, where you are. This is Morningside Heights, a plateau above the Hudson on the west side of Manhattan, and in the eighteen nineties it was almost empty: a few institutions, some rock outcrops, and the Bloomingdale Asylum for the insane, whose land the university bought. Columbia had been downtown, on Forty Ninth Street where Rockefeller Center now stands, and before that in lower Manhattan, and before that it was King's College, founded in seventeen fifty four by royal charter of George the Second, the fifth college in the colonies.

It changed its name during the Revolution, for obvious reasons. King's College closed when the war reached New York, its building became a military hospital, and it reopened in seventeen eighty four as Columbia. Alexander Hamilton studied there before the war and did not finish, because he went to fight. Gouverneur Morris, who wrote the final text of the Constitution, was a King's College man. John Jay too.

Now, the campus you are about to walk into. It was designed in eighteen ninety four by McKim, Mead and White, the same firm that built Johnston Gate at Harvard, and Charles McKim did this one himself. It is the most complete Beaux Arts campus in America and it is built to a single idea: a symmetrical composition of red brick and limestone buildings around a raised central court, with a domed library at the top of the steps as the head of the whole thing, and everything subordinate to it.

That idea came from the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago in eighteen ninety three, the White City, which McKim helped build, and which convinced a generation of American architects that the classical was the language of civic dignity. Columbia's campus is that exposition made permanent.

And it is dense. Columbia is in Manhattan and it has never had room. The campus is six blocks by three, hemmed in on every side, and the university has been buying, building over and arguing about land for a hundred and thirty years. That pressure is the story of this place, and it will come up at three of the stops on this walk.

Practical things. The campus has controlled access at times, so check Columbia's visitor page before you come; when it is open, walk in through these gates and go anywhere outdoors. Almost no building is open to the public without a card. The whole walk is about half a mile and it is flat except for the steps.

Now, walk in. Note as you go that this street, a hundred and sixteenth, used to be a public road running straight through, with traffic. Columbia closed it in the nineteen fifties and paved it as a pedestrian walk, which is why the campus reads as one place rather than two halves.

One more thing about the ground before you walk in. Morningside Heights is a ridge, and the campus sits at the top of a cliff that drops east into Morningside Park and Harlem below. That geography matters: when Columbia proposed a gymnasium in the park in the nineteen sixties, with one entrance for the university at the top and a separate entrance for the neighbourhood at the bottom, the plan became known as Gym Crow and it was the spark for the occupation of nineteen sixty eight. You will pass the buildings that were occupied. The cliff is why the argument had the shape it did.

The neighbourhood around you is worth ten minutes of its own. Within four blocks are the Cathedral of St John the Divine, which is the largest Gothic cathedral in the world and unfinished, Riverside Church with its carillon, and Grant's Tomb.

A word on the name of the walk you are about to take, since Columbia's campus has no single name for itself. The raised part in front of Low is called the plaza or the steps; the lawn below is South Field, which was a baseball field until nineteen twenty seven; the paved street through the middle is College Walk. Nobody calls the whole thing a quadrangle, because it is not one. It is a formal composition with a hill in it.

Stop halfway along, where the paths cross.
