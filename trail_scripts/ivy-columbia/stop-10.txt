This is Pupin Hall, the physics building, finished in nineteen twenty seven, and it is a National Historic Landmark for what happened in its basement.

It is named for Michael Pupin, a Serbian immigrant who arrived in New York at sixteen with five cents, worked as a labourer, got into Columbia, became a professor here, invented the loading coil that made long distance telephony possible, and won a Pulitzer Prize for his autobiography. That is the sort of career that gets a building named after you.

Now the basement.

In January nineteen thirty nine, news reached New York that Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann in Berlin had split the uranium atom, and that Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch had worked out what it meant. Enrico Fermi had just arrived at Columbia, having collected his Nobel Prize in Stockholm and gone straight on rather than returning to Mussolini's Italy, because his wife was Jewish. Niels Bohr brought the news to America. Within days, in the cyclotron in the basement of this building, John Dunning and his colleagues confirmed nuclear fission experimentally on American soil for the first time, on the twenty fifth of January nineteen thirty nine.

Then the arithmetic began, and it was done here. Fermi and Leo Szilard, working in this building, established that fission released additional neutrons, which meant a chain reaction was possible, which meant a bomb was possible. Szilard, who had thought of the chain reaction years earlier while waiting at a traffic light in London, drafted the letter to President Roosevelt in the summer of nineteen thirty nine and got Einstein to sign it. The Manhattan Project's earliest work was done in this building and it was even briefly called the Manhattan Project for that reason, though the name came from the engineering district office downtown. Fermi later moved to Chicago and built the first reactor under the stands of a football field.

There is a plaque. There is not much else, because it is a working physics department and the cyclotron is long gone.

One more thing about Pupin before you go, and it is a lighter note after the fission. On the roof of this building is the Rutherfurd Observatory, with a telescope, and the astronomy department runs free public observing nights when the sky is clear, usually with a lecture first. Anyone may come. In the middle of Manhattan, with the worst light pollution in the country, undergraduates take turns showing strangers the moons of Jupiter from the top of the building where the chain reaction was worked out.

There is one more piece of this story on the campus that most visitors never hear about. Just north west of here, Columbia's engineering and physics people worked through the nineteen forties on gaseous diffusion, the process for separating uranium isotopes, which became the basis of the enormous plant at Oak Ridge in Tennessee. The work was done in buildings on this campus with students walking past them, in wartime secrecy, in the middle of a city of seven million people.
