This is the Sundial, and it is the most used object on this campus and the one that photographs worst, because most of it is missing.

What you are looking at is a low circular granite drum, about six feet across, standing on the paving in the middle of College Walk. It looks like a plinth waiting for something, and it is.

From nineteen fourteen until nineteen forty six there was a sphere on top of it: a solid ball of green granite, sixteen tons of it, one of the largest pieces of cut stone in the city, which acted as the gnomon of a sundial with the hours marked on the drum below. It was given by the class of eighteen eighty five. In nineteen forty six a crack was found in the sphere and it was judged unsafe, and it was taken down and eventually broken up. The base has stood empty ever since, and the university has periodically discussed putting something back and never has.

So what remains is a stone table in the exact middle of the campus, at the crossing of the main paths, and what it is used for is speaking.

This is Columbia's speakers' corner. Anyone who wants to address the campus stands on the sundial. Political rallies begin here. Student groups set up here. In nineteen sixty eight the rally that led to the occupation of Low and the other buildings began at this spot, and every protest since has gathered at it, including those of twenty twenty four. If you are on campus and hear a crowd, it is at the sundial.

Read the inscription round the drum if you can find it under the wear. It says, in Latin, horam expecta veniet, which means await the hour, it will come. That was chosen in nineteen fourteen for a sundial, and it has been read since as something else entirely by people standing on it waiting for the university to change its mind about something.

Now look around from here, because this is the centre and you can see the plan. West, down the walk, to the gates and Broadway. East, down the walk, to Amsterdam Avenue. North, up the steps, Low and the dome. South, across the green, Butler and the frieze. Every one of McKim's axes crosses at your feet.

And notice the buildings on the four corners of this crossing, because they are the academic buildings and they are all in the same red brick with limestone trim, six storeys, flat roofed, and deliberately subordinate: McKim wanted no single teaching building to compete with Low. Hamilton Hall, Kent, Philosophy, Dodge, Journalism, all built to the same recipe. It is a campus of one loud building and thirty quiet ones.

There is a practical thing worth saying here, because visitors get caught out. Columbia is a working urban university with real security concerns and the gates are sometimes closed to people without a card, particularly during periods of protest. If the campus is open you may walk it freely. If it is not, the outside of Low from Broadway through the gates is still one of the great views in New York, and it costs nothing.

One more thing about the missing sphere. There have been several campaigns over the decades to replace it, including proposals for a new sphere in granite and one for a glass or steel version, and none has happened, partly because the empty base has become the thing itself. Students meet at the sundial. Nobody says meet me at the plinth where the ball used to be. An object can become more useful once it is gone, which does not happen often.

Look down at the paving around the base too. College Walk is laid in a pattern of hexagonal pavers that runs the length of the campus, and the lamps along it, the ones with the globes, are lit every year in December in a ceremony called the tree lighting, when the whole walk goes on at once and a few thousand people stand and watch. It is one of the few Columbia traditions that involves the neighbourhood as much as the university.

From the sundial, walk south east toward the building on the corner with the statue outside it.
