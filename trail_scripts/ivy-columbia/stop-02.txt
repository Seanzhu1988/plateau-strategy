Stop here in the middle of College Walk, where the crossing paths meet, and turn to look up the steps to your left, at the seated bronze woman with her arms open.

That is Alma Mater, by Daniel Chester French, nineteen oh three, and if you have been to Harvard on this tour you have already met the sculptor: he made the John Harvard statue in the Yard and, later, the seated Lincoln in Washington. This is his, in the middle of his career, and it is the best of the three as a piece of design because it has a job to do: it has to hold together an enormous flight of steps and the front of a very large building, and it does.

Look at what she is. She wears an academic gown over classical drapery. She is seated on a throne, and the arms of the throne end in lamps, for learning. In her left hand she holds a sceptre topped with a crown, which is the crown of King's College, the university's original name and still on its seal. Her right hand is raised and open in welcome. On her lap is an open book.

And in the folds of her gown, near her left knee, there is an owl.

That is the thing to find, and it is the ritual of this campus. The legend, which every student is told in their first week, is that the first person in each entering class to find the owl will be the valedictorian, and the other version is that anyone who finds it will marry a Columbia student. It is small, it is bronze on bronze, and it is genuinely not easy to spot until you know. Look at the left side of the drapery, about where her knee is, and let your eye settle. It is there.

French put it in deliberately. The owl is Athena's bird and Alma Mater is a version of Athena, so it is not a joke, but he must have known perfectly well what students would do with it.

Now the statue's other life. Alma Mater sits at the top of the steps facing down the campus, and that makes her the natural backdrop for anything anybody wants to say publicly. Protests have gathered on these steps for a hundred years. In nineteen sixty eight, during the occupation of the campus, she was a rallying point. In nineteen seventy she was damaged by a bomb, which blew a hole in her side and knocked her off the pedestal; she was repaired, and if you look closely you can still find the seam.

Sit on the steps for a minute. Everyone does. These are the steps of the whole university in the sense that Columbia has no green quadrangle to lie on, so this is where people sit, eat lunch, argue and are photographed. On a warm afternoon in term there will be several hundred people on them.

There is one more thing worth knowing about this statue's material history. It is bronze, and it was originally gilded, covered in gold leaf, and it looked entirely different: a gold figure at the top of a grey stone flight of steps. The gilding wore off over the decades and the university let it go, and what you see now is the natural dark patina of the metal. Photographs from the nineteen twenties show her gleaming.

And look at the steps themselves while you sit. They are wider than they need to be, and shallow, and they run the full width of the building. McKim designed them as an outdoor auditorium as much as a stair, and the university uses them that way: commencement, concerts, speeches. He was building a stage.

Two more things while you are on the steps. First, the plinth she sits on carries an inscription reading, in Latin, in thy light shall we see light, which is Columbia's motto, taken from a psalm. Second, look at the throne's arms and you will find the lamps, and at her feet the open book with the words. French put every symbol he could think of into one figure and somehow it does not read as cluttered, which is the difference between a good allegorical sculptor and a bad one.

And if you are here in late May, the steps are where commencement happens, with the whole of College Walk filled with chairs and the graduates in light blue.

Then walk up, past her, to the building behind.
