This is the Yale University Art Gallery, and two things about it matter. It is the oldest university art museum in the western hemisphere, founded in eighteen thirty two, and the building you are standing in front of is Louis Kahn's first major work.

Take the history first. In eighteen thirty two the painter John Trumbull, then in his seventies, made Yale an offer. He would give the college more than a hundred of his paintings, including his eyewitness pictures of the Revolution, if Yale would build a gallery to hold them and pay him an annuity of a thousand dollars a year for the rest of his life. Yale agreed. Trumbull designed the building himself, and when he died in eighteen forty three he was buried beneath it, along with his wife. He was later moved when the building was replaced, and his remains are under the present gallery. There are not many museums where the founding artist is in the basement.

Trumbull had been an aide to Washington and he painted the Revolution from life, sketching the participants when he could find them. His Declaration of Independence, the small original version, is here. You have seen it on the back of a two dollar bill. The room of his work is the reason the museum exists and most visitors walk past it to get to the modern art, which is a mistake.

Now the building. The long grey brick and glass block on the corner is by Louis Kahn, completed in nineteen fifty three, and it was the first modern building at Yale and the commission that made his career. He was fifty two and had built almost nothing of note. Go inside and look up at the ceiling, because that is the thing to see. It is a grid of concrete tetrahedra, a deep triangular waffle, and it is not decoration: it is the structure, carrying the floors, and Kahn ran the ducts and wires through the hollows so that no service is hidden behind a false ceiling. Everything is showing what it does. That idea, that a building should not conceal how it is made, is the whole of Kahn.

Look also at the great cylindrical stair tower in the middle. A concrete drum with a triangular stair inside it, lit from above, and the outer wall is blank. Kahn liked to say that a building should have a place where you feel the material.

The collection is much bigger than the Kahn box: about two hundred thousand objects across three joined buildings, which include Trumbull's successors, a great deal of African art, ancient art from Dura Europos in Syria excavated by Yale in the nineteen twenties and thirties, and a strong modern collection. Van Gogh's The Night Cafe is here, the sour green and red room in Arles that he said he had tried to paint as a place where a man could ruin himself. Hopper is here. There is a Rothko, and a good Mondrian.

There is one more Kahn detail to catch on your way through. Look at the floors and the walls where they meet, and at the way the concrete is finished. He left the marks of the timber formwork showing, and the little conical holes where the ties passed through, rather than plastering them out. Every one of those marks is a record of how the building was poured, and Kahn thought hiding them was a kind of lie.

The single most important practical fact: it is free. Not pay what you wish, not free on a certain day. Free, always, to everybody, and it has been for a long time. It is closed on Mondays.

One more thing about Trumbull, since his pictures are the founding collection. He fought in the Revolution before he painted it, as an aide to Washington at Boston, and he had lost the sight of one eye in a childhood accident, which is worth knowing when you look at how flat and diagrammatic some of his large compositions are. The small versions, painted from life, are far better than the enormous ones he later made for the Capitol rotunda in Washington, and this museum has the small ones.

Give it an hour if you have one. If you have twenty minutes, do this: the Kahn ceiling, the Night Cafe, and the Trumbull room, in that order, and then out.

When you come out, look across Chapel Street. The severe grey box on the other side, with no windows on the ground floor, is also Kahn. It was his last building, and he died before it opened. Crossing that street is crossing his whole career.
