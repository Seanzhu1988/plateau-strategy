This is the Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, finished in nineteen sixty three, by Gordon Bunshaft of Skidmore, Owings and Merrill, and it is one of the buildings people come to New Haven specifically to see.

Look at it from outside first. A rectangular box lifted clear of the ground on four corner piers, hovering over a sunken plaza, with a grid of grey Vermont marble panels in a steel and granite frame and not one window anywhere. From out here it reads as a sealed vault, which is more or less what it is.

Now go inside, and the building performs its trick.

The marble panels are cut to about one and a quarter inches thick, thin enough that daylight passes through the stone. From inside, the whole outer wall glows amber and grey and gold, with the veins in the marble showing like the grain in a leaf held up to a lamp, and the colour shifts with the weather outside. It is a windowless room full of daylight. Bunshaft chose the stone for exactly this and had samples tested for translucency.

The reason for no windows is the collection. Ultraviolet light destroys paper and ink, and this building holds around a million books and several million manuscripts, all of them rare. The marble filters out the damaging wavelengths and lets the rest through, so the reading room has natural light without the harm.

In the middle of the space stands a glass tower six storeys high, and inside it, on shelves, are one hundred and eighty thousand volumes, visible from every level. That is not the whole collection, most of which is below ground, but it is the display, and the effect is of a treasury with the door left open.

Two things in that tower and its cases are why people queue. Yale owns a Gutenberg Bible, one of about forty eight surviving copies and one of the first substantial books printed with movable type in Europe, around fourteen fifty five, and it is normally on show. And Yale owns a complete double elephant folio of John James Audubon's Birds of America, the enormous nineteenth century book with the birds printed life size, and a page of it is turned regularly.

The other famous object here is the Voynich Manuscript, a small illustrated codex in an unknown script that nobody has ever read, carbon dated to the early fifteenth century, full of plants that do not exist and diagrams of unclear purpose. Cryptographers from two world wars have failed on it. It is not always on display but Yale has published the whole thing online.

Outside, in the sunken courtyard you can see from the entrance, is a sculpture garden by Isamu Noguchi, made in nineteen sixty four: a white marble pyramid, a cube balanced on a point, and a disc, meant to suggest the sun, the earth and chance. You look down into it rather than walking in.

There is a piece of engineering under the plaza worth knowing about. The book vault below ground was originally built with a fire suppression system that would have flooded the room with carbon dioxide, and the story told for years was that it would have killed anyone inside to save the books. The system was replaced decades ago with one that does not, and Yale is now firm that the tale is exaggerated, but the fact that it was believed for so long tells you what people assume about a library that keeps its books behind glass and its readers behind a card.

Admission is free and the exhibition hall is open most days, though hours vary and the building closes for installation, so check before you come across town for it. Bags may need to go in a locker.

Stand in the middle for a moment before you leave and simply look at the walls. On a bright day the stone is orange and living; on a grey New Haven afternoon it is the colour of weak tea and the room feels like a lantern. It is a different building every time you visit, and it is doing that with stone and daylight and nothing else.

Come out and walk east, past the plaza, and the domed classical building ahead with the columns is the last stop.
