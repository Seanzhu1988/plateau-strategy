This is Connecticut Hall, and it is the oldest building in New Haven, finished in seventeen fifty two. Everything else you can see from here is younger by a century or more, and most of it by two.

Look at it and notice how out of place it is. Plain red brick, gambrel roof, small windows, no ornament, the shape of a large colonial house. Around it stand towers and buttresses and carved stone. Connecticut Hall was built when Yale had about a hundred students and no money, using funds the colony raised partly by a lottery and partly from the sale of a captured French ship. It is Georgian, and it is the only one left of the old brick row that used to stand along this side of the yard. The rest were pulled down in the eighteen nineties and early nineteen hundreds to make room for the Gothic buildings you are standing among, and the fight over whether to demolish this one too was long and close. It survived by a few votes and some money.

Students lived here for two centuries. Nathan Hale lived here, and so did his classmate and friend Benjamin Tallmadge, and the two of them are the reason this plain building is worth a stop.

Look at the statue in front of you. That is Hale, by Bela Lyon Pratt, nineteen thirteen. He was Yale class of seventeen seventy three, a schoolteacher from Coventry, Connecticut, who joined the Continental Army and volunteered for a mission behind British lines on Long Island in September seventeen seventy six. He had no training, no cover story worth the name, and he was caught within a week. The British hanged him the next morning without a trial. He was twenty one.

Now look at the statue's hands and feet. They are bound. That is the detail people miss, because from a distance he reads as a young man standing at ease, and up close he is a prisoner about to be executed. The sculptor made that choice deliberately, and it is why this is a better statue than most campus bronzes.

The line everyone knows, I only regret that I have but one life to lose for my country, was reported by a British officer who was present and wrote it down in his diary, and Hale probably was echoing a play by Joseph Addison that every educated person of his generation had read. Whether he said it exactly is unprovable. That he said something like it, calmly, at twenty one, in front of the men about to hang him, is as well attested as such things get.

His friend Tallmadge took a different lesson from it. Tallmadge became Washington's chief of intelligence and built the Culper spy ring in New York, the most successful American intelligence operation of the war, and he ran it with codes, aliases, invisible ink and dead drops, because he had watched what happened to a brave man sent out without any of those things.

The building is offices now, for the Faculty of Arts and Sciences, and you cannot go in. That is fine; the outside is the point, and so is the contrast. Stand with your back to the statue and look around this quadrangle at all the Gothic, and remember that the Gothic is the newcomer. This brick box was here for a hundred and fifty years before any of it.

Look once more at the gambrel roof, the one that changes angle halfway down. That shape was common in New England in the seventeen hundreds because it gave a usable attic without the cost of a full storey, and almost none of them survive on institutional buildings. When you see this roof line on a very plain brick box, you are almost always looking at something built before the Revolution.

There are copies of this Hale statue at the Central Intelligence Agency in Virginia and at the Department of Justice, which tells you what the country decided to make of a schoolteacher who was caught in a week.

Now walk out into the middle of the quadrangle, and look up at the tower to the west, the tall one with the carving all over it. That is where we are going, but first stand in the middle of this grass for a minute.
