This is the Yale Center for British Art, and it is Louis Kahn's last building. He designed it in the early nineteen seventies, and in March nineteen seventy four he died of a heart attack in a men's room at Pennsylvania Station in New York, coming back from Bangladesh, with his address torn out of his passport, and his body went unidentified for two days. The building was finished by his colleagues and opened in nineteen seventy seven.

Stand across the street first and look at the outside, because it is easy to walk past. It is a grey box of matt stainless steel panels and concrete frame, with shops at street level, and it deliberately does not announce itself. Kahn said he wanted the steel to look like a moth's wing on a cloudy day, which is exactly what it looks like on a cloudy day in New Haven, which is most of them.

Then go in, and the building opens. There are two interior courtyards, four storeys high, one after the other, and the whole place is finished in concrete, white oak and travertine, and lit from above through a roof of coffered skylights with baffles that cut the direct sun and leave a soft, even, changing daylight. Kahn cared more about natural light than any architect of his century. Look up in the Library Court and watch the light change if a cloud goes over; the pictures on the walls change with it.

Now the collection. It is the largest and most complete gathering of British art outside the United Kingdom: paintings, drawings, prints, rare books and sculpture, from the fifteenth century to the present, with the great strength in the age of Hogarth, Gainsborough, Reynolds, Stubbs, Constable and Turner. It was given, along with the money for the building and an endowment, by Paul Mellon, Yale class of nineteen twenty nine, whose father had made a banking fortune and who spent his life buying English pictures and horses. Mellon gave the whole thing away in nineteen sixty six.

Find the Stubbs. George Stubbs painted horses better than anyone ever has, and he did it by dissecting them for eighteen months in a barn in Lincolnshire and publishing the anatomy. The famous portrait of a horse standing alone against nothing at all is the kind of picture that ruins you for other animal paintings.

The hanging is unusual and worth understanding. The top floor is arranged as a long chronological run, and the walls are hung high, several rows deep in places, in the manner of an eighteenth century collector's house rather than a modern gallery. That is deliberate. Mellon's pictures were bought to live on walls like that.

And there is one practical thing that makes this building genuinely different: the Study Room. Anyone may ask, without an appointment in most cases, to see works on paper from the collection. A member of staff brings the drawings or prints out and puts them in front of you on a table. There is no fee and no requirement that you be a scholar.

One thing to notice about the ground floor before you go up. Kahn put shops along the street frontage, paying rent, with the museum entrance tucked between them. A museum director of that era would normally have demanded a grand entrance and a flight of steps. Kahn argued that a building on a city street owes the street a shopfront, and Mellon agreed. It is why this building does not look like a museum from outside, and why the block is still alive at street level.

Free admission, always, like the gallery across the road. Closed Mondays. The building went through a long closure for restoration and reopened in twenty twenty five, so if you have been before and remember it shut, it is open.

If the light is dull when you visit, come back on a different day rather than write the building off. Kahn designed the roof to hand you whatever the sky is doing, so the same room in June sun and February cloud are two different rooms, and people who have worked in it for years will tell you which they prefer. It is the only museum I know where the weather is part of the collection.

When you leave, walk north up High Street, back past the tower, and keep going. The long lawn on your right, with the low glass pavilion at the end of it, is the next stop, and there is a fountain on it that counts.
