This is Sterling Memorial Library, opened in nineteen thirty, by James Gamble Rogers, the same architect who aged Harkness Tower with acid. It is the main library of the university, it cost about eight million depression era dollars, and it is the most theatrical building at Yale.

Go through the doors and stop as soon as you are inside, in the entrance hall.

You are standing in a nave. That is not a loose comparison. The room has an arcade of stone piers, a high vaulted ceiling, aisles down the sides, and stained glass, and it is laid out exactly like a Gothic church. Rogers designed it that way on purpose, at a moment when American universities were building cathedrals of learning and meant the phrase seriously. Where the altar and the reredos would be, at the far end behind the desk, there is a painted panel, and in the place a church would put the Virgin, this building has a figure called Alma Mater, seated, holding a book and a tablet, with light around her.

Read the room for a minute. There are stained glass windows with scenes from Yale's history and from the history of scholarship. There are carvings everywhere, and the good ones are low down where you can see them: look at the small figures around the doorways and the corridor arches, because Rogers had his carvers put in students, scribes, monks copying manuscripts, a scholar asleep, and a man drinking. There are said to be more than five hundred carved details in this building, and hunting them is a game undergraduates play for four years.

Behind the reception desk rises the stack tower, sixteen levels of steel shelving, holding around four million volumes. It is not open to visitors and it is one of the great sights of American librarianship, a dense honeycomb of narrow aisles with low ceilings, lit by bare bulbs, where generations of Yale students have hidden to work. The whole Yale library system holds something over fifteen million volumes across its buildings, which puts it among the largest academic libraries in the world.

There are two more things in this building worth finding if you have twenty minutes and the doors are open. The Franklin Room and the reading rooms off the main corridor are decorated to the last inch, and the Music Library and the Map Collection have exhibition cases that change. Ask at the desk what is on show; the staff are used to visitors and will tell you what you may see.

And there is one detail I want you to look for above all, because it says everything about how this building was made. Somewhere in the entrance hall's stonework there is a carved telephone, and elsewhere a carved figure using a typewriter, and other small modern objects hidden in what is meant to look like fifteenth century work. The carvers put them there in nineteen thirty, and Rogers let them. A building faking six hundred years of age, with its own date smuggled into the ornament by the men holding the chisels.

It is worth saying what a building like this cost in nineteen thirty. The country was two years into the Depression, banks were failing, and Yale was finishing a library with hand carved stone by the mile and stained glass in the corridors. The money came from the estate of John William Sterling, a lawyer and alumnus who left the university roughly fifteen million dollars in nineteen eighteen, which was the largest gift any American university had received. It built this, and much else.

Visitors are welcome in the nave and the main corridor. The reading rooms and the stacks need a Yale card. Keep your voice down; people are working ten feet away.

One warning about the nave, in fairness to the people who work here. During reading period and examinations this building fills with students who have not slept, and a tour group talking at normal volume in the entrance hall is genuinely unwelcome. If you arrive in December or May, look, admire, and speak outside.

When you come out, turn right and walk east about a minute, and you will come to a building that looks like a windowless marble box on legs. It is the strangest building at Yale and possibly the most beautiful, and what is inside it is the reason it has no windows.
