This is Old Campus, and every first year student at Yale lives around this quadrangle. About fifteen hundred of them, in the buildings on all four sides, which is the reason the place has the particular noise it has in September and the particular silence it has in December.

Stand in the middle and turn slowly. What you are looking at is a single decision made in the eighteen nineties and carried out over forty years: that Yale would look old. Almost every building here is Collegiate Gothic, which is a nineteenth and twentieth century invention modelled on the medieval colleges of Oxford and Cambridge, and none of it is medieval. It is younger than the telephone. The stone was cut to look weathered, the leaded windows were made with irregular glass, and in the nineteen twenties the university went further than that, and I will show you exactly how much further when we get to the tower.

The statue of the seated man in the middle of the south side is Theodore Dwight Woolsey, president of Yale from eighteen forty six to eighteen seventy one, in bronze, by John Ferguson Weir. Look at his left shoe. It is polished bright, like the one in Harvard Yard, and for the same reason: students rub it for luck before examinations. Yale has the same tradition and the same problem with it, so photograph rather than touch.

Now, the thing that makes Yale structurally different from most American universities, and it starts here. At the end of their first year, students are sorted into residential colleges, of which there are fourteen. Each one is a walled building with its own courtyard, dining hall, library, common rooms, master and dean, and a student belongs to it for the rest of their time and, in a real sense, afterwards. It is copied from Oxford and Cambridge, and it was paid for in the nineteen thirties by Edward Harkness, an heir to the Standard Oil fortune, who offered the same money to Harvard first and then to Yale, and both took it. Harkness wanted the university broken into small human sized communities so that undergraduates would not be lost in it. That is why Yale's campus is a series of enclosed courtyards rather than open lawns: you are looking at a philosophy about loneliness, built in stone.

The trees here are worth a moment too. They are elms, or their descendants. New Haven called itself the Elm City, and in the nineteenth century it was famous for the tunnel of elms over its streets. Dutch elm disease killed most of them in the middle of the twentieth century, and the ones you see are replacements, many of them disease resistant hybrids planted in the last thirty years.

Notice the ground under your feet as you cross. Old Campus is gravel and paving rather than open lawn in most of its area, and that is because fifteen hundred people walk over it every hour of a term day. Grass does not survive that. The lawns that look immaculate at American universities are usually the ones nobody is allowed to cross.

There is one more thing to look at before we move. On the west side, set into the wall, is a memorial to the Yale men who died in the Civil War, and like Harvard's it names only the Union dead, of whom there were around fifty five. Yale had a large southern student body and lost men on the other side too. In nineteen fifteen Yale added a tablet naming Confederate dead as well, in the interest of reconciliation, and in twenty seventeen, in a different climate, that decision came up for review again. The university has been arguing about which of its dead to name for a hundred and fifty years.

One practical note about the colleges, since you will be walking past several. Each has a courtyard behind a gate, and the gates are usually locked, because students live in them. If a gate stands open and there is no sign, you may look in from the entry; do not walk into the courtyard. The one you can generally see into is Branford, under the tower, and it is the best of them anyway.

Leave through the passage in the north west corner, past the tower, and you come out onto High Street. Look up as you go. The tower ahead is two hundred and sixteen feet high, it has fifty four bells in it, and it was finished in nineteen twenty one.
