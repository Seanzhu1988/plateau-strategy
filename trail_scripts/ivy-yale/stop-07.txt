This lawn is Cross Campus, and it is the middle of Yale in the way that a village green is the middle of a village. Sterling Memorial Library closes the north end. The residential colleges run down both sides. Students cross it constantly, and in warm weather they lie on it in numbers.

Under your feet is a library. In nineteen seventy Yale built the Cross Campus Library underground, so that the lawn could stay a lawn, and in two thousand and seven it was rebuilt and renamed the Bass Library. The glass and stone pavilion at the west end is its entrance. It holds about a hundred and fifty thousand of the books undergraduates actually need, which is the collection Yale decided to put within thirty seconds of everybody.

Now find the fountain, because it is the reason I have stopped you here. On the north east corner of this lawn there is a low oval table of dark granite with water running over it. It is called the Women's Table, and it was made by Maya Lin, Yale class of nineteen eighty one, who as an undergraduate at this university won the competition to design the Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington while she was still twenty one. This is her memorial to something else.

Walk up to it and look at the surface through the water. There is a spiral of numbers cut into the stone, running outward from the centre, and each number is the count of women enrolled at Yale in a given year. Start at the middle and follow it out.

The first numbers are zeros. Yale was founded in seventeen oh one and the spiral begins there, and for a very long stretch of the spiral the number of women is zero, year after year, cut into granite one zero at a time. Then a number appears in the eighteen sixties and seventies, when women were admitted to the graduate school and the art school. Then the number stays small for decades. Then in nineteen sixty nine Yale College admitted women as undergraduates, and the numbers climb hard, and by the outer edge of the spiral they are in the thousands.

The design is doing something a plaque could not. The zeros take up as much stone as the thousands. You have to walk around the table reading nothing for a long time before the counting starts, and that emptiness is the memorial.

It was dedicated in nineteen ninety three, for the twenty fifth anniversary of coeducation in Yale College, and it was controversial when it was proposed, as Lin's Washington memorial had been. Water runs over the numbers continuously, which was also deliberate: she said she wanted people to touch it, and the water makes the figures legible in a way dry granite would not.

Sit on the edge for a minute if the weather allows. Students eat lunch on this rim without reading it, the way you walk past a war memorial in your own town.

One more thing about Maya Lin. She designed the Vietnam memorial as an undergraduate at Yale, in a class, and submitted it to a blind competition against more than fourteen hundred entries, and the design was attacked in Washington for years before it opened. She came back here a decade later to make this. Both works do the same thing: they use a list of names or numbers, cut into hard stone, and let the size of the list carry the whole argument.

Then look north at the library. From here you can see the whole of Sterling's front, and it is the largest building on this walk, and it was built to be mistaken for something else entirely. Fifteen storeys of books stand behind that facade in a tower, and the entrance hall was designed as a nave, with a high vaulted ceiling and stone arches, and where a church would put its altar this building puts the circulation desk. Whether that is reverence or a joke has been argued about since nineteen thirty.

Look at the buildings framing this lawn while you are here, because they are the residential colleges and they are the machine Harkness bought. Each one holds a few hundred students with their own dining hall and library, and a student is assigned to one before arriving and keeps it for life; alumni give to their college as much as to the university. Whether that produces community or a set of small tribes is a conversation you can start with any Yale student and not finish.

Walk up to it, and go in if the door is open, and I will show you what it does.
