This is Harkness Tower, two hundred and sixteen feet of stone, finished in nineteen twenty one, and it is the best place to explain what Yale did to itself in the nineteen twenties.

The architect was James Gamble Rogers, and the tower is the centrepiece of Memorial Quadrangle, which is now the two residential colleges of Branford and Saybrook. It was paid for by Anna Harkness in memory of her son Charles, and her other son Edward is the same Harkness who later paid for the whole residential college system. The model is the Boston Stump, the tower of St Botolph's in Lincolnshire, England, and Rogers made his version taller and thinner.

Now look at the stone closely, and this is the story worth carrying away from Yale. Rogers wanted his brand new tower to look like it had been standing since the fifteenth century, and he was systematic about it. The stone was deliberately cut in uneven courses. Acid was applied to the surfaces to eat them back. Workmen chipped edges to fake weathering, and in some places on this quadrangle, panes of glass were deliberately broken and mended with lead, so that the windows would look like they had survived four hundred years of thrown objects. Rogers is said to have supervised the aging personally.

You can decide for yourself whether that is charming or absurd, and both opinions are well represented in the architecture schools. What is not in dispute is that it worked. Visitors routinely assume this is a medieval building. It is younger than the aeroplane.

Look up at the carvings on the buttresses. There are eight figures, chosen to represent what a Yale man was meant to become: among them a scholar, a churchman, a statesman and a soldier, with Nathan Hale, Elihu Yale and Jonathan Edwards among the individuals honoured further down. Look at the lower level carvings too, around the archway into the courtyard, and you will find grotesques of students: one asleep over a book, one with a tankard, one playing a game. Rogers put the ideal on the tower and the reality at eye level.

Now the bells. Inside the tower is the Yale Memorial Carillon, fifty four bells, and they are rung by hand by students of the Yale University Guild of Carillonneurs, who audition in a fiercely competitive process each year and who play twice a day during term, usually around half past twelve and again in the early evening. It is a real instrument, played from a keyboard of wooden batons struck with the fists, and the sound falls over the whole of central New Haven. If you hear it while you are standing here, stop and listen to the end. They play everything: Bach, folk tunes, film themes, whatever the student on the bench feels like.

The Guild sometimes takes visitors up the tower for the playing, and if you can arrange it, do. The climb is a narrow spiral of a hundred and something steps, the playing room at the top is small and wooden, and you stand a few feet from the mechanism while somebody hammers out a tune above your head, and then you come out on the parapet with New Haven and Long Island Sound spread out below.

Below the tower is the archway into Branford College's courtyard, and if the gate is open you may walk in and stand in it. It is the prettiest enclosed space at Yale, ivy and stone and a small lawn, and it is exactly what Harkness was buying: an undergraduate's small world inside a large university.

There is one more thing about Rogers worth carrying with you. He built much of Yale between the wars, including the library we will come to, and his instinct was always theatrical: he wanted a student walking to a lecture to feel that they were inside something ancient and serious. Critics at the time called it dishonest, and the argument about whether a new building may pretend to be old has never really been settled. Stand under the arch and decide.

From here, walk south on High Street a couple of blocks, and on your left you will come to a long low building of stone and glass. It looks like nothing else on this walk, and the man who built it was thirty years ahead of the country.
