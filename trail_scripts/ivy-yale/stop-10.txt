This is Woolsey Hall, opened in nineteen oh one for the university's two hundredth anniversary, by Carrere and Hastings, the firm that built the New York Public Library. It is Yale's concert hall, and it seats about twenty seven hundred people, and it is a very different building from everything else you have seen today, because it is Beaux Arts classical rather than Gothic, from the brief moment before Yale decided it wanted to look medieval.

Go into the rotunda first, which is the domed entrance space, and this is the part to give time to.

The walls are marble, and cut into them are the names of the Yale men who died in war. The room was built as a memorial and it has been added to ever since: the Civil War, the Spanish American War, the First World War, the Second, Korea, Vietnam. Walk round it and read. The Second World War panel is the longest.

There is a thing about this room worth knowing. After the First World War, Yale, like Harvard, had to decide whether to name its dead who had fought on the other side, and Yale did. There is an inscription in the rotunda recording Yale men who died in the German and Austro Hungarian forces, placed there in the nineteen twenties in a spirit of reconciliation that seems remarkable now. When you have finished with the walls, look up into the dome.

Then the hall itself, if you can get in. The auditorium is a great fan of seats under a coffered ceiling, and behind the stage is the reason musicians make the trip: the Newberry Memorial Organ. It is one of the largest and most admired symphonic organs in the world, built by the Skinner Organ Company, rebuilt and enlarged in nineteen twenty eight, with around twelve thousand six hundred pipes ranging from the size of a pencil to thirty two feet. It was designed to imitate a symphony orchestra, which was the ambition of organ building in that decade, and it is one of the few great instruments of its type left unaltered. Organists come from other continents to play it.

The best way in is a concert, and many are free: the School of Music and the university orchestras perform here constantly during term, and there are organ recitals. If there is anything on the evening you are in New Haven, go, because the room and the instrument are worth more than whatever is on the programme.

One more thing about the organ, because it explains why organists care so much. Most great instruments of this age were rebuilt in the middle of the twentieth century, when fashion turned against the orchestral sound and toward a brighter, older, baroque style, and thousands of pipes were thrown away in the process. Yale left this one alone, largely for lack of money at the right moment, and so it survives as a complete example of a way of building organs that ended in about nineteen thirty. Poverty is the great preservationist.

Now, this is the end of the walk, so stand outside on the corner of College and Grove for a moment and look back the way you came. In under a mile you have gone from a seventeenth century public green, through a gate, past the only building left of the colonial college, under a tower made to look four hundred years older than it is, past the first and last buildings of one of the great American architects, across a lawn with a fountain that counts the years women were not here, into a library built like a church, and into a marble box with daylight coming through the walls.

That is the argument this university makes about itself, and it is all made in stone: that it is old, that it is serious, and that it means to last. Most of the stone is younger than the aeroplane.

If you have another hour, the Yale Peabody Museum of Natural History is ten minutes north of here and has the dinosaur that most of the world pictures when it thinks of a brontosaurus, along with a sixty foot mural of the age of reptiles. If you have an evening, go and eat the pizza on Wooster Street. Thank you for walking it with me.
