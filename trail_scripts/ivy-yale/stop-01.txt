Start on the New Haven Green, on the grass, facing the row of buildings across College Street, because Yale and this city are wrapped around each other and you should see the join before you go through it.

The Green is sixteen acres and it was laid out in sixteen thirty eight, when the Puritans who founded New Haven drew nine equal squares on the marsh and kept the middle one for the public. It is one of the oldest public squares in the country and it has three churches standing on it in a row, which is unusual and worth a look: Center Church, Trinity, and United. Under Center Church there is a crypt with several thousand graves in it, because the Green was the town burial ground until eighteen twenty one, when the stones were moved to a new cemetery and the bodies were not.

Now turn and look at the university. The archway in front of you, in the long stone building with the towers, is Phelps Gate, and it is the front door of Yale College. The building over it is Phelps Hall, from eighteen ninety six.

Here is what to know before you walk through. Yale was founded in seventeen oh one, by ten Congregational ministers, and the founding story is that each of them put books on a table and said, I give these books for the founding of a college in this colony. It began in Killingworth, moved to Saybrook, and came to New Haven in seventeen sixteen after an argument so bitter that when the Saybrook books were carted here, some of them went missing on the road and bridges were mysteriously broken. It was named Yale in seventeen eighteen after Elihu Yale, a merchant who had made a fortune with the East India Company in Madras and who sent the new school nine bales of goods, four hundred and seventeen books and a portrait of King George. The goods were sold for about five hundred and sixty two pounds. That was the naming price.

You should know the other half of Elihu Yale, because the university now says it out loud. He was an administrator of a colonial trading company in India, he governed Madras, he was removed from that post over accusations of corruption and self dealing, and he profited from the slave trade that the company was engaged in. Yale has not renamed itself, but it has stopped telling the story as a simple one, and in twenty twenty it began publishing research into its own founding money.

Now the gate itself. Walk through the arch slowly. Yale students go through this arch twice in a formal sense: in during their first days, at the ceremony that opens the year, and out at commencement, and in between they go through it constantly, because it is the shortest way to the Green and the pizza.

And the pizza is a real thing here, not a joke. New Haven has a style of its own, called apizza, thin, charred, coal fired, and the two famous places are on Wooster Street about fifteen minutes east of where you are standing. If you have an evening in this city, that is what to do with it.

One more thing about the Green while you can still see it. Look at how the three churches sit in a row down the middle. That arrangement is a seventeenth century idea of a town: the meeting house at the centre, everything else around it. New Haven's nine square plan was one of the first planned towns in North America, and the grid you will walk in this afternoon is the one drawn on a marsh in sixteen thirty eight, which makes the street pattern older than the university by sixty three years.

Through the arch is Old Campus, a green quadrangle of dormitories where every first year student lives. Stop just inside and let your eyes adjust, because you have gone from a public square in a working city into a walled garden in about four steps, and that transition is what the whole architecture of this place is arranged to produce.

The plain brick building on the far side, older and lower than everything around it, is where we are going next. It is the only survivor of the college's first buildings.
