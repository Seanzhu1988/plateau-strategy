This is Nassau Hall, finished in seventeen fifty six, and when it was built it was the largest stone building in the American colonies. It is named for William of Orange, of the House of Nassau, and for its first fifty years it was the whole College of New Jersey.

Take the outside first. Two hundred feet long, three storeys of local sandstone, plain sash windows in rows, a modest cupola. The architect was Robert Smith, the Philadelphia builder who also built Carpenters' Hall, where the First Continental Congress met, so the same man's work stands at the centre of two different stories about the founding of the country.

Look at the walls closely and you will see that they have been through fire twice, in eighteen oh two and eighteen fifty five, which is why the interior is entirely nineteenth century behind eighteenth century walls. The stone is original. Almost nothing inside is.

Now the history, and it is a great deal for one building.

In January seventeen seventy seven, Washington fought the Battle of Princeton on the fields south west of here, having crossed the Delaware and beaten the garrison at Trenton a week before. British soldiers took shelter inside Nassau Hall, and American artillery fired on it. A cannonball is said to have gone through a window of the prayer hall and taken the head off a portrait of King George the Second. The British surrendered inside the building. Look at the west end wall as you walk round, where there is a scar in the stone that is pointed out as cannon damage.

Then, from June to November seventeen eighty three, this building was the capitol of the United States. The Continental Congress had fled Philadelphia after mutinous unpaid soldiers surrounded the State House, and it reconvened here, in the library room upstairs, and sat for four months. During that time Congress received the news that the Treaty of Paris had been signed and the war was over. George Washington came here in August and was formally thanked by Congress in this building, and he stayed nearby for the autumn.

So Princeton, for four months, was the capital of the country, and this hall was the seat of its government.

Now look at the tigers on either side of the front steps. They are bronze, they were given in nineteen eleven, and they replaced two lions that had stood here before. Princeton's colours are orange and black for the House of Orange, and the tiger became the mascot in the eighteen eighties, apparently because the striped jerseys the teams wore looked like tigers to the crowds.

Inside, on the ground floor, is the Faculty Room, which is the old prayer hall, panelled, hung with portraits of the college's presidents, with a portrait of Washington by Charles Willson Peale at the front. That painting has its own story: it was painted in seventeen eighty four to fill the frame the beheaded George the Second had occupied, and Peale put the Battle of Princeton in the background. Congress paid for it. It hangs in the frame the King's portrait was in.

The Faculty Room is sometimes open to visitors and is often not, because the trustees and the faculty use it. Ask at the door; if it is open, go in and look at the frame.

One more detail on the outside before you walk round. Look at the roofline and the cupola. Neither is original. The building burned in eighteen oh two, and again in eighteen fifty five, and it was rebuilt each time in the fashion of the moment: after the first fire Benjamin Latrobe, who worked on the Capitol in Washington, put it back, and after the second John Notman added the towers at the ends and the present cupola in an Italianate manner. So the plain colonial box you are looking at is a colonial box with two layers of nineteenth century repair on it, which is honest enough once you know.

One more thing about the building's later life. Nassau Hall is now the administration building, with the president's office in it, and like Massachusetts Hall at Harvard that makes it the place where the university's arguments arrive. Students have occupied it more than once, most famously in nineteen sixty nine over the university's investments in South Africa, and there is a long tradition of the graduating class walking out of its front door on commencement day. The building has been the college, the capitol, a barracks, a hospital, a library and an office, which is a fair record for two hundred and seventy years.

Then walk round the building to the back, and the lawn on the far side is where we are going.
