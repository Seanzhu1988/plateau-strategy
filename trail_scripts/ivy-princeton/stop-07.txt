This is the Princeton University Chapel, finished in nineteen twenty eight, and by most counts it is the third largest university chapel in the world. It is two hundred and seventy feet long, the nave is seventy six feet high, and it seats around two thousand people.

The architect was Ralph Adams Cram, who was the great American Gothic revivalist of his generation, and who was Princeton's supervising architect. He also worked on the Cathedral of Saint John the Divine in New York. Cram believed, and wrote at length, that the Gothic was the only proper architecture for a Christian university, and that modern building was a spiritual failure. He got to prove it here.

Go in. That is the whole of this stop: go in and sit down at the back and give your eyes two minutes to adjust.

What you are in is a full scale medieval cathedral built in the nineteen twenties out of Pennsylvania sandstone and Indiana limestone, with a stone vault overhead, not a plaster imitation. The windows are the thing. There are around ten thousand square feet of stained glass, made by the best studios then working, in England and America, and the scheme runs from the Bible through the history of learning: there are windows for the four great teachers of the church, for Dante and Milton, for the university itself. The great west window is enormous.

Look at the woodwork of the choir stalls in the middle. It is oak from Sherwood Forest in England, brought over for the purpose, which is the kind of decision a man like Cram made without embarrassment.

Now, the history that makes this building more than a very good imitation. Princeton was founded by Presbyterians to train ministers, and for two centuries chapel attendance was compulsory, daily, and enforced. The chapel that stood before this one burned in nineteen twenty. When Princeton rebuilt, it built the largest chapel it could, at exactly the moment when the university was becoming secular and compulsory attendance was on its way out. It was abolished in the nineteen sixties.

So this is a colossal act of faith in stone, finished just as the thing it was built for was ending. It is now used for services of many faiths, for concerts, for memorials, and for the university's own ceremonies, and it is open to anyone during the day.

There are two practical things. First, the organs. There is a large Aeolian Skinner instrument and a smaller mechanical action organ, and there are free recitals; if you hear one starting, stay. Second, the acoustics are extraordinary and a whisper carries, so if there is anyone praying or practising, whisper or be silent.

Sit at the back for a moment more and look down the length of it. When you leave, continue south for about two minutes toward Prospect House, the pale Italianate villa set behind its garden. After this immense stone interior, the next stop is deliberately domestic.

There is a practical note about the chapel that catches people out. It is used constantly: services on Sunday, memorials, weddings, rehearsals, and the university's own ceremonies, so on any given day the doors may be shut for an hour. If you find it closed, come back later in the day rather than crossing it off; it is worth the second trip, and it is the single most impressive interior at Princeton.

One more number for scale, since people always want to know how it compares. The chapel seats about two thousand, which is more than the entire undergraduate body of many colleges, and it was built for a university of around twenty two hundred students. In other words, Princeton built a church that would hold every single person who studied here at once, and required them to attend. That is the clearest statement anyone could make about what a university thought it was for in nineteen twenty eight, and it is why the building feels oversized today.

One last look at the outside as you go. Cram's chapel is deliberately not aligned with the rest of the campus grid; it stands at its own angle, oriented so that the altar end faces east, as a medieval church must. He would not compromise on that even though it makes the building sit awkwardly among its neighbours. If you want to know what a true believer's architecture looks like, it looks like a building that will not turn to fit the path.
