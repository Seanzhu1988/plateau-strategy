This is Cannon Green, the lawn behind Nassau Hall, and it is named for the thing buried in the middle of it. Walk to the centre and look down. There is a cannon barrel sunk vertically into the ground, muzzle first, with a few feet of it standing above the grass, set in concrete.

Here is why a university would bury a cannon upside down in its main lawn.

The gun is from the Revolution, left in Princeton after the battle of seventeen seventy seven, and it lay about the town for decades. During the War of eighteen twelve it was hauled to New Brunswick and then, afterwards, students from Princeton hauled it back, and it became college property in the way that such things do. Then, in eighteen seventy five, students from Rutgers came down the road and took it. They believed, wrongly, that it had been theirs. Princeton students went to New Brunswick and took it back. The two colleges are about eighteen miles apart, they had played the first ever intercollegiate football game against each other six years earlier in eighteen sixty nine, and they were in the mood for this sort of thing.

To end it, Princeton sank the cannon in concrete, muzzle down, where you are standing. It has not moved since. There is a second, smaller cannon buried the same way behind Whig Hall, over to your right, which is the one Rutgers took in the first place, or the one Rutgers thought it was taking, depending on which account you read.

Now look around this lawn, because it is the ceremonial heart of the campus. Commencement was held here for many years. The class of each year gathers here. And after certain victories, particularly in football against Yale and Harvard in the same season, students build a bonfire on this grass, with the permission of the university and the attendance of the fire department. That has happened rarely enough in recent decades that when it does the whole town comes.

The buildings around you are worth naming. The two matching classical temples on the south side, white and columned, are Whig Hall and Clio Hall, and they are the homes of the American Whig Cliosophic Society, which is the oldest college political and debating society in the country, founded in the seventeen sixties, with James Madison and Aaron Burr among its early members. Madison and Burr were classmates here, in the same small college, and went on to be respectively the fourth president and the vice president who shot Alexander Hamilton.

Whig Hall has a story of its own you can see from here. It burned in nineteen sixty nine, and rather than rebuild the temple exactly, the university had the architect Gwathmey Siegel insert a modern concrete and glass structure inside the surviving classical shell. Walk round to look at the side and you will see the join: a Greek temple front with an unmistakably nineteen seventies building visible through and behind it. People argue about it. It is at least honest, which is more than can be said for the acid aged stonework at some of the other places on this tour.

East of here, the path leads to a group of older buildings and then to the chapel. Before you leave the green, look back at the rear of Nassau Hall and notice that this side, the college side, is plainer than the front. The front faced the road and the town and was meant to impress travellers on the Philadelphia to New York run. The back faced the students.

Before you go, look at the ground on the far side of the green toward the west, and know that the battle was fought about a mile from here, in fields that are now a state park. Washington rode across that ground in January seventeen seventy seven and turned a losing campaign around in ten days. If you have a car and an afternoon, the Princeton Battlefield is worth the short drive; there is a colonnade there and an oak that stood through the fighting until it fell in two thousand.

Take the path east, past the ivy covered building with the arches, and the very large church ahead of you is next.
