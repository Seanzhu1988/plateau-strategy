These two buildings, standing together east of Nassau Hall, are Chancellor Green and East Pyne, and they are the prettiest small spaces at Princeton. Almost everybody walks through them on the way across campus.

Chancellor Green is the octagonal one, built in eighteen seventy three, and it was the university's library. It is by William Appleton Potter, in Venetian Gothic, which means banded stone, pointed windows and a great deal of colour, and it was paid for by John Green in memory of his brother, the Chancellor of New Jersey.

Go inside. It is a cafe and reading room now, and anyone may walk in. Stand under the middle and look up: an octagon rising into a stained glass dome, with iron columns and galleries around the sides. It held the college's entire book collection for fifty years, and by nineteen twenty the books had outgrown it entirely, which is why it is now a place to drink coffee under a rose window.

That is a thing worth noticing across all these campuses. Every one of them has a building that was the library and is now something else, because collections double and buildings do not. Harvard has Gore Hall, gone entirely. Yale has Dwight Hall, now a chapel. Princeton has this, filled with students and espresso.

Now walk through to East Pyne, the building attached to it, from eighteen ninety seven, by Potter again, built for the college's hundred and fiftieth anniversary. Go under the arch into the courtyard in the middle, and this is the stop.

It is a small enclosed quadrangle, stone, with ivy and arched openings on all four sides and a single tree. It is the closest thing at Princeton to an Oxford or Cambridge court, and it is quiet in a way the rest of the campus is not, because you have to go through an arch to get into it and people do not wander in.

In the passageway there are two statues, of John Witherspoon and James McCosh, both presidents of the college. Witherspoon is worth two sentences. He was a Scottish minister brought over in seventeen sixty eight to run the place, he taught James Madison, and he was the only active clergyman to sign the Declaration of Independence. He is a large presence in American history for a man who ran a small college in New Jersey, and the reason is that he taught the generation that wrote the constitution: something like a fifth of the members of the Constitutional Convention had studied here under him.

McCosh, the other one, ran the college from eighteen sixty eight to eighteen eighty eight and transformed it, doubling its size and building most of the campus you are walking through.

There is one more thing about Witherspoon worth carrying with you, since his statue is here. He arrived from Scotland to run a small colonial college and within eight years he was signing the Declaration of Independence, and he did it as a clergyman, which nobody else in that room did. He also brought the Scottish Enlightenment with him in his luggage: the philosophy he taught, common sense realism, is all over the founding documents, because the men who wrote them had sat in his classroom in this village and taken notes.

The two buildings you are standing between are a good place to notice how a campus grows. Chancellor Green in eighteen seventy three, East Pyne in eighteen ninety seven, joined together, one Venetian and one English, built twenty four years apart by the same architect who had changed his mind about what a university should look like in between.

Look up at the stonework of East Pyne as you leave the courtyard, at the gargoyles and the carved heads on the corners. Cope and Stewardson and Potter both used real hand carving, and the carvers were given latitude, so the figures on these buildings include grotesques, animals, and small jokes that nobody commissioned by name. Every old campus on this tour has them, always high up and always worth the crick in the neck.

Sit in this courtyard for a minute if it is empty. Then leave on the north-east side and follow the path for about two minutes toward the low Gothic building beyond the lawn. That is Firestone Library. It looks only three storeys high, but most of the building is hidden under your feet.
