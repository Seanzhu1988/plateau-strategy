This is Alexander Hall, from eighteen ninety four, and it is the oddest looking building at Princeton and worth ending on, because it is the one that was hated the longest.

Look at it. Romanesque, which means round arches rather than pointed ones, in yellow and grey stone with bands of colour, with a great arched entrance, squat towers and a huge semicircular apse, and across the front a carved stone frieze of figures. The architect was William Appleton Potter, who also built Chancellor Green that you saw earlier, and the money came from Harriet Crocker Alexander, of the California railway family, in memory of her husband's family.

Now look at the frieze above the entrance. It is a procession of figures representing learning, carved in stone, and at the centre is a seated figure of Learning enthroned with the arts and sciences bringing their gifts. It is nineteenth century allegory of the most earnest kind and it repays a close look, particularly the individual figures at the ends.

Inside is Richardson Auditorium, a horseshoe hall seating about nine hundred, with an organ, and it is where the university's orchestras and choirs perform, and where visiting speakers and lecturers appear. It is a good room to hear music in. Concerts are the way to see the inside, and many during term are free.

Here is the part worth knowing. For most of the twentieth century, this building was considered an embarrassment. When Collegiate Gothic took over Princeton after Blair Hall, Alexander looked wrong: too heavy, too colourful, too Victorian, standing at an angle to everything else, and there were proposals to demolish it or reface it. It was called ugly in print by people who worked here. Then taste turned again, as it always does, and it is now protected and admired, and the carving is treated as one of the university's treasures.

That is the note to end this walk on, because you have just walked through four different centuries of people building what they thought a university should look like, each convinced the last lot had got it wrong. A plain stone barrack in seventeen fifty six because there was no money and no time. A Victorian octagon with a stained glass dome in eighteen seventy three because the age wanted colour. This, in eighteen ninety four, heavy and Romanesque. Then Blair in eighteen ninety seven and everything after it pretending to be English and medieval. Then a concrete and glass insertion inside a burned Greek temple in the nineteen seventies. Then Adjaye's museum in the twenty twenties.

None of them agreed, and they are all still standing within ten minutes of each other, which is the best thing about walking an old campus.

One practical thing before you go, if you have any time left. The eating clubs are on Prospect Avenue, five minutes east of here: eleven large houses, some of them very grand, which are private clubs that most upperclass students join for their meals and their social life. They are not university buildings and you cannot go in, but the street is public and the architecture is worth the walk, and they are the institution Woodrow Wilson tried and failed to abolish, still standing, still arguing about who gets in.

Look once more at how the building sits. Alexander Hall stands at an angle to the buildings around it and close to the road, on a site that a later generation would have kept clear. That awkwardness is why it was disliked, and it is also why it is unmistakable: you cannot mistake it for anything else on this campus, whereas the Gothic buildings of the nineteen twenties can be hard to tell apart even for people who studied here.

There is one more thing about the frieze worth a close look. The figures were carved by hand in the eighteen nineties, and the individual heads at the outer ends are portraits rather than types. Somebody sat for them.

From here, Nassau Street and the town are a few minutes north through the gate you came in by, and you may walk out through the middle of it, since you are not a student and nothing bad will happen. The Dinky, if you came by train, is south. Thank you for walking it with me.
