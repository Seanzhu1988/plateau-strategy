A small warning about this stop before you reach it. Prospect House is a working faculty club in the middle of a working campus, and there is often an event on. The garden is normally open to walk through in daylight, and occasionally it is roped off for a lunch or a wedding. If it is, look from the path; you will still see the shape of it.

This is Prospect House, and it is a mid nineteenth century Italianate villa sitting in the middle of a university, with a walled garden behind it, and it is the most domestic thing on this walk.

It was built in eighteen fifty one for a Princeton family, and the college bought it in eighteen seventy eight to be the president's house. Presidents lived here for ninety years, until nineteen sixty eight, and it is now the faculty club, which means the ground floor is a dining room for professors and the outside is yours to walk around.

The reason to stop is the man who lived here between nineteen oh two and nineteen ten and what happened next. Woodrow Wilson was president of Princeton before he was governor of New Jersey, and governor before he was President of the United States, and he lived in this house through the Princeton years with his wife Ellen and their three daughters.

Walk round the back to the garden. Ellen Axson Wilson laid it out. She was a trained painter who had studied in New York and who kept painting all her life, and she designed this garden in the shape it still holds, with the beds arranged formally and planted for colour through the season. She later did the same at the White House, where she designed the rose garden in nineteen thirteen, the ancestor of the one that is there now. So the garden you are standing in is the first draft of the most photographed garden in America.

Ellen Wilson died in the White House in nineteen fourteen, of kidney disease, seventeen months into her husband's presidency.

Now, Wilson at Princeton. He reformed the teaching, bringing in the preceptorial system of small group tuition that Princeton still uses and is proud of. He fought and lost a bitter battle over the eating clubs, the private dining clubs on Prospect Avenue that dominated undergraduate social life and that he thought were snobbish and divisive; he wanted residential colleges instead, and the alumni defeated him. He left in nineteen ten for politics.

He also, and this is the part the university now states plainly, was a segregationist. As President of the United States he permitted and presided over the re segregation of the federal civil service, which put thousands of Black federal employees out of work or into separate and worse conditions. As president of Princeton he did not admit Black students, in an era when Harvard and Yale did, and discouraged those who applied. In twenty twenty Princeton removed his name from its School of Public and International Affairs and from one of its residential colleges, after years of argument and an earlier decision to keep it.

That is worth standing with for a moment, in his garden. The building is not a shrine and the university is not pretending the man was simple.

The garden is open to walk through in daylight. The house is the faculty club and not open to visitors. There is often a wedding marquee up in summer.

One more thing to look at in the garden itself. There are old trees here, including a large tulip poplar, and the walled beds are still planted to Ellen Wilson's plan in outline. Gardens are the least permanent thing a university owns, because they need somebody to weed them every year for a century, and the fact that this one survives in recognisable shape is a small act of institutional stubbornness.

There is a second reason to walk round the back, which is the view. From the terrace behind the house you are looking south over lawns toward the newer part of the campus, and this is the highest ground on the walk. The house is called Prospect for that reason: it was built here for the view before there was a university around it, and the university grew up the hill toward it.

From here, walk north west across the campus, and you will come out at a long Gothic dormitory with a great arch through it and a flight of steps under the arch. That is the next stop, and it used to lead to a railway.
