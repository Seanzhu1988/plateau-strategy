This is FitzRandolph Gate, the main entrance to the campus from Nassau Street, and it was given in nineteen oh five in memory of Nathaniel FitzRandolph, the local farmer who in seventeen fifty three gave the land the college was built on. The eagles on top of the piers are the FitzRandolph family emblem.

Now the rule, and it is the best known piece of student lore at Princeton. Undergraduates do not walk out through the centre of this gate until the day they graduate. Walking in is fine. Walking out through the middle before commencement is said to mean you will not graduate, so students detour to the side openings, for four years, in all weathers. On commencement day the whole graduating class walks out through the centre together, in a procession, and that is the moment the university is arranged around.

Watch for a minute and you will see people step round it without appearing to think.

Here is the thing that makes the gate more interesting than the superstition. For most of its life this gate was locked shut. It was closed except for ceremonies, and the fence and gate were a real barrier between the town and the college, which is exactly what a nineteenth century American university wanted. Then in nineteen seventy, at the request of the class of nineteen seventy, the university opened it permanently and it has stood open ever since. The class asked for it as a statement: that the university should not be a closed institution in a town it dominated. There is an inscription about it on the gate.

So the gate now means two opposite things at once. It is permanently open as a promise that anyone may walk in, and students refuse to walk out of it as a private ritual. Both are taken seriously.

Walk through the middle, because you are a visitor and the rule does not bind you, and stop just inside.

You are now at the top of the front campus lawn, and the building at the far end, the long grey stone one with the cupola, is Nassau Hall, which is the reason Princeton is on the map of American history rather than only of American education. Take the path toward it slowly.

Notice the trees on your way. Princeton's campus has been planted deliberately for two hundred years, and there are specimen trees here that arboriculturists come to see. The two enormous sycamores flanking the path just inside the gate are the Stamp Act sycamores, said to have been planted in seventeen sixty five to mark the repeal of the Stamp Act, which if true makes them older than the country and the only living things on this walk that were here for the Revolution.

Look at Nassau Hall as you approach and notice what it is not. It is not Gothic, it is not columned, it has almost no ornament, and it looks more like a very large barracks than a university building. That is because it was one building that had to be the entire college: dormitory, classrooms, chapel, library, kitchen and dining hall, for a school with no money in a village in seventeen fifty six.

While you are standing here, look left and right along Nassau Street at the wall. It runs the length of the campus front, low, in local stone, and it is the only edge the university has: everywhere else the campus simply becomes the town without a boundary. A university that had a wall built and then a class asking for its main gate to be opened forever is a university arguing with itself about how separate it wants to be, and that argument is a hundred years old and is not settled.

There is one more thing to notice about the front campus as you walk down it. It is a lawn, open, with paths across it and nothing built on it, and it has been kept that way deliberately for two centuries: every proposal to put a building on this grass has been refused. What you are looking at is the view of Nassau Hall that a traveller on the Philadelphia road would have had in seventeen sixty, preserved on purpose, and it is the reason the university still photographs well from the street.

Stop at the two bronze tigers by the front steps.
