Start on Palmer Square, on the green, with the shops around you and the Nassau Inn behind, because this is the town and in about ninety seconds you are going to walk into the university, and the border between them is one street.

Palmer Square is not old. It was built in the nineteen thirties by Edgar Palmer, a zinc company heir and Princeton alumnus, who bought up a stretch of the town and had it rebuilt in a colonial revival style to look like a New England village green. Buildings that were genuinely eighteenth century were pulled down to make room for new buildings that looked eighteenth century. It is charming and it is a stage set, and knowing that is the right way to start a walk through a place where a great deal of the architecture is doing the same thing.

The Nassau Inn is the exception with real history behind it, in that an inn has stood at this crossroads since seventeen fifty six, though the building you see is from the nineteen thirties as well. Inside the Tap Room there is a wooden panel carved with initials by generations of students, and a Norman Rockwell mural of Yankee Doodle painted in nineteen thirty seven.

Now, the town. Princeton is small, about thirty thousand people, and it is one of the wealthiest places in America, and the university is the reason for both. It sits halfway between New York and Philadelphia, which mattered enormously in the eighteenth century, when the College of New Jersey moved here in seventeen fifty six precisely because it was the midpoint of the road between the two largest cities in the colonies.

The college was founded in seventeen forty six, in Elizabeth, by Presbyterians of the evangelical wing, who wanted a school to train ministers of their own persuasion because Yale and Harvard were run by people they disagreed with. It moved to Newark, then here. It was called the College of New Jersey for a hundred and fifty years and only became Princeton University in eighteen ninety six.

Cross Nassau Street when the light allows, and look at the wall and the iron gate ahead of you as you approach. That gate is the front door of the university and there is a rule about it that students obey.

One practical thing about this walk before we go in. Princeton's campus is large and green and open, with no wall around most of it, and the buildings are scattered through lawns and trees rather than packed into a quadrangle. It is beautiful and it is easy to get turned around. The route we are taking runs roughly east from the gate to the chapel, then south to the museum, then west and back, and it is about a mile in total with plenty of grass to sit on.

And the campus is genuinely open to the public. You may walk anywhere outdoors. The buildings mostly need a card. The art museum, when it is open, is free, and so is the chapel.

One more thing to know before you cross, because it explains the shape of the day. Princeton has no medical school, no law school and no business school. It is an undergraduate college with a graduate school attached, and it is much smaller than Harvard or Yale: about fifty five hundred undergraduates in total. That is why the campus feels like a single place rather than a city of schools, and why you can genuinely walk the whole of the important part in an hour.

And one warning about term dates. In the summer the campus is beautiful and empty and slightly dead. In term it is full of people and everything is open. If you have a choice, come when the students are here.

A word about the name of the town, because visitors ask. Princeton was named around seventeen twenty four, well before the college arrived, and nobody is certain for whom: possibly for Prince William, possibly for a local landowner called Prince, possibly simply because it sounded well. The college took the town's name a hundred and seventy years later, not the other way about, and for most of its life the institution was called the College of New Jersey while everybody in the country called it Princeton anyway. The university eventually gave in to what people were already saying, which is how most institutions get their names.

Cross now, and stop in front of the gate.
