This is Blair Hall, from eighteen ninety seven, and it is the building that decided what Princeton would look like for the next fifty years.

Look at it: a long range of grey stone with towers, oriel windows, battlements, and in the middle a great arch with a broad flight of steps running up through it. This is Collegiate Gothic, and Blair was the first full blooded example of it at Princeton, by the architects Cope and Stewardson of Philadelphia, and its success is why almost every building put up here between eighteen ninety seven and the nineteen thirties is in the same manner. Yale and Chicago and Penn did the same thing at the same time, and the American idea of what a university ought to look like was fixed in about twenty years by half a dozen firms copying English colleges.

Now walk up into the arch and stand at the top of the steps looking outward, west, and picture what is not there.

Until nineteen eighteen the railway came right to the bottom of these steps. The Princeton branch line ran up to a station that stood just below where you are standing, and this arch and this staircase were built as the ceremonial entrance to the university for anyone arriving by train. You got off, you looked up, and there was the arch and the towers, and you climbed the steps into the college. It was a deliberate piece of theatre.

The line was cut back in nineteen eighteen and the station moved south, and it moved again in the nineteen sixties, and today the shuttle train from the main line, universally known as the Dinky, runs to a station about half a mile south of here. It is one of the shortest scheduled railway lines in the country, two and a half miles, one stop, and generations of students have arrived and left on it.

So these steps now go down to a lawn. The building was designed around an arrival that no longer happens, which is a thing you see all over old universities once you start looking.

The arch itself has a second life. The acoustics under it are very good, and Princeton's a cappella singing groups perform here, standing on the steps, in what are called arch sings. They happen on evenings during term, they are free, and anyone may stand and listen. If you are here on a warm evening and you hear singing, follow it.

Look up at the tower over the arch as you go through, and at the carved detail: shields, grotesques, small figures. Cope and Stewardson gave their buildings the same hand carved ornament the medieval originals had, and it was expensive, and it is why these buildings still repay looking at from three feet away rather than only from across a lawn.

Look at the stone as you stand in the arch. Cope and Stewardson used a local argillite, a grey green stone quarried nearby, laid in rough irregular courses with the pointing raked back so the shadows are deep. That texture is the whole trick of Collegiate Gothic: it makes a building put up in eighteen ninety seven look as though it has been rained on since fourteen hundred, and unlike Yale nobody had to attack it with acid to get there. The stone simply is that colour.

There is a second arch worth finding a hundred yards further on, in the building called Holder Hall, with a tower over it, from nineteen ten. It is the other great arch at Princeton and it is where the other singing groups perform.

One more thing about the Dinky, since it is part of student life rather than a footnote. It is a single carriage shuttle that runs the two and a half miles to Princeton Junction on the main line, and it has been threatened with closure repeatedly and defended fiercely each time. Students, faculty and townspeople have petitioned, protested and sued over it. A university town's relationship with its railway is not sentimental; it is the difference between being an hour from New York and being nowhere.

From here, walk north east along the front of the building and you come to a large Romanesque hall with a carved frieze across the front, standing on its own. That is the last stop.
