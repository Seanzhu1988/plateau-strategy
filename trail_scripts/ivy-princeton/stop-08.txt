This is the Princeton University Art Museum, and if you are standing in front of a building that looks brand new, that is because it is. The old museum on this site was taken down in twenty twenty one and a completely new one, designed by the architect David Adjaye, opened here. Check the museum's own page for hours before you make the walk, because a new building's schedule settles slowly.

The collection is the reason to come, and it is much better than most people expect from a university this size: around a hundred and ten thousand objects, from ancient Mediterranean and Byzantine work, through European painting, to Chinese bronzes and painting, to a strong holding of art of the Americas, and photographs in real depth.

Princeton has been collecting since eighteen eighty two, and the character of the collection comes from that: it was assembled for teaching, object by object, by professors who wanted particular things in front of particular classes, rather than bought wholesale from one collector's estate. So it is uneven in the way a working teaching collection is, with astonishing individual objects sitting next to modest ones.

The things to find, if they are on view: the Byzantine mosaics from Antioch, excavated by Princeton archaeologists in the nineteen thirties, floor mosaics from Roman houses in what is now Turkey, and among the best of their kind outside the region. The Chinese painting collection, which is one of the finest in the west. And a small number of very good European pictures, including work by Monet, Cezanne and Van Gogh.

There is one practical fact that matters more than any of that: admission is free.

Now, whether or not the museum is open, do this before you move on. Walk around the outside of the building and look at the campus sculpture. Princeton has one of the best collections of outdoor modern sculpture on any American campus, given largely by John B. Putnam Junior, class of nineteen forty five, who was killed flying in the Second World War, and whose family endowed it in his memory. There are more than twenty pieces scattered across the campus, and they are not fenced or labelled with much ceremony; they simply stand on lawns.

Within a few minutes of here you can find work by Picasso, a large concrete piece called Head of a Woman. There is a Henry Moore, an Oval with Points, over by Stuyvesant Hall, that students walk through. There is a Louise Nevelson, and an Alexander Calder, a huge black steel piece called Five Disks: One Empty, near the plasma physics laboratory. Ask anyone where the Picasso is; every student knows.

That is the thing about this campus that separates it from the others on this tour: it is a park with buildings and sculpture in it, rather than a set of courtyards. Use the lawns.

One more thing about a university museum, which is worth saying at any of the eight campuses on this tour. These collections exist to be taught from, which means the objects were chosen to make an argument in a classroom rather than to draw a crowd, and it means the museum will lend a fifteenth century panel to a professor for a seminar. If you ask at the desk what is used for teaching, you will usually get a better answer than the one on the wall label.

It is worth saying why a university this small has a collection this good. Princeton has taught art history since the eighteen eighties, when Allan Marquand founded the department, and it was one of the first places in the country to do so; the museum was built to serve it. So the collection grew for a hundred and forty years alongside a department that was often the best in the country, and the professors bought what they needed to teach with. Objects came from Princeton's own excavations, at Antioch in the nineteen thirties and elsewhere, which is why some of the Byzantine material here is not in Istanbul or Paris.

Give it ninety minutes if it is open. If it is shut, the sculpture on the lawns is the consolation and it is a good one.

From here, walk west, back toward the middle of the campus and beyond it, and the next stop is a house rather than an institution.
