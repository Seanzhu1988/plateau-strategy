This is Firestone Library, opened in nineteen forty eight, and the joke about it is that you are standing on most of it.

First, one practical thing. Firestone welcomes visitors, but tourist access and research access are different. Anyone may use the public lobby spaces, the Milberg Gallery and the Cotsen Children's Library gallery during their opening hours. Independent researchers can request a free day pass for the general collections, normally in advance. Tour groups may not roam through the working stacks, because this is still a quiet research library with several million volumes in it. Check the library's visitor page before relying on access beyond the public galleries.

Look at the building. It is a low Gothic block, three storeys, stone, and from outside it looks like a large but not enormous library. Now look at the lawn beside it and understand that the collection continues under your feet, floor after floor down. Firestone was the first large American university library built primarily below ground, and it holds several million volumes on something like seventy miles of shelving, most of it in basements. It was the largest open stack library in the world when it opened, meaning readers could walk into the shelves and take books down themselves rather than filling in a slip and waiting.

That decision, open stacks, is not a small one. It says that an undergraduate can be trusted alone with rare and valuable books, and that browsing, wandering along a shelf and pulling out something you were not looking for, is a form of education. Every American university library argued about it in the twentieth century and Princeton came down firmly on one side.

The building is named for Harvey Firestone, the tyre manufacturer, whose family gave the money.

What is here that matters to a visitor is the special collections, and Princeton's are unusually strong for a school of this size. The papers of F. Scott Fitzgerald are here: he was Princeton class of nineteen seventeen, or would have been, since he left without a degree, and This Side of Paradise is set here and is the reason a certain kind of person still applies. The library holds his manuscripts, his ledger, his letters. Toni Morrison taught here for seventeen years and her papers are here too, more than four hundred boxes of them.

The exhibition gallery on the ground floor is free and open to the public and always has something out of the vaults. That is the way in for a visitor: you cannot use the stacks without a card, but you can always see the exhibition.

There is one more thing about this building worth knowing, and it is about how universities age. Firestone was renovated over about a decade, finishing in twenty nineteen, at a cost of well over two hundred million dollars, because a nineteen forties library is the wrong shape for how people work now. They wanted daylight, group study rooms, and power sockets, and a building designed as a warehouse for books with readers squeezed in around them had almost none of those. Walk in and you will see the result: the front half is now glass and light and tables, and the books have gone further down.

There is a piece of Princeton lore attached to this library that students still repeat. The stacks go down far enough, and are quiet enough, that generations of undergraduates have treated the lower levels as somewhere to disappear. Every old library in this country has the same stories attached to it, and every librarian will tell you they are mostly untrue and slightly true.

Look at the outside once more before you go, and notice that Firestone is Gothic in the same manner as the buildings from thirty years earlier. It went up in nineteen forty eight, when the rest of American architecture had gone modern, and Princeton was still building pointed arches. The style did not finally end here until the nineteen sixties.

One more note for anyone who reads. Firestone runs public exhibitions drawn from those collections two or three times a year, in a proper gallery with proper labels, and they are among the best small exhibitions in New Jersey precisely because the material is extraordinary and nobody has to sell tickets. Recent ones have shown medieval manuscripts, the Fitzgerald material, and photographs. It is on the ground floor, on your right as you go in, and it costs nothing.

Come out and walk south-east across the lawn. In about a minute, the enormous Gothic church ahead of you is the Princeton University Chapel. It is one of the largest university chapels in the world, and it was built as an argument about what a university ought to be.
