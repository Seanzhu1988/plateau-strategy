Before you look at the chapel, turn and check the view back across the Green, because you are now on the high side of it and this is the best angle on Dartmouth Row: four white buildings in a line, slightly above you, with the grass falling away in front. That is the photograph, and almost nobody takes it from here because everybody takes it from the middle of the lawn.

This is Rollins Chapel, from eighteen eighty five, and it is the odd one out on this campus and a relief after all the white clapboard.

It is Richardsonian Romanesque, which by now you will recognise: rough faced stone in two colours, round arches, a squat corner tower, deep window openings. The architect was Samuel J. F. Thayer. It is the same manner as Sever Hall at Harvard and Sayles at Brown, from the same decade, when every American institution wanted to look heavy and permanent.

It was given by Edward Rollins, a New Hampshire senator and railway man, in memory of his parents, and it was built as the college chapel at a time when attendance at daily chapel was compulsory. That requirement ended in nineteen twenty five, and the building is now used for services of many faiths, for meditation, for concerts and for weddings.

Go in if the door is open. The interior is a single space with a wooden roof, warm and dim, with stained glass, and it seats a few hundred. It is one of the quietest rooms in Hanover.

Now, this is a good place to say something about how the college treats its buildings, because you are standing between two very different attitudes. Behind you is Dartmouth Row, rebuilt in eighteen ninety and nineteen oh five to look exactly like the seventeen nineties. In front of you is a building that made no attempt to match anything and was put up in the style of its own decade.

Dartmouth has done both, repeatedly, and the campus is a series of arguments about it: colonial revival white clapboard, Romanesque stone, Georgian brick from the nineteen twenties, a nineteen sixties arts centre by a modernist, and recent buildings in glass. On a campus this small you can see all of them from one another.

The nineteen twenties are the dominant note, and there is a reason. Between about nineteen twenty five and nineteen forty, under President Ernest Martin Hopkins, Dartmouth built a great deal and built it in Georgian revival brick with white trim, which is the style of most of what you see around the Green today. It is a nineteen twenties idea of the eighteenth century, and it is so consistent that visitors take the whole campus for colonial.

There is one more thing about this chapel worth knowing. It stands on the north east corner of the Green, and behind it the land rises toward the observatory and the older residential streets, and beyond that the woods begin. Dartmouth is on the edge of the White Mountains and the Green Mountains, and from the upper floors of some of these buildings you can see hills in three states.

Look up at the tower and the roofline before you go, and then look back across the Green at Baker's white spire, and you have the two nineteenth and twentieth century instincts side by side: one building trying to look like the eighteenth century, and one trying to look like the eleventh. Neither is what it claims. Both are what an American college wanted to be at the moment it had the money to build.

It is worth saying why compulsory chapel ended when it did, because it happened at all eight of these colleges within about fifty years of each other. The requirement came from the founding purpose: these were institutions for training ministers, and daily worship was the point rather than an addition. As the colleges became secular, research based universities, the requirement survived by inertia and then collapsed, mostly between nineteen hundred and nineteen sixty. Dartmouth's went in nineteen twenty five, which is early. Princeton built its enormous chapel three years after that, which tells you the two things were happening at once.

From here, walk west, toward the large brick building with the tall white tower, which is the reason most visitors come.
