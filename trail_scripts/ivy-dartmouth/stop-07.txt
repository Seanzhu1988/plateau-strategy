Before you go in, look at how this building is joined to the library. Sanborn was built as part of the same programme as Baker, in the same brick and the same manner, and the two are connected inside, so the English department sits directly against the college's main library with a door between them. That was deliberate in nineteen twenty eight and it is the kind of adjacency that a modern campus plan, with departments in separate towers, has largely given up on.

This is Sanborn House, from nineteen twenty eight, attached to the library, and the reason to come is that they will give you a cup of tea.

The building was given by the family of Edwin Sanborn, who taught English at Dartmouth for decades in the nineteenth century, on the condition that his book collection be housed in a room reproducing his own study. The English department has been here ever since.

There is one more thing about the tea worth knowing, because it explains why it has lasted. It is not run by the college's catering service. It is made and served by the English department, by its staff and its graduate students, in their own room, for whoever turns up. An institutional tradition run by an institution becomes an event with a budget line and dies in a restructuring; one made by a handful of people in a room they use every day simply continues.

Go in and find the Sanborn library on the ground floor. It is a two storey panelled room with a gallery running round it, dark wood, a fireplace, leather chairs, portraits, and books to the ceiling. It looks like the library of an English country house and it is the most comfortable room on any campus on this tour.

At four o'clock on weekday afternoons during term, tea is served in it. There is a pot, and cookies, and it costs a few cents or nothing at all depending on the year, and it has been happening since the building opened in nineteen twenty eight. Students come in from class, take a cup, sit in the chairs and read. Anybody may join, including you.

That is the stop. Come at four if you possibly can.

The tradition is unbroken except for closures, and it is the sort of thing that survives at a small college and dies at a large one, because it depends on somebody making tea every day for a hundred years and on the room staying a room rather than being converted into offices.

While you are in there, look at the gallery and the spiral stair, and at the fireplace, and notice that this is a library nobody has modernised. There are no computers in the middle of it and no glass partitions. It is one room, doing one thing, since nineteen twenty eight.

There is a second thing about this building worth knowing. Because it is joined to Baker, you can walk from the Orozco murals to this room in about ninety seconds, which means you can go from the most politically furious painting in New England to a fireside tea party without going outdoors. That contrast is Dartmouth in a sentence: a college that has kept both, in the same building, and sees no contradiction.

One more thing about the room, because it is not only comfortable but historically specific. Panelled two storey libraries with galleries were a standard feature of American houses and clubs of the eighteen nineties and nineteen twenties, and almost all of them have been broken up. This one survived because it was given with a condition attached: the donor required that Edwin Sanborn's study be reproduced and his books kept in it, and a condition in a gift is the strongest form of preservation there is.

If you are here out of term, or after four, the room is usually still open and you can sit in it without the tea.

There is one more thing about Sanborn worth knowing, and it is about who Edwin Sanborn was. He taught here from eighteen thirty five, he was a classicist and a writer, and he wrote a history of New Hampshire. His son's family gave the building. That pattern, a professor of no great national fame commemorated with a building by a grateful family, is the ordinary way these campuses were built, and it is worth remembering among all the industrial fortunes: most of these buildings came from people who loved a teacher.

From here, go back outside and walk down the west side of the Green.
