You are standing in the middle of the Green, and this is the whole plan of Dartmouth, so turn slowly and take it in.

Five acres of grass. The college cleared it of white pines in the seventeen seventies, which took years, and it has been open ever since. To the north, the library with its bell tower. To the east, a row of white clapboard buildings on a slight rise. To the south, the inn you came from and the arts centre. To the west, the college's offices and the student centre. Around all of it, the village.

In summer this is a lawn with people lying on it. In February it is the site of the Winter Carnival, which has been held since nineteen eleven and which involves building an enormous snow sculpture in the middle of this grass, sometimes forty feet high, which the whole college works on. In autumn, on Homecoming weekend, the first year class builds a bonfire here, a tower of railway ties, and lights it, and runs around it, and the number of laps traditionally matches the class year. The fire department attends. It is one of the last unmediated large college traditions left in America and the college has spent years arguing about its safety.

Now, the thing that makes this college different from the other seven, and it is not the buildings.

Dartmouth is small. About forty five hundred undergraduates, which is fewer than any of the others, and its graduate schools, in medicine, engineering and business, are small too. It has fewer than seven thousand students in total, in a village in New Hampshire, and the nearest city of any size is an hour and a half away. That isolation is deliberate and it is the whole culture: people who go to Dartmouth are, for four years, in the woods with each other.

It also runs on a calendar nobody else uses, called the D Plan, adopted in nineteen seventy two: four ten week terms a year, including summer, and students are required to be here for one summer and may otherwise arrange their own pattern of terms on and off. It means the campus never closes, that students are constantly leaving and returning, and that a class scatters and reassembles for four years.

And the outdoors is not a hobby here. The Dartmouth Outing Club, founded in nineteen oh nine, is the oldest and largest collegiate outing club in America; it maintains a long stretch of the Appalachian Trail, which passes through Hanover, owns cabins across New Hampshire, and takes most of the first year class into the mountains before term starts.

That is Dartmouth: a small college on a green in the woods with a bonfire and a snow sculpture and a trail running through the middle of town.

One more thing about the D Plan, since it produces the strangest feature of student life here. Because students choose which terms to attend, a Dartmouth undergraduate's closest friends are often away for a term while they are here, and the phrase for the resulting problem, being off while your people are on, is part of the local vocabulary. It also means the college runs at full strength through the summer, which almost no other American university does, and a summer term in Hanover, with the river warm enough to swim in, is the thing alumni talk about most.

The trade is real: more flexibility, more time abroad and in internships, and a social life that constantly reshuffles.

One more thing about the Green itself, since you are standing on it. It is owned by the college and it is used by the town, and that arrangement has held without a formal boundary for two hundred and fifty years. The farmers' market is on it, the Fourth of July is on it, the town's children play on it, and the college's ceremonies happen on it. In most American college towns that ambiguity would have been settled by a fence a century ago.

Look at the elms. Hanover lost most of its elms to disease in the middle of the twentieth century, like every New England town, and the ones on this green are replanted, some of them disease resistant hybrids, and the town has a tree warden who takes the job seriously.

Now walk east, toward the row of white buildings on the rise.
