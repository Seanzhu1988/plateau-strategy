Daniel Webster deserves a fuller sentence than the one in the last stop, because he is the most famous person this college produced and because his career is a fair emblem of what these colleges were for. He was a New Hampshire farmer's son, he came here at fifteen, he became the greatest courtroom advocate and orator of his generation, he served as a senator and twice as Secretary of State, and he argued and won some of the cases that built American constitutional law, including his own college's. He also spent his career defending compromises over slavery that damaged his reputation permanently, and the argument about him has never settled.

This is Webster Hall, from nineteen oh seven, named for Daniel Webster, the man who argued the college's case before the Supreme Court, and since two thousand it has held the Rauner Special Collections Library.

The building outside is Georgian revival brick with a columned portico, a public hall built for lectures and ceremonies, and it did that job for most of the twentieth century. The conversion put the college's rare books, manuscripts and archives into it and, crucially, made them easy to reach.

That last part is the thing worth stopping for, because it is unusual.

At most universities on this tour the special collections are behind an appointment, a reader's card, and a certain amount of institutional throat clearing. Rauner is deliberately not. Any Dartmouth student may walk in and ask to see anything, without an appointment and without explaining why, and they will bring it out and put it on a table. Undergraduates handle medieval manuscripts in their first term. The library's whole philosophy is that a rare book kept safe from students is not being used.

Visitors are welcome in the reading room and in the exhibition space, and there is nearly always something on show.

What is in here. The college's own archives back to seventeen sixty nine, including the charter. A very strong collection on polar exploration, because Vilhjalmur Stefansson, the Arctic explorer, brought his library here in the nineteen fifties, and it is one of the great polar collections in the world. The papers of Robert Frost, who was a Dartmouth student for a few months in eighteen ninety two before dropping out and who came back to teach much later. A large collection of Daniel Webster material. And the papers and the working library of Dr Seuss, Theodor Geisel, class of nineteen twenty five, who is Dartmouth's most beloved alumnus and whose name is on the medical school.

Geisel is worth a sentence. He edited the college humour magazine, was caught drinking in his room during Prohibition, was made to resign the editorship, and kept writing for it anyway under a pen name, using his middle name, Seuss. That is where the name came from: a punishment he worked around.

Ask at the desk what is out. If you are lucky it will be something extraordinary and they will let you look at it from a foot away, which is a thing almost no institution allows a stranger.

One more thing about the polar collection, because it is genuinely unexpected in New Hampshire. Vilhjalmur Stefansson was an Arctic explorer and ethnographer, born in Canada to Icelandic parents, who spent years living with Inuit communities and who was a controversial figure in his own lifetime for both his methods and his claims. His library and papers came to Dartmouth in nineteen fifty one and he taught here until his death. The collection is now one of the primary archives for the history of Arctic exploration anywhere in the world, and it sits in a New England village a very long way from the ice.

There is one more thing about Rauner's open door policy worth thinking about, because it is a real institutional choice with costs. Letting undergraduates handle four hundred year old books without an appointment means accepting a certain amount of wear and a certain risk, and most research libraries have decided the other way. Dartmouth's argument is that a book nobody is allowed to open is only an asset, not a resource, and that the education of a nineteen year old who holds a medieval manuscript is worth the risk to the object.

It is a small college's answer, and it depends on the fact that everybody knows everybody.

From here, walk out and north, and the very large brick building with the white bell tower ahead of you is Baker Library, and it holds the best thing in this state.
