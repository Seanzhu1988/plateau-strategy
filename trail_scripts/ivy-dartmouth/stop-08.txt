Walk out of Sanborn House and continue south along the west side of the Green. The long red-brick building ahead, with pale stone trim and a central entrance, is Parkhurst Hall. Do not mistake its restrained Georgian appearance for an ordinary classroom building. This is Dartmouth's main administration building, and the president's office is still on the second floor.

Parkhurst opened in nineteen eleven. Lewis and Emma Parkhurst gave it to the college as a memorial to their son, Wilder Lewis Parkhurst, who had completed only one year at Dartmouth before he died. A bronze tablet inside records their hope that his ideals of learning, friendship, and duty would endure through the work done here. The building was designed to bring the college's principal administrative offices together under one roof. Dartmouth's own account at the time described that as unusual: a purpose-built college administration building with the president, dean, registrar, treasurer, trustees, and faculty meeting space all gathered in one place.

Stand across the pavement and study the front. The building is Colonial Revival in red brick, with limestone trim, a slate roof, and a short granite stair. It is formal without being monumental. On a campus organised around one open Green, Dartmouth put its governing offices right on the edge of everyday college life, visible from the lawn rather than hidden in a separate precinct.

The event that made Parkhurst nationally known happened on May sixth, nineteen sixty nine. Opposition to the Vietnam War had grown across American campuses, and at Dartmouth the immediate argument concerned the Reserve Officers' Training Corps, or R O T C. More than fifty demonstrators entered Parkhurst, removed administrators from their offices, and occupied the building for roughly twelve hours while supporters and onlookers gathered outside.

The occupiers demanded that Dartmouth end R O T C immediately. The college had already announced a slower phase-out, but the protesters believed continued military training made the institution complicit in the war. After a court order to leave was ignored, New Hampshire state police entered the building in the early hours of the following morning. The demonstrators were arrested; many received thirty-day jail sentences, and Dartmouth students among them were suspended.

It is worth resisting a neat version in which everyone on campus stood on the same side. R O T C also had supporters, including students who depended on its scholarships or believed in military service. The disagreement was over the war, the college's obligations, and who had the authority to decide. That conflict is why Parkhurst matters. The building made to centralise institutional power became the place where students most dramatically challenged that power.

Dartmouth's archives now preserve oral histories from people who occupied Parkhurst, watched from outside, supported the protesters, or opposed them. Their accounts do not line up perfectly, and that is useful. A campus history is not a plaque with one conclusion; it is an argument remembered by people who experienced it differently.

Parkhurst became a focus of protest again in January nineteen eighty six. Students had built shanties on the Green to press Dartmouth to divest from companies doing business in apartheid South Africa. After opponents attacked and damaged the shanties, more than two hundred protesters occupied Parkhurst to demand disciplinary action. The same doors therefore connect two different eras of campus activism: Vietnam and R O T C in the nineteen sixties, apartheid and divestment in the nineteen eighties.

Today the building has returned to its everyday administrative work. The president's office remains upstairs, and students traditionally pass through Parkhurst during matriculation. That mixture is characteristic of Dartmouth: ceremonies that welcome students into the institution take place in the same building where earlier students forced an argument with it.

This is mainly an exterior stop. Unless you have college business, look from the sidewalk rather than treating the offices as a public museum. The detail to carry with you is the contrast between the calm façade and the history behind the doors.

From Parkhurst, keep walking south along North Main Street, still beside the Green. In about two minutes you will reach the Collis Center, Dartmouth's student centre. Parkhurst is where the college is governed; Collis is where students gather, eat, organise, and turn institutional questions into everyday campus life.
