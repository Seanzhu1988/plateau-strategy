You have come down the west side of the Green to get here, past the college's administrative buildings, and it is worth noting how few of them there are and how small. Dartmouth has a smaller administration than any of its peers, which is partly a matter of size and partly a matter of pride, and the college's own literature makes a point of it.

This is the Collis Center, the student centre, and it is where the college eats and meets between classes.

It is built around College Hall from nineteen oh one, which was Dartmouth's first student union building, and it has been extended and remodelled so many times that the original is hard to see. Inside are the food counters, common rooms, offices for student organisations, and a large lounge.

There is usually something happening in the lounge, a table selling tickets or collecting signatures, and the noticeboards are the best five minutes of anthropology available to a visitor on any campus: what is advertised tells you what the students actually do.

This is a good place to talk about the thing visitors notice at Dartmouth and cannot always name, which is that the social life of this college is unusually visible and unusually organised.

Roughly two thirds of eligible Dartmouth undergraduates join a Greek letter organisation, which is by a long way the highest proportion in the Ivy League. The houses are on Webster Avenue and the streets around it, a short walk north of here, and they are the centre of the college's social calendar in a way that has no equivalent at Harvard or Yale or Brown. The film Animal House was written by a Dartmouth alumnus, Chris Miller, based on his own fraternity here in the early nineteen sixties, and the college has been living with that ever since.

The system has been repeatedly investigated, reformed, and criticised, including by the college's own presidents. In twenty fifteen Dartmouth banned hard alcohol on campus and required Greek houses to have residential advisers, among other measures. The argument about whether the system should exist at all is a permanent feature of life here and Dartmouth students will give you strong opinions in both directions within about a minute of being asked.

Why does this matter to a visitor walking a green in a small town? Because it explains the shape of the place. A college of forty five hundred people in a village an hour and a half from a city has to make its own social world, and it has made a highly structured one. The outing club, the fraternities and sororities, the traditions on the Green, the D Plan sending people away and bringing them back: these all do the same job, which is to organise a very small population in a very isolated place.

There is one more thing to say about Animal House, since it is the single most widely known fact about this college and it irritates a good many people here. It was written by Chris Miller from his own experience in Alpha Delta Phi in the early nineteen sixties, filmed in Oregon rather than here, and released in nineteen seventy eight, and it defined the American college fraternity in the popular imagination for two generations. Dartmouth's relationship with it is complicated: it is a joke the college did not choose and cannot escape, and it describes a real place at a real moment that many alumni would rather was not the thing strangers know.

One more thing about the isolation, because it explains more of this college than any single building. The nearest large city is Boston, about two and a quarter hours away by road. There is no passenger railway station in Hanover. The regional airport is small. In winter it is genuinely cold and genuinely dark, and the snow stays from December to March.

Everything about Dartmouth's culture, the outing club, the fraternities, the bonfire, the intensity of alumni loyalty, follows from the simple fact that for four years there is nowhere else to go and nobody else to be with, and the college has built its whole life around making that a feature.

From here, walk south along the west side of the Green toward the modern buildings at the corner.
