The building deserves a word before the collection, because it is a rare case of a museum being rebuilt around a criticism. Charles Moore's nineteen eighty five building was admired by architects and disliked by visitors for one specific reason: its entrance was tucked away down a passage between two other buildings, so that people could not find the front door. The twenty nineteen rebuilding by Williams and Tsien fixed exactly that, giving the museum a proper face on the Green with a wide entrance.

This is the Hood Museum of Art, and it holds around sixty five thousand objects, which is a great many for a college of forty five hundred undergraduates, and among them are some of the oldest works of art in any American collection.

The museum's collection goes back to the college's own beginnings; Dartmouth has been acquiring since the eighteenth century. The building is newer: Charles Moore designed it in nineteen eighty five, in a postmodern manner, tucked between the Hop and Wilson Hall, and it was substantially rebuilt and expanded by Tod Williams and Billie Tsien, who opened the new version in twenty nineteen with a proper front on the Green.

Go in. It is free, always, and it is closed on Mondays.

The thing to find first, because it is the oldest and the least expected, is the Assyrian reliefs. There are nine of them, carved gypsum panels from the North West Palace of Ashurnasirpal the Second at Nimrud, in what is now Iraq, made around eight hundred and eighty years before Christ. They show winged genies attending the king, with cuneiform inscriptions running across them.

How they got to New Hampshire is a nineteenth century story. American missionaries in the Ottoman Empire acquired panels from the excavations at Nimrud in the eighteen fifties and shipped them to their old colleges in the United States. Dartmouth's arrived in eighteen fifty six. Several American colleges got them the same way; Bowdoin and Amherst and others have panels from the same palace. They are almost certainly the oldest works of art on this entire tour, and much of what remains at Nimrud itself was destroyed in twenty fifteen, which makes the scattered panels in small American colleges more important than anyone intended.

The rest of the collection is strong in American art, in Native American art, in African art, and in Papua New Guinean material, and there is a good deal of modern and contemporary work. The museum is a teaching museum in the same sense as Harvard's and Princeton's: classes are taught in front of the objects, and the study room will show things to people who ask.

Now, that is the end of the walk, so step back outside and look at the Green one more time.

You have walked the whole of Dartmouth in about half a mile and forty minutes: a lawn, a row of white clapboard buildings that generated a Supreme Court case, a library with a Mexican mural of the conquest in its basement, a tea party that has run since nineteen twenty eight, an arts centre with the post office in it, and Assyrian palace reliefs from eight hundred BC.

One more note about how the reliefs are shown, because it is unusual and good. They are not behind glass in a dim room. They are mounted at eye level in a lit gallery where you can stand a foot away and read the cuneiform band that runs across the middle of each panel, the same standard inscription repeated on every slab of the palace, recording the king's titles and conquests. Get close enough to see the chisel marks. Very few places will let you do that with a nine hundred year old object, let alone one closer to three thousand.

There is one more group of objects here worth asking about, because it connects to the first stop on this walk. The Hood holds a significant collection of Native American art, and the college's relationship with that material is bound up with the charter of seventeen sixty nine and everything that followed from it. The museum has worked with Native communities on how the objects are described and displayed, and some material has been returned under the federal repatriation law. If the Native American galleries are open, spend time in them; they are the part of this museum that is doing the hardest work.
