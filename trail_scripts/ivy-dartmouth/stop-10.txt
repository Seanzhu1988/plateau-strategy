One thing before you look at it: the name. It is called the Hopkins Center for Ernest Martin Hopkins, who was president of the college from nineteen sixteen to nineteen forty five and who built most of what you have walked past today. He was the president who backed the Orozco murals, who oversaw the Georgian revival building programme of the nineteen twenties, and who ran the college through the Depression and two wars.

This is the Hopkins Center for the Arts. Wallace Harrison's original building opened in nineteen sixty two, and if its great glass arches look familiar, that is because you have probably seen their bigger sibling in New York.

The architect was Wallace Harrison, and the front is a wall of tall glass arches. Harrison went on to complete the Metropolitan Opera House at Lincoln Center in nineteen sixty six, with the same idea carried out on a far larger scale. Dartmouth people call the Hop a rehearsal for the Met. The comparison is informal, but the family resemblance is unmistakable.

Look at the arches themselves for a moment. There are five of them across the front, tall and thin, glazed from the ground to the top, and behind them the lobby runs the width of the building on two levels so that the whole interior is visible from the Green at night. In a village where it is dark by half past four in December, a lit glass wall on the main square is doing something quite deliberate.

Everybody here calls it the Hop. Harrison's building brought music, theatre, film, galleries, studios, and workshops under one roof. The college also put the student mailboxes beside the galleries and snack bar so that every student would pass the arts in the course of an ordinary day. That was social engineering disguised as a floor plan, and it worked.

Those little mailboxes, known as Hinman boxes, became obsolete in the age of email and large parcel deliveries. During a major renovation and expansion, Dartmouth removed them but set hundreds of their small metal doors into a corridor wall as a memorial. Look for that installation inside: an obsolete piece of campus infrastructure has become the building's own archaeological display.

The reimagined Hop reopened in October twenty twenty five after two and a half years of construction. Snøhetta renewed about fifty five thousand square feet of Harrison's building and added the fifteen-thousand-square-foot Daryl and Steven Roth Wing. The project retained the famous arches, Spaulding Auditorium, Moore Theater, and the Top of the Hop, while adding a recital hall, performance lab, dance studio, theatre rehearsal space, and a new forum and plaza.

Go in during public hours. The new forum is designed as the main welcome space, and there is nearly always something on a wall or noticeboard. Check the day's programme and cafe hours rather than assuming every room is open; performances and rehearsals still control how the building works.

The original building was a landmark in its moment for another reason: it was one of the first buildings in America to put all the arts under one roof, performing and visual together, with teaching space and public halls in the same structure. That model, the campus arts centre, was copied everywhere afterwards. The twenty twenty five rebuilding deliberately kept that idea while changing the way people enter and move through it.

Now, walk round to the east side, because the building next door is joined to it and is the last stop.

Before you do, a word about the town, since you have now walked the whole Green. Hanover has about eleven thousand people, a main street with a bookshop and a hardware store and a couple of restaurants, and a hospital. The Appalachian Trail runs straight through the middle of it: white blazes on the lamp posts on Main Street. Through hikers walking from Georgia to Maine come out of the woods here, and the town and the college have a long tradition of feeding them and letting them sleep on floors.

That is not a decorative detail. It is the truest thing about Dartmouth's location: this is a college in the woods, on a long distance footpath, an hour and a half from a city, and everyone who comes here chooses that.

One more thing about Harrison, because he is not a household name and he built a great deal of what people picture when they think of mid century America. He worked on Rockefeller Center, he chaired the design team for the United Nations headquarters in New York, and he built Lincoln Center. That such a man designed a small arts centre in a New Hampshire village is a measure of how well connected Dartmouth's trustees were in nineteen sixty.

One more thing about the Hop as a building, since architectural fashion has swung against its decade. The arched glass front was a genuinely new idea in nineteen sixty two: a public building whose façade is transparent, so that the life inside is visible from the street rather than hidden behind masonry. That is now so ordinary that it takes an effort to see it as a choice. Harrison did it here, then at the Metropolitan Opera at a scale that made it famous. Snøhetta's addition answers the same idea with curved concrete, copper-coloured steel, and more glass rather than imitating the old building.

Before you leave, compare the old brick arches with the new Roth Wing. The point is not to decide which half you prefer. It is to see how a campus keeps a building people remember while changing it enough to work for another generation.

Go east now, to the museum.
