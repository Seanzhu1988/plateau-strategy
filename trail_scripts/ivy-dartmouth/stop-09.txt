One thing before you look at it: the name. It is called the Hopkins Center for Ernest Martin Hopkins, who was president of the college from nineteen sixteen to nineteen forty five and who built most of what you have walked past today. He was the president who backed the Orozco murals, who oversaw the Georgian revival building programme of the nineteen twenties, and who ran the college through the Depression and two wars.

This is the Hopkins Center for the Arts, from nineteen sixty two, and if the front of it looks familiar, that is because you have probably seen its bigger sibling in New York.

The architect was Wallace Harrison, and the front is a wall of tall glass arches. Two years after finishing this, Harrison designed the Metropolitan Opera House at Lincoln Center, which has the same arched glass front on a far larger scale. Dartmouth people will tell you the Hop was the rehearsal for the Met, and Harrison did design them in that order, so the claim is fair enough.

Look at the arches themselves for a moment. There are five of them across the front, tall and thin, glazed from the ground to the top, and behind them the lobby runs the width of the building on two levels so that the whole interior is visible from the Green at night. In a village where it is dark by half past four in December, a lit glass wall on the main square is doing something quite deliberate.

Everybody here calls it the Hop. It contains a concert hall, two theatres, a cinema, studios for art, workshops, and the student mail boxes, which is the crucial detail: Harrison and the college deliberately put the post office inside the arts centre so that every student in the college would walk through it every day. That is a piece of social engineering disguised as a floor plan, and it worked. The Hop is the crossroads of this campus.

Go in. The lobby is open, there is a cafe, and there is nearly always something on a wall or a noticeboard.

The building was a landmark in its moment for another reason: it was one of the first buildings in America to put all the arts under one roof, performing and visual together, with teaching space and public halls in the same structure. That model, the campus arts centre, was copied everywhere afterwards.

Now, walk round to the east side, because the building next door is joined to it and is the last stop.

Before you do, a word about the town, since you have now walked the whole Green. Hanover has about eleven thousand people, a main street with a bookshop and a hardware store and a couple of restaurants, and a hospital. The Appalachian Trail runs straight through the middle of it: white blazes on the lamp posts on Main Street. Through hikers walking from Georgia to Maine come out of the woods here, and the town and the college have a long tradition of feeding them and letting them sleep on floors.

That is not a decorative detail. It is the truest thing about Dartmouth's location: this is a college in the woods, on a long distance footpath, an hour and a half from a city, and everyone who comes here chooses that.

One more thing about Harrison, because he is not a household name and he built a great deal of what people picture when they think of mid century America. He worked on Rockefeller Center, he chaired the design team for the United Nations headquarters in New York, and he built Lincoln Center. That such a man designed a small arts centre in a New Hampshire village is a measure of how well connected Dartmouth's trustees were in nineteen sixty.

One more thing about the Hop as a building, since architectural fashion has swung against its decade. The arched glass front was a genuinely new idea in nineteen sixty two: a public building whose façade is transparent, so that the life inside is visible from the street rather than hidden behind masonry. That is now so ordinary that it takes an effort to see it as a choice. Harrison did it here, then at the Metropolitan Opera at a scale that made it famous, and every arts centre and concert hall built in the western world since has argued with it.

There is a cafe in the lobby, the Courtyard Cafe, universally called the Hop, confusingly, and it is where a good deal of the college eats late.

Go east now, to the museum.
