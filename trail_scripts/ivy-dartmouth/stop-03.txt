This is Dartmouth Hall, and this row of white buildings is Dartmouth Row, and it is the picture the college puts on everything.

Dartmouth Hall is the middle one and the oldest, in the sense that a building has stood on this spot since seventeen ninety one. Look at it: white clapboard, three storeys, a cupola, green shutters, entirely plain. It is a New England meeting house scaled up, and it is what an American college looked like before anyone thought colleges should look like England.

The thing to know is that it has burned down. In February nineteen oh four the original wooden building caught fire and was destroyed overnight, and the college rebuilt it the following year in brick, then covered the brick in white painted clapboard so that it looked exactly like the building that had burned. There was another serious fire in nineteen thirty five. So this is a replica of an eighteenth century building, faithfully done, and the college is open about it.

The two flanking buildings are Wentworth, to the north, and Thornton, to the south, both from eighteen twenty eight, and with Dartmouth Hall between them and Reed Hall beyond, they make the Row: four white buildings in a line on a rise, facing the Green.

Here is the piece of American constitutional history that happened because of this college, and it is the reason Dartmouth matters far beyond its size.

In eighteen sixteen the state of New Hampshire tried to take the college over. The legislature passed acts converting it into a state institution, renaming it Dartmouth University, appointing new trustees and taking control of the property. The old trustees sued, arguing that the college's charter of seventeen sixty nine was a contract and that a state could not simply rewrite it.

The case went to the Supreme Court of the United States, and the college's advocate was Daniel Webster, class of eighteen oh one, then the most famous orator in America. He argued for hours. The tradition, probably embellished, is that he ended by saying: It is, sir, as I have said, a small college. And yet there are those who love it. It is said that Chief Justice Marshall wept.

Marshall's court decided for the college in eighteen nineteen. The judgment established that a charter granted to a private corporation is a contract protected by the Constitution, and that a state may not impair it. That decision is one of the foundations of American corporate law: it is why private universities, and by extension private corporations, have a legal existence the government cannot casually rewrite. Every private university on this tour has more security because of what happened to this small college in New Hampshire.

Look at the buildings once more. Nothing about them is grand, and the argument they generated changed the country's law.

There is a detail about the fire of nineteen oh four that says something about the place. The building burned overnight in February, in deep snow, and students formed a line and carried out what they could, including the portraits and some of the furniture. The college held classes elsewhere and had a replacement designed within months. A small institution rebuilt its central building in eighteen months, in the same shape, because everybody agreed immediately what it should look like.

Look at the cupola. It carries a bell, and the bell is rung at the start of the academic year and on certain other occasions, and it is one of the few surviving pieces of the original building.

There is a second Dartmouth case worth knowing, from much later, because this college keeps generating constitutional argument out of proportion to its size. In the nineteen seventies the college's president was John Kemeny, a Hungarian born mathematician who had worked as a young man on the Manhattan Project and been an assistant to Einstein at Princeton. At Dartmouth, with Thomas Kurtz, he invented BASIC, the programming language, in nineteen sixty four, and built one of the first general purpose time sharing computer systems, so that ordinary undergraduates rather than specialists could use a computer.

That is a genuinely enormous thing to have happened in this village: the idea that computing should be available to everybody, not only to trained operators, was worked out here and given away free.

From here, walk north along the Row and then west toward the tower.
