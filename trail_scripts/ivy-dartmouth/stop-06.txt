This is Baker Library, from nineteen twenty eight, and downstairs in it is one of the great works of twentieth century art, in a room where students are doing their reading.

The building first. Georgian revival brick with a white tower modelled on Independence Hall in Philadelphia, by Jens Fredrick Larson, who was the college architect and who gave Dartmouth most of its nineteen twenties look. The tower has a bell and a clock and it is the tallest thing in Hanover and the thing you see from the road coming in. Baker was joined to the Berry Library in two thousand, so the complex is now called Baker Berry and it runs a long way back.

Now go in, and ask for the Orozco murals, and go down to the lower level, to the reserve reading room.

José Clemente Orozco was one of the three great Mexican muralists, with Rivera and Siqueiros. Dartmouth invited him in nineteen thirty two, as a visiting artist, and gave him the walls of this room and about two years. He painted The Epic of American Civilization, twenty four panels, some three thousand two hundred square feet of fresco, finished in nineteen thirty four. It is a National Historic Landmark.

Here is what it does. It runs the length of both long walls and it tells the history of the Americas as a single arc, beginning with migration and the pre Columbian world, Quetzalcoatl, human sacrifice, the arrival of Cortés in armour, the machinery of conquest, and then modern industrial society: a mechanised, dehumanised world of soldiers, bureaucrats and machines. There is a panel of academic figures in gowns presiding over a skeleton giving birth to a skeleton, which is Orozco's opinion of formal education, painted on the walls of a library, by invitation.

The last panel is Christ, having destroyed his own cross, standing in front of the wreckage of the weapons of the world.

It is furious, and it is not decorative, and it was painted in a New Hampshire college by a Mexican radical during the Depression, and the college has kept it exactly as it is for ninety years, including through decades when there were calls to cover it up.

Now, this is the part that matters most. That room is a working reading room. There are desks, and lamps, and students at them, every day, with those walls around them. Nobody roped it off and made it a gallery. Undergraduates revise for exams inside one of the most important murals in North America and stop seeing it, which is the fate of all great art that people live with.

It is free, it is open to visitors whenever the library is, and you should stand in the middle and go round the room once slowly, panel by panel, from the left.

Do not talk loudly. People are working.

There is one more thing about the murals worth saying, because visitors often ask how a college in nineteen thirty two came to invite a Mexican radical to paint its library. The answer is a professor of art, Artemas Packard, who had seen Orozco's work and argued for it, and a president, Ernest Martin Hopkins, who backed him. Orozco was in the United States because the political situation in Mexico had made work difficult, and he had already painted at Pomona College and at the New School in New York.

He worked in true fresco, painting into wet plaster, which means every session had to be finished before the plaster dried and nothing could be corrected afterwards. Look closely and you can see the day joins in the plaster if you know what you are looking for.

One more thing about the room and the argument over it. The murals were controversial from the start: alumni objected, the New Hampshire press objected, and there were periodic calls over the following decades to paint over them or cover them, particularly the panel satirising academia. The college never did. In the nineteen thirties it defended them on the grounds of artistic freedom, and in later decades it conserved them properly and made them a National Historic Landmark in twenty thirteen.

A public institution that commissions a work of art attacking public institutions, and then keeps it for ninety years on the walls of the room where its students study, has done something harder than commissioning it.

From here, come out and go west across the top of the Green.
