One more thing about the pattern of naming on this campus, because you will have noticed it. Rollins, Sanborn, Webster, Wilson, Baker, Collis, Hopkins, Hood: almost every building here carries the name of a person, and most of those people gave money or taught. That is the ordinary American practice and it has a consequence, which is that a campus is a list of the people an institution wanted to remember, edited by whoever was willing to pay.

This last stop is Wilson Hall, on the corner between the Hop and the Hood, and it is here because of what it says about how a small college uses what it has.

The building is from eighteen eighty five, Richardsonian Romanesque again, rough stone with a round arched entrance and a tower, by Samuel Thayer, who built Rollins Chapel on the other side of the Green in the same year and the same manner. It was Dartmouth's library, given by George Wilson, and it held the college's books for forty three years until Baker opened in nineteen twenty eight.

After that it did what every superseded college library does: it became something else, repeatedly. It has been a museum, offices, a film studio and classroom space. Today it holds the Black Family Visual Arts Center's neighbour functions and the college's film and media studies, and it has been renovated inside more than once.

That makes four buildings on this short walk that were once the library: Wilson, then Baker, then Berry attached to Baker, and Rauner in Webster Hall holding the rare books. A college of this size has rebuilt its library four times in a hundred and fifty years, and each time the old one stayed and found a new job.

Look at the stonework by the entrance arch. Thayer's carving here is worth a close look and almost nobody gives it one, because the building is between two much more visited neighbours.

Now, the last thing to say about Dartmouth, standing on this corner.

Everything you have seen today is within three hundred yards of everything else. The whole college, its library, its museum, its chapel, its arts centre, its student centre, its oldest buildings and its green, fits in a square you could cross in five minutes. That is unusual and it is the entire character of the institution: a small college that has stayed small on purpose while its peers grew into universities, and whose graduates are famously and sometimes tiresomely loyal because for four years they lived in a village in the woods with each other.

Daniel Webster's line at the Supreme Court, whether he said it exactly or not, is still what Dartmouth says about itself. It is a small college, and yet there are those who love it.

If you have an afternoon, walk north up to the Connecticut River, or find the Appalachian Trail blazes on Main Street and follow them out of town for an hour into the woods, which is the thing that most Dartmouth students would tell you to do. If you have an evening, look at what is on at the Hop.

There is one more thing worth knowing about this corner, which is that it is where Dartmouth put its arts. The Hop, the Hood and Wilson stand together at the south east corner of the Green, and the college deliberately clustered the arts buildings so that a student going to a concert passes the museum and a student collecting mail passes both. On a campus where everything is within three hundred yards, that kind of decision changes what people bump into.

One more practical note before the walk ends. If you have come to Hanover for the day, the two things that most reward extra time are the Orozco murals, which will take longer than you expect once you start reading them, and the river. The Connecticut River is ten minutes downhill to the west, the college's boathouse is on it, and there is a path along it. Dartmouth's rowing crews are on the water at dawn, and the river is the boundary with Vermont, so you can walk across a bridge into another state before lunch.

Thank you for walking it with me.
