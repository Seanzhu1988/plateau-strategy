The building deserves a word before the collection, because it is a rare case of a museum being rebuilt around a criticism. Charles Moore's nineteen eighty five building was admired by architects and disliked by visitors for one specific reason: its entrance was tucked away down a passage between two other buildings, so that people could not find the front door. The twenty nineteen rebuilding by Williams and Tsien fixed exactly that, giving the museum a proper face on the Green with a wide entrance.

This is the Hood Museum of Art. Its encyclopedic collection now contains more than seventy two thousand works, which is a great many for a college of roughly forty five hundred undergraduates, and among them are some of the oldest works of art in any American college collection.

The museum's collection goes back to the college's own beginnings; Dartmouth has been acquiring since the eighteenth century. The building is newer: Charles Moore designed it in nineteen eighty five, in a postmodern manner, tucked between the Hop and Wilson Hall, and it was substantially rebuilt and expanded by Tod Williams and Billie Tsien, who opened the new version in twenty nineteen with a proper front on the Green.

Go in if it is open. Admission is always free. Current regular hours are Wednesday through Friday from eleven to five and Saturday from ten to five; it is closed Sunday, Monday, and Tuesday, with additional holiday closures, so check before making a special trip.

The thing to find first, because it is the oldest and the least expected, is the Assyrian reliefs. Six monumental gypsum panels came from the North West Palace of Ashurnasirpal the Second at Nimrud, in what is now Iraq, and were carved between eight hundred and eighty three and eight hundred and fifty nine years before Christ. They show the king, attendants, and supernatural figures called apkallu, with a cuneiform inscription running across them.

How they got to New Hampshire is a nineteenth century story. In eighteen fifty three, Dartmouth librarian Oliver Payson Hubbard wrote to Austin Wright, an alumnus working as a missionary in Iran, asking for objects from the ancient cities then being excavated along the Tigris. Through Wright and the British diplomat and antiquarian Henry Rawlinson, six slabs were designated for Dartmouth. Cut into blocks and packed into twenty eight boxes, they travelled by pack animal to Mosul and across the Syrian desert, then by ship to Beirut and Boston, and finally by rail to Hanover in December eighteen fifty six. Several New England colleges acquired reliefs in the same era. Much of the archaeological site at Nimrud was destroyed in twenty fifteen, giving the dispersed panels an importance no one in the eighteen fifties could have anticipated.

The rest of the collection is strong in American art, in Native American art, in African art, and in Papua New Guinean material, and there is a good deal of modern and contemporary work. The museum is a teaching museum in the same sense as Harvard's and Princeton's: classes are taught in front of the objects, and the study room will show things to people who ask.

Now, that is the end of the walk, so step back outside and look at the Green one more time.

You have walked the core of Dartmouth in about two thirds of a mile, plus whatever time you spent inside: a lawn, a row of white clapboard buildings that generated a Supreme Court case, a library with a Mexican mural of the conquest in its basement, a tea party that has run since nineteen twenty eight, an arts centre that once put student mailboxes beside its galleries, and Assyrian palace reliefs from the ninth century BC.

One more note about how the reliefs are shown, because it is unusual and good. They are not behind glass in a dim room. They are mounted at eye level in a lit gallery where you can read the cuneiform band that runs across the middle of each panel, the same standard inscription repeated across the palace, recording the king's titles and conquests. Keep the museum's required safe distance, but look closely enough to see how the figures and text were cut. Very few places let you study an object nearly three thousand years old this directly.

There is one more group of objects here worth asking about, because it connects to the first stop on this walk. The Hood holds a significant collection of Native American art, and the college's relationship with that material is bound up with the charter of seventeen sixty nine and everything that followed from it. The museum has worked with Native communities on how the objects are described and displayed, and some material has been returned under the federal repatriation law. If the Native American galleries are open, spend time in them; they are the part of this museum that is doing the hardest work.
