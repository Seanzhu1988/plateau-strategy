Start on the porch of the Hanover Inn, on the south side of the Green, and look north across the grass, because from this one spot you can see almost everything on this walk.

That is the point of Dartmouth. It is the smallest of the eight universities on this tour, in the smallest town, and the whole of it is arranged around one five acre lawn. There is no long axis, no ceremonial gate, no walled quadrangle. There is a green with buildings round it, which is what a New England town looks like, and the college is simply the town's largest neighbour.

The inn has been here in some form since seventeen eighty, when a college steward was licensed to run a tavern on this corner, and it has been the college's own hotel since. The building you are standing in is from the twentieth century and has been rebuilt more than once, but the corner has been selling beds and dinners to people visiting Dartmouth for two hundred and forty years.

Now, the college. Dartmouth was founded in seventeen sixty nine by Eleazar Wheelock, a Congregational minister from Connecticut, under a charter from King George the Third, and it is the last of the nine colonial colleges. Its charter says it was established for the education of Youth of the Indian Tribes in this Land, and also of English Youth and any others.

That founding purpose needs stating carefully, because Dartmouth does. Wheelock had run a charity school in Connecticut where he educated Native students, and his most successful pupil, Samson Occom, a Mohegan who became a Presbyterian minister, went to England and preached and raised something over twelve thousand pounds, an enormous sum, from British donors for the education of Native Americans. Wheelock used that money to found this college in New Hampshire, moved away from the Native communities the money had been raised for, and educated overwhelmingly white students with it. Occom felt betrayed and said so in writing, and the two men fell out permanently.

In the college's first two hundred years it graduated a very small number of Native students. In nineteen seventy John Kemeny, then president, recommitted the college to the founding purpose in earnest, and Dartmouth now has one of the largest Native American programmes of any university in the country and graduates more Native students than most. It also retired a genuinely offensive sports mascot at the same time.

So the charter, the betrayal and the recommitment are all part of the same story and the college tells all three.

Practical things. Hanover is a village of about eleven thousand people and the campus and the town are the same place. Everything on this walk is within five minutes. The Green is public. Almost every building is closed to visitors, but the two museums and the library are open and free, and the library holds the best thing in New Hampshire.

One more thing about the charter, because the object itself is on this campus. The original seventeen sixty nine charter, signed on behalf of George the Third by the royal governor John Wentworth, survives and is held in the college's special collections in Webster Hall, which is a stop on this walk. It is one of very few colonial college charters still in the possession of the college it created, and it is the document that the Supreme Court case of eighteen nineteen turned on.

And the name. Dartmouth is named for William Legge, the second Earl of Dartmouth, who was the most prominent of the English donors Occom's preaching tour brought in. He was a Methodist sympathiser and a colonial secretary, and he never visited.

A word about Samson Occom, because he deserves more than a footnote in his own story. He was Mohegan, born in Connecticut around seventeen twenty three, converted during the Great Awakening, learned Latin, Greek and Hebrew under Wheelock, and became a Presbyterian minister and a schoolmaster. In seventeen sixty five he sailed to Britain and preached something like three hundred sermons over two years, and he was a sensation: crowds came to hear him, King George the Third himself gave money, and the fund he raised was the largest sum ever collected for a colonial cause.

He came home to find his own family had been left near destitute while he was away, and then found that Wheelock had used the money to found a college in New Hampshire for English youth. He wrote to Wheelock that the college had become alba mater, a white mother, and that he was afraid it would be a bird of another kind. He never set foot here. He spent his last years founding Brothertown, a Christian Indian community in New York, and he died in seventeen ninety two.
