This is the Freedom Trail, and the first thing to know is that it is a line you
can see. A strip of red brick set into the pavement, running two and a half
miles from Boston Common up to the Bunker Hill Monument. You do not need a map.
You follow the brick.

Sixteen sites sit along it. The walking between them is only about forty five
minutes altogether, which surprises people. What takes the day is stopping. Go
inside all sixteen properly and you are looking at six hours. Most people walk
the first eleven, which are all downtown and take about three and a half. The
last five cross the river into Charlestown, and that is worth deciding before
you start rather than at four in the afternoon.

Let me walk you through it, so you know what is coming.

You start on Boston Common. It is the oldest public park in the country, older
than the country, and it was grazing land first. Cows were still on it in
eighteen thirty. The British camped here before they marched on Lexington.

Up the hill is the State House with the gold dome. That dome was wood, then
copper rolled by Paul Revere, and it is gold leaf now. In the Second World War
they painted it black so it would not catch the light for bombers.

Then three stops close together. Park Street Church, on a corner people called
Brimstone Corner because gunpowder was stored there. The Granary Burying Ground,
where Samuel Adams, John Hancock and Paul Revere are all buried in one small
yard, and where the headstones were rearranged to suit a lawnmower, so the
stones and the graves no longer match. And King's Chapel, the first Anglican
church in a Puritan town that did not want it.

Next comes the site of the first public school in America, where Benjamin
Franklin was a pupil and did not graduate. His statue stands there. Then the Old
Corner Bookstore, which published Hawthorne and Emerson and is a burrito shop
now.

The Old South Meeting House is where it turns serious. Five thousand people
packed into that room in December seventeen seventy three, could not get the tea
sent back, and walked out to the harbour. The Tea Party started inside that
building.

Then the Old State House, the oldest public building in Boston, with the lion
and the unicorn still on the gable. The Declaration of Independence was read
from that balcony, and it is still read from it every Fourth of July. Directly
below, in a traffic island, a ring of cobblestones marks where five men died in
the Boston Massacre.

Faneuil Hall is the meeting room where the argument for revolution was actually
made out loud. If you only have two hours, walk from the Common to here and
stop. That is the densest mile of the whole thing.

After that the trail goes into the North End, which is the Italian
neighbourhood, and the walking gets more pleasant. The Paul Revere House is the
oldest house in downtown Boston and he owned it on the night of the ride. It is
small and dark and low, and it is the most convincing room on the trail. Then
the Old North Church, where the two lanterns hung for about a minute, long
enough to be seen across the water and not long enough to be caught.

Copp's Hill is the burying ground the British used as a gun position to shell
Charlestown. Some headstones still carry musket damage from soldiers using them
as targets.

Then you cross the bridge. USS Constitution is still a commissioned Navy ship,
the oldest warship afloat anywhere in the world, and active duty sailors give
the tours. Bring photo identification, because she sits on a working naval base.
And the Bunker Hill Monument is the end, two hundred and ninety four steps with
no lift, on a hill where the colonists lost the battle and convinced everyone
the war could be won.

A few practical things.

Wear the shoes you would wear to walk all morning. The trail is cobblestone in
places and the old streets are uneven.

Go north, from the Common toward Charlestown. The sites open in the order the
story happened and the afternoon sun stays behind you.

You can join anywhere and leave anywhere. Nobody is checking.

And this is a working city, not a museum. People live above the sites. The
graveyards are wedged between office buildings and you will hear traffic the
whole way. That is not a flaw. It is what makes it different from a
reconstruction: these things stayed where they happened while the city grew
around them.

Start at the Common, find the brick, and turn right.
