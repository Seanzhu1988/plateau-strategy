You are now in Tercentenary Theatre, which is not a theatre but a lawn, named for the university's three hundredth birthday in nineteen thirty six, when Harvard held a celebration here so large that President Franklin Roosevelt came to speak at it. This lawn is where commencement happens every May, with a stage at one end and thousands of chairs, and it is the ceremonial centre of the university. The white steeple in front of you is the Memorial Church.

Look at the two buildings facing each other across this grass, because they were designed as a conversation and they are the reason this lawn feels the way it does. On one side, the church, white and wooden and slender, in the New England meeting house tradition. On the other, Widener Library, enormous, grey, and columned, in the Roman manner. Faith on one side, learning on the other, arguing across a lawn. That was deliberate; the church was placed here in nineteen thirty two to answer the library, which had gone up in nineteen fifteen.

The church is a memorial, and that is the reason to go in. It was built to the Harvard men who died in the First World War, and it was consecrated on Armistice Day, the eleventh of November, nineteen thirty two. Go up the steps and through the door, and inside, on the right, is a room called the Memorial Room, and this is the part of the building that matters.

The walls of that room carry the names. They are cut into the stone, by war, in long columns, and the first list is the three hundred and seventy three Harvard men who died in the First World War. Later wars were added as they came: the Second World War, Korea, Vietnam. Stand and read a few of them. They are just names and initials, and that is the whole force of it.

There is one detail in that room that people miss and that says something about the place. Among the First World War dead, Harvard chose to include men who died fighting on the German side. They were Harvard men too, alumni who had gone home to Germany, and after a long argument the university decided that a memorial to its dead had to mean all of its dead. Their names are there, set apart, with a Latin inscription. Given that the building went up fourteen years after the war, with the men who fought it still alive and running the university, that was not an easy decision.

The rest of the church is plain and light, in the way of New England churches: white box pews, clear glass, a high pulpit. There is a service on Sunday mornings and morning prayers on weekdays, which have been held daily since the seventeenth century and take about fifteen minutes. The choir is very good. If the door is open on a weekday and there is nobody about, you may sit at the back for a moment, and it is one of the few genuinely quiet places in Cambridge.

If you are here on the hour you may hear the bells. Harvard's are in the tower of Lowell House, a few streets south, and they are Russian: seventeen bells taken from the Danilov Monastery in Moscow, sold for scrap metal in nineteen thirty, bought by an American industrialist and given to Harvard, where students rang them for eighty years. In two thousand and eight Harvard sent them home to the monastery and had exact copies cast in Russia to replace them, which is the rarest kind of ending to a story about a museum object.

Now come back out onto the steps and turn round to face the other way, because the view from here is the one that goes on the postcards and it is worth taking properly. Across the lawn, up a great flight of steps, behind twelve columns, is Widener Library, the largest university library building in the world. It is the next stop, and it exists because of a shipwreck.

Before you cross, notice the size of the lawn itself. At commencement in May this grass holds something like thirty thousand people. On an ordinary Tuesday in term it holds perhaps a dozen students lying on it with books. Both are the university.
