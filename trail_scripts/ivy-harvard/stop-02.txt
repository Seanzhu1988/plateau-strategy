This is Johnston Gate, and it is the front door. Twenty six gates open into Harvard Yard and this is the oldest and the largest of them, built in eighteen eighty nine, given by Samuel Johnston of the class of eighteen fifty five, and designed by Charles McKim of McKim, Mead and White, who were the most fashionable architects in America at the time and who went on to build the Boston Public Library and Pennsylvania Station in New York.

Before you walk through, stop and look at the brick. McKim was obsessed with it. He wanted the new gate to look as though it had always been there, next to buildings that were a hundred and fifty years older, so he had the bricklayers build sample panels and he rejected them, over and over, until he got a wall whose colour and irregularity matched the old halls inside. The story told in Cambridge is that he had them lay it and tear it down more than a dozen times. Look at the variation in the brick and you can see what he was after: no two are quite the same colour.

Now the panels. Set into the piers on either side, in stone, are two inscriptions, and they are worth reading in order because together they explain the whole institution. One carries the vote of the Great and General Court of Massachusetts in sixteen thirty six agreeing to give four hundred pounds toward a school or college. Four hundred pounds was roughly the entire annual tax revenue of the colony. These were people living in wooden houses on the edge of a continent they did not understand, and they spent a year of public money on a school.

The other panel carries a passage from a pamphlet called New England's First Fruits, printed in London in sixteen forty three to raise money, and it is the sentence every Harvard student eventually hears. After God had carried us safe to New England, it says, and we had builded our houses, provided necessaries for our livelihood, reared convenient places for God's worship, and settled the civil government, one of the next things we longed for and looked after was to advance learning and perpetuate it to posterity, dreading to leave an illiterate ministry to the churches when our present ministers shall lie in the dust.

Read that last clause again. Dreading to leave an illiterate ministry. That is the founding fear, and it is a strange and rather moving thing to find carved on a gate: not ambition, but dread. They were frightened that when the educated generation died, nobody would be left who could read the texts.

There is a piece of student lore about this gate that you should know, because you may see it happen. It is said that a student walks through the centre of Johnston Gate exactly twice: in through it at the start, at convocation, and out through it at the end, at commencement. Walking through in between is supposed to cost you your degree. Like most such traditions it is enforced entirely by people who claim not to believe it, and you will see students step around and use the side openings without appearing to think about it.

There is a second thing carved here that most people miss entirely. Look at the smaller stone shield work on the piers and you will find the university's arms: three open books, and on them the word VERITAS, which is Latin for truth. The three books once carried a joke of sorts, in that the third was shown face down, to mean the truth that cannot be known by reason alone and must be revealed. When the arms were redrawn in the twentieth century the third book was turned face up. A college founded to train ministers had quietly changed its mind about how much of the truth is available to study.

You are a visitor, so you may walk straight through the middle, and you should. Go through slowly and look up at the ironwork as you pass under it.

Now stop, just inside. You are in the Old Yard, the original college ground. It is grass and gravel paths and elms, though the elms are not the original elms; Dutch elm disease took most of those and the ones you see are replacements and resistant hybrids. The plain brick buildings around you are dormitories, and first year students live in them, all of them, which is a deliberate policy: everybody in the first year lives in the Yard, whatever their money.

The building immediately to your left, three storeys, very plain, with white trim, is the oldest one Harvard has. That is where we are going.
