This is Memorial Hall, and the first thing to say is that it is not a hall but three buildings in one, and the second is that it is a war memorial, and the third is that Harvard students eat their dinner in it.

Look at it first. It was finished in eighteen seventy eight, in High Victorian Gothic, which means polychrome brickwork, banded stone, steep roofs, pointed windows and a great tower, and the whole thing is very red and very tall and very much of its decade. The architects were William Ware and Henry Van Brunt, both Harvard men. When it was new, people compared it to a cathedral, which was the intention. The tower burned in a fire in nineteen fifty six and stood without its top for forty years; it was rebuilt in nineteen ninety nine, so the spire you are looking at is younger than most of the students inside.

Now the three parts, from the far end toward you. At the east end is Sanders Theatre, a horseshoe shaped auditorium in wood, seating around a thousand, modelled on the Sheldonian in Oxford. It is one of the finest rooms for music in New England, and it is where Harvard holds its lectures, its concerts and its December carol services. Yo Yo Ma has played there. Winston Churchill spoke there. If there is a concert while you are in town, buy a ticket for the room alone.

At the west end, the part nearest you, is Annenberg Hall, and it is a dining room. Every first year student at Harvard eats here, under a hammer beam roof eighty feet high with stained glass windows down both sides. It looks exactly like the dining hall in the Harry Potter films, and you should know that the films were not shot here; they were shot in Oxford, and both rooms descend from the same medieval English original. Annenberg is closed to visitors, because it is a room full of eighteen year olds having breakfast.

Between the two is the transept, and that is the memorial, and it is the reason the building exists. Go in through the door and stand in it. The walls are lined with marble tablets, and on them are cut the names of the Harvard men who died fighting for the Union in the Civil War. There are one hundred and thirty six of them. The building was paid for by subscription from alumni in the eighteen sixties and seventies, while the war was still a thing people had lived through.

And here is the fact that makes the room worth thinking about. Only the Union dead are named. Harvard had many students from the South, and around sixty four of them died fighting for the Confederacy, and they are not on these walls. That was a deliberate decision at the time and it has been argued about ever since; there have been proposals over the years to add them, and Harvard has not. Whatever you make of it, notice that this is a memorial that took a side, which is unusual, and that the university has left the decision of eighteen seventy eight standing rather than smoothing it over.

The transept is normally open in daytime and there is often somebody at a desk. It is free. Take five minutes with the tablets.

It is worth knowing how nearly this building was lost. By the middle of the twentieth century Victorian Gothic was the most despised style in America, Memorial Hall was thought hideous, and there were serious proposals to demolish it. What saved it was that it was too useful and too expensive to knock down, and then that taste turned. It was made a National Historic Landmark in nineteen seventy. Almost every building people cross a city to see was ugly to somebody's grandparents.

One more thing to look at before you go. Above you, the roof of the transept, and the stained glass in the windows: some of it is by Louis Comfort Tiffany and by John La Farge, two of the great American glass makers of the century, and it is worth finding the signatures.

From the door, walk west across the wide plaza in front of the building. The stepped concrete thing on the far side is the last stop on this walk, and it is the newest building you will see.
