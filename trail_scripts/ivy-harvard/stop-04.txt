Here is the crowd, and here is the statue. This is the John Harvard statue, made by Daniel Chester French in eighteen eighty four, and it sits in front of University Hall in the middle of the Yard. Daniel Chester French is the sculptor who later made the seated Lincoln in the Lincoln Memorial in Washington, and if you have stood in front of that one you will recognise the hand: the same care with the fabric, the same heavy calm in the pose.

Watch the crowd for a moment before you look at the statue. What they are doing is rubbing the toe of the left shoe, which has been polished to a bright gold by several million hands, and having their photograph taken doing it. It is supposed to bring luck. I am going to ask you not to, and I will tell you why in a minute.

First, the inscription. It reads John Harvard, Founder, sixteen thirty eight. That is three statements and every one of them is false, which is why this is known in Cambridge as the Statue of Three Lies.

Take them in order. First, it is not John Harvard. No portrait of him exists, and none ever did; he died young and nobody painted him. So French needed a face, and he used a live model, a student named Sherman Hoar, who was a descendant of an early Harvard family and had the right sort of head. The man on this plinth is a nineteenth century undergraduate.

Second, he was not the founder. The college was founded by a vote of the colonial legislature in sixteen thirty six. John Harvard was a young minister who arrived in the colony from England, fell ill, and died in sixteen thirty eight at about thirty, leaving the new school half his estate and his library of some four hundred books. That gift, at a moment when the school had almost nothing, was large enough that they named it after him. He was a benefactor, and an important one, but the college existed before he gave it anything.

Third, the year. Sixteen thirty eight is the year John Harvard died and the year of the bequest. The college dates from sixteen thirty six, which is the year on the university's own seal, and on the gate you walked through, and on every diploma.

Now look past the errors at the object, because it is a good piece of sculpture. He sits with a book open on his knee, the pose of a scholar rather than a founder, in seventeenth century dress, and French has given him a face that is thoughtful and slightly sad rather than heroic. Walk around the back of the plinth. Almost nobody does, and from behind you can see how carefully the fall of the cloak is cut.

And the shoe. Here is the thing the tour groups do not say out loud: the Yard is a residential college, the statue stands where students walk at night, and generations of undergraduates have treated the shoe as a target for the kind of prank that eighteen year olds find hilarious. Ask a Harvard student what has been on that foot. They will tell you, and then they will tell you they have never touched it. The shoe is cleaned, but not as often as it is touched. Photograph it. Do not rub it, and if you have children with you, this is the moment for hand sanitiser.

One more note on French, since you may go on to Washington. He made this statue at thirty four, near the beginning of his career, and the seated Lincoln thirty six years later, at the end of it. The two figures are the same idea worked out twice: a seated man, a heavy fall of cloth, hands that carry the meaning. If you have seen one, you have half seen the other.

The building behind the statue, the grey granite one with the four columns and the two curving flights of steps, is University Hall, from eighteen fifteen, by Charles Bulfinch, who designed the Massachusetts State House and worked on the Capitol in Washington. It is the only stone building in the Old Yard, and its granite came from Chelmsford up the road.

From here, walk through the gap between the buildings behind University Hall, into the larger open space beyond. The lawn on the other side is called Tercentenary Theatre, the white steeple ahead of you is the church, and the great colonnade to your right is the library.
