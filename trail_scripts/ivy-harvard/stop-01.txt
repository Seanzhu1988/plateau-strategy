Start on this side of the wall, in Harvard Square, because the wall is the whole point of the place. Stand where you can see the newsstand island in the middle of the traffic, with the kiosk on it, and look across Massachusetts Avenue at the long brick wall on the other side. Behind that wall is Harvard Yard, and the university has been behind it since sixteen thirty six.

Take a minute here first. This square is where the town meets the college, and the two have been arguing about it for four centuries. The subway comes up in the middle of it, the red line, and it deposits about twenty thousand people a day into a space the size of a large living room. There are street musicians most afternoons. There is a chess table where an old man will beat you for a few dollars. The kiosk on the island was Out of Town News, which sold newspapers from everywhere in the world before the world went online, and its little copper roofed shelter is a landmark even though what it sold is gone.

Look around at the shops. Almost none of them are old, and that is the modern argument about this square: the bookshops and the record shops and the cheap places to eat have been going out one at a time, replaced by banks and chains, and every Cambridge resident over fifty will tell you about it if you let them. The Harvard Coop, the big brick store on the corner, is the survivor. It started in eighteen eighty two as a cooperative so that students could buy books and coal without being gouged by the local shops, and students still get a share of what they spend.

Now the college. Harvard is the oldest institution of higher learning in the country, founded in sixteen thirty six by a vote of the Massachusetts Bay Colony, sixteen years after the Mayflower landed, when Boston was a village of a few thousand people on a peninsula and the country did not exist. It was founded to train ministers. It had one master, nine students and a college yard with a cow in it. That yard is the one behind the wall.

Here is what to hold in your head as you cross the street. Everything on this walk happened within about a quarter of a mile of where you are standing, and most of it within the Yard itself. The oldest building still in use is a five minute walk from here. The statue everybody photographs is six minutes. The library that a mother built for a son who drowned when the Titanic went down is eight. You are not going to be walking far today; you are going to be looking closely.

One practical thing. The Yard is open to the public in daylight and the buildings around it are mostly not, because students live and study in them. You can walk anywhere you can see, and you should walk slowly, but do not try the doors. There are usually student led tours starting near the gate, and they are good and they are free, and if you fall in behind one nobody will mind.

And one thing to notice as you cross. The traffic here is genuinely bad, the crossings are odd angles, and Cambridge drivers have opinions. Use the light.

One last thing about the name of this place. Cambridge was called Newtowne when the colonists laid it out, and it was renamed in sixteen thirty eight after the English university where many of them had studied, because the new college was here. So the city is named after a university in England, on account of a college that had been going for two years and had nine students in it. That is the order of things here, and it explains why the town feels arranged around the Yard rather than the other way about.

The gate we are heading for is the big one with the brick piers and the wrought iron, about a hundred yards to your left along the wall. It is called Johnston Gate, it is the main entrance to the Yard, and there is something written on it that is worth reading before you walk through. That is the next stop.
