Stand at the bottom of the steps and look up, because the size is the argument. Twelve Corinthian columns across the front, a flight of steps that takes a while to climb, and behind it the largest university library building in the world. This is the Harry Elkins Widener Memorial Library, and it was built by a grieving mother.

Here is the story, and it is worth telling properly. Harry Elkins Widener was a Harvard man, class of nineteen oh seven, from a very rich Philadelphia family, and he was a serious book collector by his mid twenties, which is unusual; most rich young men collect other things. In the spring of nineteen twelve he was in Europe buying books, and he bought a rare edition of Francis Bacon's essays, and he and his parents boarded a ship home. The ship was the Titanic. Harry died. His father died. His mother, Eleanor Elkins Widener, survived in a lifeboat.

Two years later she gave Harvard the money for this building, on one condition, or rather several, and the conditions are why it looks like this. She wanted a library big enough to hold the whole university's collection under one roof, and she wanted her son's own books kept in it in a room of their own, unchanged. There is a story, repeated everywhere, that she also required every Harvard student to pass a swimming test, because her son could not swim. That part is not true, and the university says so; Harvard did have a swimming requirement for decades, but it predates the Widener gift and had nothing to do with it. The book collection, the memorial room and the building are true. The swimming test is a story people prefer.

It opened in nineteen fifteen. Behind that front is a structure ten storeys of book stacks deep, most of it below ground level, holding something over three million volumes, with more than fifty miles of shelving. The whole Harvard library system, which is the largest academic library in the world, holds around twenty million items across dozens of buildings, and this is its heart.

Inside, at the top of the main stair, is the Widener Memorial Room, and in it is Harry's own library, about three thousand three hundred volumes, arranged the way he had them, including a Gutenberg Bible and a Shakespeare First Folio. Under the terms of the gift the room is kept as it was, and fresh flowers are placed there. The rare books are behind glass. Students walk past it every day on their way to a carrel.

Now, the practical part. The reading rooms and the stacks are for people with a Harvard identity card, so you cannot simply walk in and browse. On some days the entrance hall is open to visitors, and the Memorial Room is sometimes shown; ask at the door and be prepared to be told no. That is not rudeness. It is a working library with students in it during exams.

So here is what to do instead, and it is genuinely the better experience. Climb the steps to the top, and turn around. From up here, between the columns, you are looking straight across Tercentenary Theatre at the white spire of Memorial Church, with the whole lawn between them. That view, church and library facing each other, is the picture of Harvard that people carry in their heads, and you can only get it properly from this platform. In May, from these steps, you would be looking down on thirty thousand people in chairs.

One more thing while you are up here. Look at the columns. They are not stone all the way through in the way a Greek temple's are; this is a nineteen fifteen building in the classical manner, built with modern structure behind a classical face, and that is what almost every grand American building of that decade is. The style is called Beaux Arts and it was the language of the era, from railway stations to state capitols.

Then come back down the steps and turn left, going east, past the corner of the library, toward a red brick building with a heavy arched doorway. It is smaller than everything around it and it is by the best American architect of the nineteenth century.
