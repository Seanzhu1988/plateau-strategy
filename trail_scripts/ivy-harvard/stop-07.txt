This is Sever Hall, finished in eighteen eighty, and it is by Henry Hobson Richardson, who is generally reckoned the greatest American architect of the nineteenth century and who worked mostly in stone. Here he worked in brick, and this building is the reason architects come to the Yard.

Look at what he did with a material that everything around it is also made of. The plain dormitories in the Old Yard are brick boxes with windows in rows. Sever is brick that has been carved. Look at the doorway first: a deep arch, and around it the brickwork twists into rope and leaf patterns, cut by hand into shaped bricks made specially for it. Then look at the corner towers, which are round and swell outward, and at the way the windows are grouped in bands rather than punched in a grid, and at the roofline, where the brick steps and turns. There is no stone trim, no ornament in any other material. Everything you are looking at is brick, and it was all made to order.

Richardson gave his name to a style, Richardsonian Romanesque, which is the heavy round arched manner you can see in city halls and train stations and libraries all over the country, most of it built after he died, by other people, copying him. He died at forty seven, in eighteen eighty six, six years after this was finished. Sever is a National Historic Landmark.

Now the thing to do, and you will need a friend or a patient stranger. Go to the arched entrance and look at the two sides of the arch, the recessed jambs where the brick curves inward. Stand facing the brick on one side, close, with your face near the wall. Have the other person do the same on the other side, about twelve feet away, also facing the brick. Now whisper, quietly, into the wall. The other person will hear you perfectly clearly, as though you were speaking beside them, and anybody standing in the middle of the arch between you will hear nothing at all.

This is the Sever whispering arch, and it is famous. It works because the curve of the arch is close to a section of an ellipse, and sound leaving one focus is reflected round and gathered at the other. Whether Richardson designed it deliberately is not documented, and Harvard is careful to say so; it may simply be a consequence of the geometry he chose for other reasons. Either way it has been amusing students since eighteen eighty, and it works best when the Yard is quiet, so early morning or a Sunday is the time.

Inside are ordinary classrooms, and the building is in daily use, so unless you have business there, the outside is the visit. Look up once more at the front before you leave, at the way the whole face is composed: a big central arch, the towers holding the corners, the windows gathered in the middle. It is a lesson in making a very ordinary material do something.

It is worth knowing what Richardson was reacting against. The buildings of the eighteen fifties and sixties in America were mostly thin: stone veneers, applied ornament, shapes borrowed from pattern books. Richardson wanted weight, and mass, and walls that looked like they were holding something up. That is why the arch here is so deep, and why the corners are round rather than sharp. Put your hand flat on the wall by the door. It is cold, and it feels thick, and that is the whole nineteenth century argument in one touch.

There is one more piece of history attached to this spot that has nothing to do with Richardson. Sever Hall stands on the site of an earlier building, and the whole east side of the Yard here has been rebuilt more than once. Harvard has been demolishing and replacing its own buildings for four hundred years, and what survives is not always what was best; it is what nobody happened to knock down. Massachusetts Hall survived because it was useful. Sever survives because by the time anyone might have replaced it, people had learned to value it.

From here, leave the Yard through the gate in the east wall, cross Quincy Street, and the glass roofed building on the corner is the art museums. That is the next stop, and it holds a quarter of a million objects.
