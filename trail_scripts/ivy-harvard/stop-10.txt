This is the Harvard Science Center, finished in nineteen seventy three, by Josep Lluis Sert, and it is the building on this walk that people either love or refuse to look at. Sert was Catalan, a friend and collaborator of Le Corbusier and of Joan Miro, and he was Dean of the Harvard Graduate School of Design from nineteen fifty three to nineteen sixty nine, and in those years he built several buildings here. This is the biggest of them.

Look at the shape. It steps back as it rises, in stages, in pale concrete, with strip windows. The story every student tells is that Sert designed it to look like a Polaroid camera, because the money came from Edwin Land, who invented the Polaroid instant camera and founded the company. Harvard does not confirm the story and Sert never said it. Stand at the corner and look at the massing, and you will see why the story survives: the stepped body, the lens like drum at one end. It may be an accident. It is a very good accident.

What matters more, and this is the thing to notice, is what the building does with its ground. Sert believed that the failure of modern architecture was that it put buildings on plinths and left dead space around them. So the Science Center sits at the top of a plaza that runs down toward the Yard, with cafes and a wide flight of steps, and in term time this plaza is where the university actually gathers: food trucks along the edge, students on the steps, somebody selling something, a table with a petition. It is the busiest square foot at Harvard, and it is deliberately not grand.

Inside are lecture halls, laboratories, the mathematics department and the Cabot Science Library, which was renovated a few years ago into a large open study space and which is generally full. There is one thing inside worth going in for, and it is free and almost nobody knows about it: the Collection of Historical Scientific Instruments, on the first floor. Harvard has been buying scientific apparatus since seventeen sixty five, and it kept it. There are orreries and telescopes and early electrical machines, a set of geometric models, and instruments that Benjamin Franklin handled. The gallery is small and the objects are beautiful and it takes twenty minutes.

Now turn around, with your back to the building, and look south down the plaza and through the gap toward the Yard. This is the last thing to do on this walk, and it is why I have brought you here at the end rather than the beginning.

From here you can see, in one view, most of what you have walked. The brick wall of the Yard. The trees. The roofs of the dormitories where the first years live, one of which is three hundred years old with the president underneath. Beyond them, out of sight, the statue with the polished shoe and the three lies, the library the mother built, the church with the names cut into the wall, and the brick arch that whispers.

And you have crossed the whole of it in something under a mile. That is the thing about Harvard that surprises people who arrive expecting a campus: the old college is small. The university is vast, with schools scattered across Cambridge and Boston and a medical campus miles away, but the part that people mean when they say Harvard, the part that is four hundred years old, is a walled garden you can cross in ten minutes.

Behind you, north and west, is the modern university: laboratories, the law school, the divinity school, the museums of natural history with the glass flowers, which are worth a separate morning. In front of you is the beginning of it, in sixteen thirty six, with one master, nine students, and a fear of leaving an illiterate ministry.

One more piece of Sert while you are standing here, because it is around the corner and worth two minutes if you have them. He also built the Carpenter Center for the Visual Arts, on Quincy Street beside the art museums, and it is the only building by Le Corbusier in North America, designed by him with Sert supervising the work. A concrete ramp runs straight through the middle of it, in one side and out the other, so that anyone walking past is carried through the building whether they meant to go in or not. That was the idea: art should be unavoidable.

Thank you for walking it with me. The square, the coffee and the subway are five minutes down the hill.
