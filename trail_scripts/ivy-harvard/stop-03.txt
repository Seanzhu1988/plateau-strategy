This is Massachusetts Hall, and it is the oldest building at Harvard and one of the oldest academic buildings in the United States. It was finished in seventeen twenty, which makes it older than the country by fifty six years, older than the Liberty Bell, older than every other building on this walk by a long way.

Look at it honestly for a moment, because it is not much to look at. Three storeys of red brick, a plain roof with dormers, white trim, sash windows in regular rows, and no ornament of any kind. That is the point. It was built by a colonial society with no money for beauty, to house sixty four students, and it was designed by two Harvard presidents, John Leverett and Benjamin Wadsworth, who were not architects. It has the shape of a large New England house because that is what its builders knew how to build.

Now, three things happened here that are worth standing still for.

The first is that during the siege of Boston, in seventeen seventy five and seventy six, the Continental Army quartered soldiers in this building. The students had been sent away to Concord. About six hundred and forty men lived in a building meant for sixty four, and they did what soldiers do: they stripped the brass doorknobs and the lead window weights to melt into musket balls, they broke the windows, and they burned parts of the interior for firewood. After the war the college billed the new government for the damage. It took years, and Harvard eventually got a payment.

The second is that this building has done nearly every job a university has. It has been a dormitory, a classroom building, a chapel, a barracks and an office. Today it is both ends of the institution at once, and this is the thing to notice. On the ground floor is the office of the president of Harvard University. On the top floor, above the president's head, live first year undergraduates. Fourteen of them, in ordinary rooms, eighteen years old, in the oldest building the university owns, with the president of the university directly underneath.

The third is the protest history, and it follows from the second. Because the president's office is here, and because it is at ground level with a door on the Yard, this building is where Harvard's arguments arrive. Students occupied it in nineteen sixty nine over the Vietnam war and the university's connection to military training, and the president called in the state police to clear the Yard, and that day changed the relationship between Harvard and its students for a generation. They occupied it again in two thousand and one, for three weeks, over the wages of the people who clean and cook and guard the university, and that one worked: Harvard raised the wages. The building has been occupied several times since. If you want to know what Harvard is arguing about in any given decade, look at who is sitting on these steps.

There is a detail in the brickwork worth finding while you are here. Look along the wall at the level of the first floor windows and you will see that the brick is not one colour but many, and that some of the darker headers are set in patterns. Colonial bricklayers fired their bricks unevenly and used what came out of the kiln, laying the over burned dark ones in deliberate arrangements. It is the only decoration this building has, and it was free.

Stand back and look at the top floor windows once more. Behind them, eighteen year olds are asleep, or awake and worried about an essay, in a room where soldiers of the Continental Army slept in seventeen seventy five, in a building that was already fifty five years old when they got there. There is no other room in America quite like that, and the students who get it are chosen by lottery.

Do not try the door. The president works here and the students live here and neither is on display.

Walk east now, into the middle of the Yard, toward the seated bronze figure with a crowd around it. That is the most photographed object in Cambridge, and almost everything the crowd believes about it is wrong.
