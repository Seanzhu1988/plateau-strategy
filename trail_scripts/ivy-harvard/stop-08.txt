This is the Harvard Art Museums, plural, because there are three of them and they were separate institutions until this building joined them. The Fogg Museum, which is western art from the Middle Ages onward. The Busch Reisinger, which is art from the German speaking countries, and which is the only museum of its kind in North America. And the Arthur M. Sackler, which is Asian, Islamic and later Indian art. Together they hold roughly a quarter of a million objects, which makes this one of the largest university collections anywhere.

The building you are standing in front of is by Renzo Piano, the Italian architect who did the Pompidou Centre in Paris and the Shard in London, and it opened in twenty fourteen. What he did here was keep the old nineteen twenty seven Fogg building, with its courtyard modelled on a sixteenth century Italian palazzo, and build a new structure around and above it, capped with a glass roof shaped like a tent. The old red brick facade on Quincy Street is still there. Go in and the palazzo courtyard is the first thing you see, six storeys of daylight above it.

Here is how to use it, and I would give it an hour and a half. Go straight up to the top level first, before you look at anything. From the fifth floor you are looking down into the courtyard through all those floors, which is the best view of the building, and you are standing next to the reason this museum is different from every other museum you will visit.

The top floor is the Straus Center for Conservation, and its laboratories have glass walls. You can stand in the corridor and watch conservators working on paintings and objects: a canvas on an easel under raking light, someone with a microscope and a swab. This is the oldest fine arts conservation centre in the country, founded in nineteen twenty eight, and it is where much of the science of looking after art was invented. Beside it is the Forbes Pigment Collection, thousands of glass jars of coloured powder collected from all over the world, arranged by hue on shelves, and it is one of the most photographed things in Cambridge and one of the most useful research tools in the field, because to identify the pigment in a painting you need a reference sample of the real thing.

Then work downward. The collections are hung so that you move through time as you descend. There are Van Goghs and Monets and a very good Rothko. The Busch Reisinger rooms hold the largest collection of Bauhaus material outside Germany, including work by Kandinsky and Klee and the archives of Walter Gropius, who taught at Harvard and whose own furniture is there. The Asian galleries hold Chinese bronzes and Japanese prints and Buddhist sculpture. There are Roman coins and Greek vases downstairs.

The one thing that makes this a teaching museum rather than a picture gallery is the Art Study Center, and it is open to the public by appointment. You may ask to see almost any object in storage. A curator will bring it out and put it on a padded table and let you look at it, up close, in the room with you. Very few museums in the world do that with strangers.

There is a piece of Harvard history in the building's name that is worth a sentence. The Sackler wing carries the name of Arthur M. Sackler, a psychiatrist and collector who gave the money for the earlier Sackler building in nineteen eighty five. His family's later fortune, made by other members of the family through the marketing of the painkiller OxyContin, became the subject of lawsuits and protests, and museums around the world removed the Sackler name in the twenty tens. Harvard reviewed its own and kept it, on the grounds that Arthur died in nineteen eighty seven, before that drug existed. Whether you find that persuasive is your business; the university's reasoning is on the record and it is not hiding from the question.

The practical detail that matters most: admission is free to everyone, and has been since twenty twenty three, when the university dropped the charge. It is closed on Mondays. Bags go in a locker. Photography without flash is allowed in most of the galleries.

When you come out, turn left and walk north along Quincy Street. In three minutes you will see a very large, very red, very Gothic building with a tower, and it looks like nothing else on this walk.
